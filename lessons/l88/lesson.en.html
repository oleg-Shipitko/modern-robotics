Economics: where a robot pays off

A robot can move 90,000 parts and still fail to pay off: it waits for work, calls an operator, and sits idle during repairs. The hardware price therefore says very little on its own. We will calculate cost per successful task, compare a month of robot operation with the human alternative, and find a process where task flow, quality, and support produce sustainable economics.

1 What to measure

Calculate cost per successful task

A customer gains no value merely because a robot is powered on. Value appears when a tote reaches the conveyor, a part reaches the machine, or an order is completed to the required quality and safety standard. The denominator must therefore be the number of successful tasks.

An attempt and a successful task are different quantities. If a robot starts one hundred operations, finishes ninety, and five of those require rework, all one hundred attempts incurred cost while only eighty-five produced useful results. Dividing expenses by attempts makes the system cheaper on paper, but not in the process.

Speed cannot be read in isolation either. A fast robot with low availability may complete fewer tasks in a month than a slower robot that operates predictably. We need throughput: successful tasks per hour or shift. It combines cycle time, utilization, and success rate.

This metric has a boundary. A dangerous action cannot be offset by many good tasks. Quality, safety, and timing from the previous lessons remain mandatory criteria. Economic analysis begins only among options that pass them.

2 Total cost

The hardware price is only one expense

Total cost of ownership includes a purchase or subscription, deployment, service, spare parts, compute, connectivity, energy, the work area, and the labor of people who handle exceptions. For a purchased robot, the cost is spread across its service life; for a robot supplied as a service, the period includes the RaaS payment and everything the agreement excludes.

Coverage matters more than payment form. An $8,000 monthly subscription may include updates and repairs, while a $20,000 hardware platform may include neither the skill, integration, nor support. Comparing those figures directly means comparing a production-ready shift with a box of parts.

Deployment cost is often largest at the first site: zone mapping, the WMS or MES interface, fencing, connectivity, staff training, and acceptance tests. Some expenses repeat at every site; others become shared engineering. The model must therefore show one-time cost separately and state how many sites share it.

Remote assistance is another visible cost. It increases task completion and collects useful data, but consumes operator time. The economics may work if one specialist helps ten robots with rare cases. If the specialist drives every movement, the system remains teleoperation with expensive equipment.

One calculation from a shift to a task

Consider an educational tote-handling flow. The robot is available for 320 hours per month, one cycle takes 78 seconds, and utilization is 84%. That does not mean 320 hours of motion: only 269 hours become attempts. At a 98% success rate, the process produces about 12,000 completed transfers.

Now assemble the numerator. Purchase and deployment are amortized over the service life, then service, energy, compute, and remote-operator time are added. The educational model produces about $5,000 per month. Dividing by successful transfers gives about $0.42 per task.

The number itself is not the main point. If the same expenses are divided by all attempts, failures disappear from the report. If calendar hours replace useful hours, idle time appears to be work. If operator assistance is omitted, greater autonomy has no economic effect on paper. The correct unit forces each technical improvement into the right row.

This calculation is not a price promise for a real product. Every input in the lab is educational. It shows the structure of a model that must later be filled with the provider's agreement, the site log, and the cost of the customer's specific process.

4 The main denominator

What share of time became useful work?

Utilization is the share of available calendar time in which the robot performs useful tasks. It falls for more reasons than a mechanical failure. The robot may wait for a tote, a charging station, an open work zone, an operator response, or the next shift. Each cause needs a different remedy.

A new model cannot fix low demand. If tasks arrive for only two hours per day, a robot's round-the-clock capability does not create more work. The project must share the robot across processes, choose a smaller fleet, or accept that fixed automation is a better fit.

Idle time caused by exceptions is tied to autonomy. MTBI from Lesson 8.3—the mean time between interventions—is useful here, but the economics also need the duration of each intervention. One brief hint per hour and one thirty-minute recovery per hour have the same MTBI and entirely different cost.

Charging is not always a loss either. If it occurs during a natural process pause, calendar availability decreases while useful throughput does not. The log must therefore connect robot state to the task queue. Otherwise every idle hour looks like a technical failure.

Utilization matters especially when comparing a purchase with RaaS. Capital has already been spent and is allocated across actual tasks. A subscription creates a similar fixed payment per period. In both cases, an empty hour increases the cost of every result.

Purchase and RaaS change risk, not process physics

With a purchase, the customer pays a large amount up front and accepts the risk that the robot will be underused or become obsolete before the end of its service life. Once the capital has been paid, however, the capital line decreases and the customer keeps the equipment. This model works well when the process is stable and the team can service the fleet.

RaaS transfers part of the risk to the provider. A recurring payment may include repairs, updates, and guaranteed availability. The customer can start small and stop expanding if the process does not work. But the agreement matters more than the word “subscription”: integration, consumables, connectivity, and the person handling exceptions may remain outside the fee.

Compare the same outcome and the same allocation of responsibility, not merely payment totals. If an RaaS provider guarantees availability, its fee already includes spare parts and an on-call team. Under a purchase, the same expenses appear in the customer's own service operation. They cannot be removed from one column.

Volume creates another risk. A fixed monthly payment is more attractive under a steady task flow and more expensive under seasonal demand. Payment per completed operation shifts demand risk to the provider, but requires an accurate, trusted success counter. Otherwise the parties will dispute whether an attempt, rework, or stopped task counts as a delivered service.

The contract form does not repair a poor process. A robot that waits for work, frequently asks for help, or blocks the line remains expensive whether it is purchased or rented. First remove the cause of the loss; then choose the financing model and risk allocation.

6 Where to begin

The first process: a bounded task with steady flow

A robot does not necessarily pay off fastest where human wages are highest. The combination of repeatability, available hours, and a measurable outcome matters more. Moving a tote between fixed points is simple but has high volume. Nonstandard repair is expensive, yet every case requires a new diagnosis, tool, and safe plan.

The process needs a clear input and output. The order system names the required tote; a sensor confirms that it reached the conveyor. Success can then be counted automatically. “Helps a worker” is too vague for economics: it does not say when the task started, when it ended, or which share of the result belongs to the robot.

The physical environment must fit as well. A humanoid form is valuable where stairs, passages, shelves, and tools are already designed for people and rebuilding the site is expensive. If a simple conveyor or fixed manipulator can handle the process, a general-purpose robot body must justify more complex operations through flexibility or transfer across tasks.

The first process must tolerate a safe stop. If the robot can freeze and call a person without disrupting the line, rare failures become measurable interventions. If a stop ruins material or blocks the entire plant, the required reliability and redundancy cost rise sharply.

Finally, there must be a queue of subsequent tasks. One successful flow pays for only a limited fleet. Expanding skills makes sense if the same robot hardware, integration, and service can be reused. Otherwise every new skill becomes another deployment with its own economics.

What to measure before buying a robot

First observe the process itself, not the provider's demonstration. Across several shifts, record the start and end of each task, active work time, waiting, rework, human involvement, and the reason for every stop. Without these observations, future utilization has to be guessed from the shift schedule.

Next, separate process constraints from human constraints. An employee may wait for a machine not because the person is slow, but because the equipment cycle takes three minutes. A robot will not remove that wait. Conversely, if a line sits idle at night because no person is available, added availability can create new capacity.

The third record is diversity. How many object types, poses, packages, and exceptions occur in a week? Ten thousand repetitions of one box are easier than one hundred dissimilar repairs. A mean task count without a list of rare variants hides precisely the cases that will call an operator.

The fourth record is failure cost. An incomplete transfer may be safely retried. An incorrectly installed part may ruin a product, and a dangerous motion may injure a person. Rework, lost material, and line downtime enter the economics, but safety remains a separate mandatory check.

The fifth record is whether the result can be confirmed. A good process emits its own digital event: a tote is scanned on the conveyor, a machine begins its cycle, or an order changes status. If only a person evaluates success after the shift, disputes over performance and the RaaS fee may cost more than the metric itself.

After this observation, the team can select the simplest automatable segment and set pilot thresholds. Provider and customer then know in advance which data the decision needs. The pilot stops being a showcase and becomes a measurement experiment.

8 Public evidence

Productivity is not yet payback

BMW reports that Figure 02 moved more than 90,000 parts over ten months in approximately 1,250 hours. That is about 72 parts per operating hour in one specific process. But payback cannot be reconstructed without the cost of the robot, engineers, fencing, connectivity, downtime, and the alternative. The supported conclusion is limited to the pilot's observed productivity.

GXO and Agility report more than 100,000 totes and a multi-year RaaS agreement. This confirms commercial operation of the system, but the fee and total cost are undisclosed. We can say that the agreement is expanding; we cannot calculate cost per tote.

In investor materials, Agility presented an illustrative five-year model of Digit revenue and cost. The company explicitly warns that the figures rely on internal assumptions and may not materialize. The lesson therefore uses them to illustrate the components of a business model—deployment, subscription, software, service, and cost of goods—not as an actual margin.

1X publishes NEO's early-access price and planned subscription. This lets us compare payment forms, but not the cost of a completed home task: actual autonomy, expert frequency, operating time, and the set of guaranteed chores are unknown. Unitree publishes the G1 platform price; the rest of the product structure still has to be added.

A missing number is also a result of verification. It must not be filled with an industry average from another task. In a project model, an unknown cost remains a range, and the decision is tested under adverse, base, and favorable scenarios.

9 Deployment decision

Payback must survive a bad month

A base-case calculation is only the middle. Before a purchase, test three scenarios. In the adverse case, utilization is lower, interventions are more frequent, site launch takes longer, and service costs more. In the favorable case, task flow grows, but the model should not depend on that growth merely to avoid a loss. If a project works only at perfect autonomy, it is a research bet, not an operating plan.

Set the stop threshold in advance. For example: if utilization remains below 55% after eight weeks, MTBI does not improve, or task cost stays 30% above the alternative, the team does not buy the next fleet. This rule prevents every new failure from being explained away as temporary.

Value may extend beyond replaced labor hours. A robot may open a night shift, reduce injuries, absorb peak load, or add capacity without rebuilding the facility. Each benefit receives a separate measurable row: extra orders, hours of availability, injury frequency, or the cost of changing the line. Generic promises of “flexibility” cannot be added to a financial result without an observable event.

Human work must also be compared fairly. The full cost of the human process includes not just wages, but training, shift management, turnover, and the workplace. At the same time, every robot hour cannot be counted as one eliminated paid human hour: a worker may remain for exceptions, quality control, or adjacent operations.

After launch, update the model with observed data. Planned intervals become observed distributions, and every policy-version change receives a new performance row. Economics then becomes another quality loop rather than a presentation prepared for procurement.

Four scenarios instead of one precise forecast

The technical minimum asks whether the system meets mandatory quality and safety criteria. If it does not, financial analysis stops. A cheap but dangerous action does not become a deployment candidate.

An adverse operating month lowers utilization, increases assistance time, and adds unplanned service. It shows how much money the project loses before the causes are repaired. This scenario sets a reserve and a stop threshold; it is not meant to prove inevitable failure.

A base month uses values already observed in the pilot, not targets from a presentation. When data are sparse, preserve a range instead of one point estimate. The more the decision changes inside that range, the more valuable the next measurement experiment becomes.

A scaled month tests more than a larger robot count. Some deployment cost is shared across sites, one operator assists a fleet, and spare parts move closer to the customer. At the same time, an update error becomes more costly and an on-call service is required. Scale economies and new expenses must appear side by side.

After the scenarios, run a sensitivity analysis. If the result depends most on utilization, the next experiment should measure task flow and idle time. If it depends on interventions, log their causes and duration in detail. If it depends on service life, test actuator life and replacement cost. A good financial model does more than produce a number: it identifies the next unknown to measure.

Value beyond labor substitution

Sometimes a robot is not meant to reduce headcount. It may open a nighttime window that was previously unavailable, reduce hazardous lifting, or provide reserve capacity during a seasonal peak. These effects are real, but they cannot be hidden inside a generic “flexibility” factor.

Connect every benefit to an observable event. A night shift means additional completed orders. Fewer injuries means a change in incident frequency and severity at comparable work volume. Faster product changeover means setup hours actually avoided. If the event cannot be measured, the benefit remains a hypothesis.

Finally, account for the change in human work. An exception operator, a service engineer, and a quality-control employee are not “zero labor.” But one person may support several robots, while a hazardous manual operation may disappear entirely. The economics should show this new process, not subtract an abstract full-time position.

11 Lesson takeaways

A product connects quality, responsibility, and economics

  • Cost per successful task combines the period's total cost with the actual useful result.
  • Utilization, interventions, and downtime can reverse a comparison even when robot prices are identical.
  • The first process needs steady flow, observable success, and a safe stop.
  • Published operating hours and operation counts do not establish payback without costs and a fair alternative.
  • The decision to scale follows predefined thresholds and an adverse scenario, not the best month.
