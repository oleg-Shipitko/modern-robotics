7.5 Part 7 · World models

World models for testing policies and generating data

Training a new task leaves dozens of checkpoints. Testing every one on a robot is slow and risky. In this lesson, the world model becomes a test bench: it rolls out policy actions, compares candidates, and helps decide which ones should reach the real robot. In the labs, we test when this filter saves robot trials and when it fails on the dangerous candidate.

1 Cost of testing

There are more checkpoints than the robot can test

In Lesson 7.4 we compared finished policies on a few scenes. That is not enough in a research loop. One training run saves dozens of checkpoints, and each must be tested across objects, starting poses, and homes. A weak checkpoint still occupies the rig, heats the actuators, and needs a person to reset the scene. Action error on held-out data does not solve this problem. A policy may reproduce the expert precisely in a familiar frame and fail after its own first mistake. Testing must therefore close the loop: the policy observes the consequence of its action and continues from the state it created. A real robot provides that loop, but with low throughput.

A complete exam is more than one average success rate. Starting poses, objects, and lighting must be repeated, then failures must be classified. If checkpoints run in long blocks, conditions drift: actuators warm up, cloth moves, and the operator resets the cup differently. Candidates are therefore interleaved and the trial order is fixed in advance. A world model can also begin every rollout from exactly the same state. Cost grows as failures become rarer. A few trials reveal ordinary success but may miss a rare fall near the table edge. Adding homes and objects multiplies combinations. Engineers must trade breadth against rig time even though they need both. A fast preliminary filter does not remove this trade-off, but spends real time on candidates that still have a chance to ship.

A world model offers a second test bench. It receives the same initial frame, accepts policy actions, and generates the next observations. Many trials can run in parallel on GPUs without risking hardware. This acceleration matters only if the candidate order in the model is preserved on the table. This differs from evaluating a prerecorded video. The policy must receive frames generated in response to its own actions, or its errors never change the future. The video model must also observe each action, not only the initial instruction. Otherwise two checkpoints with different commands may receive the same continuation, and the measured ranking belongs to the generator rather than the policies.

Policy testing in this lesson means measuring behavior and the fraction of successful trials. It is not policy evaluation from Lesson 5.1, where we compute the value function \(V^\pi\).

A world model does not need to predict the exact success percentage. It must answer a practical question: which checkpoint should receive the limited real-world test budget? That requires a closed-loop test bench.

One more rule is fixed before the run: how to handle uncertainty. The ensemble mean may look safe while individual networks diverge near the edge. The bench must not silently choose the convenient continuation. It marks the episode for real testing. Ensemble spread is not a failure probability, but it reveals where the conclusion depends on particular weights and data. Calibrate the abstention threshold on held-out real trials: among episodes the model calls confident, measure the missed-failure rate separately. If it rises after a camera or object change, expand the queue of real trials even when the average correlation still looks strong. This makes abstention a measured safety property rather than a visual impression from one rollout. Keep this rule in force until release.

2 Evaluation loop

The policy acts inside a learned scene

We begin with a real frame. The policy emits an action chunk, the world model advances the scene, and the new imagined observation returns to the policy. After several exchanges a judge decides whether the instruction was completed. The judge may be a person, a dedicated classifier, or a VLM, but its error becomes part of the evaluation.

Inside the model the policy must observe the consequences of its own actions. A prerecorded video tests recognition, not control. If the world model receives expert actions instead of the tested policy's actions, it never visits states where the candidate fails. This is why policy rollouts matter more than polished demonstrations. Real trials remain necessary after imagination. First, they provide final confirmation for the selected checkpoint. Second, they add states created by the policy itself to the world-model data, including slips, impacts, and stops near the edge.

Action conditioning may be explicit or hidden. Some models receive joint commands; others consume policy-decoder features; still others generate video and recover actions with an inverse-dynamics model. A common action interface is preferable for testing a new policy family. Features from one decoder may make the bench accurate only for the architecture on which it was trained. Judges also differ. A binary classifier answers whether a short task finished. A progress scale distinguishes partially completed long tasks but introduces a choice about which stage matters most. A VLM transfers to new instructions yet may reward a static scene because the language sounds plausible. It must first be compared with people on held-out clips, and its version and prompt must be stored with the results.

Testing has three independent error sources: dynamics may continue a contact incorrectly, the policy may exploit that error, and the judge may score the outcome incorrectly. A final percentage without a loop specification says little. We need the model, fine-tuning data, horizon, and success rule. How can two series—model success and robot success—be compared without demanding identical values?

The comparison uses the same starts and commands. If the imagined rollout begins from an edited frame while the real trial begins from an approximately reset scene, setup error is mixed with dynamics error. Store the start identifier, policy version, and generator seed. Then the mismatch can be replayed frame by frame to identify what left the shared state first: policy, world model, or judge.

3 Agreement metrics

Selection needs the right candidate order

Let \(R_i\) be checkpoint \(i\)'s real-world success rate and \(S_i\) its world-model score. We compare two sets of scores for the same candidates. The metrics answer different questions: how closely the scores follow a linear relation, whether the order agrees, and how much ranking errors matter.

Pearson correlation measures how closely the score pairs follow a line. The model and robot rates do not have to match for correlation to be high. But when real checkpoint results are close, even small random differences between them can change the coefficient sharply.

Spearman correlation first replaces values with ranks and compares checkpoint order. If a model systematically underestimates every rate but preserves the order, ρ remains high. It does not account for the size of the real performance gap: the same rank swap receives the same penalty for nearly equal policies as for a strong and a weak one.

MMRV, Mean Maximum Rank Violation, includes that real gap. For each checkpoint, consider the pairs whose order the model reversed and find the largest difference in real success rates. Then average these penalties across checkpoints. Swapping 50% and 51% therefore costs almost nothing; swapping 20% and 70% is expensive.

Under the hood: Pearson and MMRV formulas
\[r=\frac{\sum_i(S_i-\bar S)(R_i-\bar R)}{\sqrt{\sum_i(S_i-\bar S)^2\sum_i(R_i-\bar R)^2}}\]
\[\operatorname{RV}(i,j)=|R_i-R_j|\,\mathbf{1}\!\left[(S_i<S_j)\ne(R_i<R_j)\right]\]
\[\operatorname{MMRV}=\frac1N\sum_i\max_j\operatorname{RV}(i,j)\]

MMRV ranges from zero to one; lower is better. Equal real values add no penalty because there is no important order between them.

Mean bias error, MBE, shows whether the model systematically over- or underestimates success. MBE does not test order: a bench can underestimate every policy equally and preserve a perfect ranking. The reverse is also possible: zero mean bias while a strong and a weak policy are swapped. Read bias next to a rank metric.

A model may preserve candidate order while changing the gaps between their scores nonuniformly. Pearson and Spearman can then respond differently. For example, a model may separate failures confidently while compressing all strong candidates into a narrow band. Ranks remain correct while the linear relation is weaker. That model can still screen candidates if the cutoff lies below the compressed group. Selecting one leader inside the group needs more real trials or a model trained to separate near-ties.

Metrics also depend on the candidate set. If all checkpoints are nearly equal, random real outcomes can reorder them and reduce correlation. Adding an obviously failing policy may raise the coefficient without improving discrimination among leaders. A fair report lists checkpoints, shows uncertainty, and does not transfer a coefficient to another policy family without a new comparison. Mark the region around the release threshold in advance and recompute the metrics inside it. The global coefficient asks whether the bench distinguishes obviously different policies; the local coefficient asks whether it can choose among the candidates the team is actually debating. Reporting both prevents an easy failure from making a difficult selection look solved.

No metric replaces failure inspection. High correlation may hide one dangerous object class; low correlation may come from noise between almost equal checkpoints. The lab therefore keeps ranks, real trajectories, and failure causes side by side.

Real evaluation is itself noisy. Two independent trial sets on the same robot do not produce identical rates. Agreement between an automated real rig and human labels is a useful ceiling. Once a world model approaches it, further gains may require more real trials and a better judge rather than better dynamics.

We can now give the world model a concrete exam: eight checkpoints, one table, and identical starting states.

      Node generated 24 cautious demonstrations, then trained five ensembles of three MLPs. Variants differ in the number of rollouts from twenty policies and whether failed episodes were retained.       The result is not simply that more data always helps. Two rollouts with real failures already repair much of the order. Six rollouts without failures may look reasonable because they broaden coverage, but they do not teach the model that a fall is a failed episode. Experience composition matters more than one count.

      The checkpoints are not eight prerecorded percentages. Each is defined by three controller values: push speed, route offset, and release point.       The “successes only” variant removes complete failed episodes, not just one final label. Several transitions before a fall can look locally normal even though the sequence already leads to the edge. The failure-aware variant keeps both the path and its outcome, linking a familiar start to a dangerous continuation.

      A soft judge is useful for long tasks where binary success is rare, but it may reward a clip that approaches the saucer gracefully and violates contact in the final frame. Fix the success rule before comparing checkpoints.

      4 Published results

      Test benches preserve rankings to different degrees

      WorldEval compared manipulation policies in a video model and in RoboTwin. Within that experiment the video model was much closer to the real ordering. The result cannot be transferred directly to another paper: robots, tasks, trial counts, and judges all change.

      Veo tested eight Gemini Robotics checkpoints and preserved their overall order on standard scenes. WorldGym rolled out policies in under an hour on one GPU. These results show that a learned test bench is possible, not that one universal trust coefficient exists. 1X added a success head to a world model and used it to select checkpoints. The authors stress that autonomous policy rollouts—especially failures—are critical. Failures staged by an operator carried a human signature and did not teach the model the hard failures created by a policy. Data must come from the states of the deployed solution.

      NVIDIA compared the recipe across task lengths. Without policy rollouts, agreement degraded most on long manipulations where an early small error changes later contact. The model could complete an object into the gripper or freeze a weak motion. A one-step frame metric cannot expose this; the rollout must reach the first point where imagination and reality diverge.

      The most important control is reality versus reality. Even an automated real rig and human labels do not agree perfectly because trials and judges are noisy. Requirements for a world model should respect this ceiling rather than demand an unattainable coefficient of one. A red team uses the bench differently: it searches for instructions and scenes where the policy behaves dangerously. Veo helped find behavior that was later reproduced on a real robot. Here a false alarm is cheaper than a missed hazard, so the criterion and confirmation budget differ from ordinary ranking.

      Each paper chooses a unit of analysis: tasks, checkpoints, starts, or episodes. High correlation across task averages does not guarantee the right checkpoint order within one task. Before transferring a result, verify that its ranking axis matches the engineering decision you need to make. Open code and weights strengthen the conclusion. A final video alone does not reveal whether the model received future actions, how samples were selected, or what the judge discarded. A reproducible bench publishes starts, the action interface, sample count, and aggregation rule.

      Results apply only to the tested distribution. A new object or contact type may break the ordering. The next section explains where this mismatch comes from.

      Read the main chart together with the appendix. Check how many real trials support each point, whether starts match, how tied ranks are handled, and whether the judge saw policy names. A reproducible bench publishes score pairs and protocol, not only a final correlation.

      5 Mismatch

      A new policy creates unfamiliar states

      A model trained on demonstrations sees clean operator trajectories. A weak policy reaches different states: side contacts, stalls near obstacles, or faster pushes. Without those transitions the model may complete a convenient grasp or forgive a collision. This creates optimistic bias. The reverse also happens. A generator deforms a small object or freezes it after a weak touch, and the judge records failure where the robot would succeed. Bias direction cannot be inferred from the model name; measure it on paired real trials.

      Contacts and small objects are harder than backgrounds because a few pixels can change the physical outcome. Long horizons accumulate error, and a VLM judge may be softer than a person. These errors are independent: better video does not guarantee a stricter judge. An optimistic bench overestimates weak policies most. Successful clips resemble demonstrations; failed policies leave the data and trigger more hallucinations. The model can compress gaps, reverse the order, or shift all scores upward. Real failures improve both dynamics and success-head calibration.

      A pessimistic bench discards working checkpoints because of generator artifacts: a vanished handle, deformed needle, or frozen object. Measure false accepts and false rejects separately, and retain the original clip and first suspicious frame for important candidates.

      Long tasks benefit from checkpoints at meaningful stages. The judge can mark where progress stopped, and the world model can be updated around that short mismatch. This turns one binary failure into a diagnostic signal.

      The practical design is therefore two-stage. The world model screens most candidates quickly; a small set receives real testing. We still need to choose that set's width under a fixed budget.

      There is a third outcome: uncertainty. Do not merge it blindly with success or failure. Many uncertain clips may indicate either a useful new area or states outside the model. Both require real trials, but for different reasons: confirming a strong candidate or collecting diagnostic failures.

      Under the hood: what 500 selections mean

      Each candidate's true success probability is measured by L71 physics on eighty identical starting states. In every repetition a real trial is a Bernoulli draw with that probability. After the fixed budget, the candidate with the highest observed success rate is selected. The two-stage variant admits only the top \(k\) candidates from the embedded world model. The baseline divides the same budget among all twenty. The seed is fixed, so identical settings reproduce the same result.

      Too small a \(k\) turns ranking error into a ban: the true best never reaches the table. Too large a \(k\) leaves few trials per candidate and restores the noise the first stage was meant to remove. The best boundary depends on model quality and budget. Both stages must use the same criterion. If the model ranks task completion but the robot chooses speed among successful policies, the leader can change even with correct dynamics. The lab declares the rule in advance: success rate first, then mean time.

      The budget may be allocated unevenly. Clearly weak candidates need little confirmation; close leaders need more. Such adaptation must be specified before the run or reproduced by a seeded algorithm. Adding trials only to a favored checkpoint introduces a hidden degree of freedom.

      Choose the boundary again after updating the world model. A better preliminary rank may permit a smaller \(k\). A new object or camera narrows the domain, so temporarily increasing \(k\) is sensible. The admitted count belongs in the bench specification.

      This lab does not ask how many trials distinguish 80% from 90% statistically. It asks how to divide an already fixed budget between fast filtering and real confirmation. Exact statistical power belongs to the reliability lesson.

      A real project reserves part of the budget to audit the filter itself. Random model outliers still reach the robot. If strong policies appear among them repeatedly, the preliminary ranking is stale. This exploration prevents a loop in which the model observes real outcomes only for its own favorites.

      6 Data generation

      A synthetic trajectory needs a filter

      In Lesson 4.5, synthetic data came from a physical simulator. Here the generator is itself learned and may violate physics. It creates video for a new task, the inverse-dynamics model from Lesson 4.4 recovers actions, and a filter rejects clips that miss the instruction or contain implausible contact. DreamGen shows an average gain when real demonstrations are scarce. Synthetic-only data remains weaker than a mixture, and generated trajectories hurt some tasks. Generation is not a free extension of the dataset.

      A filter must inspect more than a pleasing final frame. It needs instruction following, object permanence, plausible contacts, and actions the robot body can execute. A failed clip may be discarded or retained as a negative example for the world model. Action labeling adds another error source. Inverse dynamics sees adjacent states, but the same object motion may come from different contacts. Replay pseudo-actions in a digital twin or a small set of real starts. A correct final frame does not make a video a demonstration if the recovered command cannot reproduce it.

      Filters can form a cascade: cheap object and joint-limit checks, a multimodal instruction judge, then a person on a small boundary sample. Rejected clips need not be lost; their failure reason can improve the generator, judge, or inverse-dynamics model. World-model data is strongest for new tasks and visual variants whose semantic outcome can be checked. It is weaker when success depends on fine force, latching, or hidden state. Real trajectories remain the source of physical grounding there.

      Mixtures need explicit weights. A flood of synthetic trajectories can displace rare real contacts from mini-batches. Report source proportions, filter quality, and performance on a real held-out set together. Generated hours alone do not measure useful experience.

      The link to Part 4 is direct: a world model creates no information from nothing. It recombines structure learned from video and robot data. A new instruction may compose known motions, but an unknown contact needs a real example or a strong physical prior.

      Keep provenance after mixing. Store robot and session for real data; model, prompt, seed, and filter for synthetic data; inverse-dynamics version for pseudo-actions. Then a regression can be traced to one source instead of rebuilding the entire dataset. Freeze each dataset version as a list of immutable sample identifiers rather than a clip count alone: deduplication or a new filter can silently change the contents under the same label. Store the exact list with every trained checkpoint, so a repeated evaluation can separate a data change from an architecture change and reproduce the route by which a synthetic example entered training.

      The policy test set must not contain the same synthetic clips used for training. Otherwise policy and judge share generator artifacts. Final evaluation uses new real starts; a synthetic holdout diagnoses coverage and filter errors only.

      7 Reinforcement learning

      A policy can find the model's loopholes

      The Dreamer line from Lesson 7.1 extends to VLAs. A policy acts repeatedly in a world model, receives an outcome score, and updates without a new physical motion for every step. Real trajectories remain necessary to adapt the model to the current policy.

      An optimizer explores the model more aggressively than ordinary testing. If the judge rewards an object moving closer, the policy may learn to create a convincing frame instead of physical success. If the model misses a jam, the policy repeats an action that wins only in imagination. This is classic model exploitation in high-dimensional video. A rare action may trigger a convenient generator artifact. The longer optimization runs without a real update, the farther behavior leaves the dataset. A high imagined reward after many iterations is a reason to strengthen testing, not proof of readiness.

      The reward must match the task. An off-the-shelf VLM may prefer visible progress and miss contact force. Similarity to an expert path is safer in-domain but cannot surpass the expert. A trained success head needs labels, yet its errors can be measured and calibrated on the bench clips. Protection is the same as in the lab: short fine-tuning cycles on current-policy rollouts, a separate failure detector, and regular real tests. The more the policy changes, the faster the world model becomes stale.

      A useful loop alternates optimization and data collection. Run high-spread or judge-disagreement states on the robot, add outcomes, and recompute ranks. Real budget goes where it reduces uncertainty most.

      The world model saves real experience but does not remove it. It decides which trials are worth running next.

      Stopping also uses both sides. If imagined reward grows while held-out real starts do not, the optimizer is exploiting bias. Growth on both sides with rising spread calls for more data. Only aligned imagined and real improvement justifies a longer horizon or less frequent real testing.

      8 Engineering protocol

      Testing ends on the real robot

      First fix the checkpoints, starting states, and success definition. Then obtain imagined and real trials under the same conditions. Compute metrics over all candidates and classify failures by contact, object, and horizon. After selection, return new real trials to the world-model data. Keep failures as well as successes: autonomous failures reveal the states created by the policy. Operator-staged mistakes may have a different signature.

      Separate the comparison set from fine-tuning data. If the same real trial repairs the model and validates it, agreement becomes optimistic. Keep a closed group of starts and objects for final version testing, and add each new failure class to regression tests. Fix the temporal contract too. Action, frame, and judge decision must refer to one state version. A dropped frame or delayed command creates a false mismatch. Record observation-to-actuator latency on the rig and generation steps and hardware for the model.

      Each model version needs a card: data source, autonomous rollout count, failure fraction, horizon, judge, Pearson, Spearman, MMRV, and known mismatch classes. Without it, correlation numbers from different benches cannot be compared fairly. The card also records tested policy versions, the exact start list, the scene-reset rule, and confidence intervals for real success rates. That turns a chart into a reproducible contract: another engineer can rebuild the candidate set, repeat the screen, and determine whether a mismatch belongs to the world model or to the experimental setup. A version is not releasable until this contract can be replayed. Add a shift matrix for background, camera, object, contact, initial pose, and task length. An average coefficient may remain high while one critical cell fails. Hazardous contact deserves its own release threshold and independent real sample.

      The process must also abstain. High ensemble spread, an unseen object, or judge disagreement sends a checkpoint to real testing rather than assigning a confident score. Calibrated abstention preserves the model's value as a filter.

      Compare bench versions on one regression matrix. A better average rank must not erase a known dangerous contact. If a faster model also changes the judge or horizon, treat that as a separate experiment.

      10 Summary

      Lesson takeaways

      1. A world model tests a policy in closed loop: an action changes the imagined observation returned to the policy.
      2. Checkpoint order matters more than exact percentages. Pearson, Spearman, and MMRV expose different aspects of that order.
      3. Fine-tune the model on autonomous policy rollouts, including failures, or it becomes optimistic outside demonstrations.
      4. Two-stage screening saves budget when enough—but not all—candidates reach the real stage.
      5. Imagined data and RL help only with filters, model updates, and final robot validation.
      Open question: can a world model replace real testing?

      No. It helps rank policies and find hazards, but its domain is limited by data. The final candidate still runs real trials, whose outcomes update the model.

      Open question: which failures are most useful?

      Failures created by current policies, not staged by an operator. They reveal new states, contacts, and ways to exploit model error. Keep both the lead-up transitions and the outcome.

      A working loop ranks all checkpoints on fixed starts, sends leaders and diagnostic outliers to real trials, measures bias, updates rank metrics, and adds autonomous failures. A closed holdout then checks that improvement is not memorization. Imagined data and RL follow only after calibration. Generated trajectories pass instruction, physics, and feasibility filters; the optimized policy returns to paired testing. A new action distribution requires a new trust measurement.

      The central engineering habit is separating search speed from permission to ship. A world model can test thousands of variants and generate data; real behavior still depends on a small, carefully designed physical test and an external limiting loop. This completes Part 7's shared experiment on Ada's table. The same physical world supported dynamics learning, representation-space planning, generated continuations, joint future and action prediction, and checkpoint testing. A shared world changes one mechanism without changing the task.

      If a result cannot be reproduced on the shared scene with the same starts, it remains a demonstration rather than a test. Data, weights, seed, and success criterion are part of the lesson just like the chart.
