4.2 Part 4 · Data for training robots

Teleoperation and data collection devices

In lesson 4.1, it turned out that robots have no internet of their own: someone recorded every demonstration on purpose, and it took a person's time. How much such a recording costs and how good it is depends on what it is recorded with. A person can guide the robot with a small copy of its arm, see through its eyes in a headset, put on gloves or an exoskeleton, or do without the robot entirely: take a gripper with a camera in hand and do the task themselves. We'll look at five such methods on diagrams: what the person holds, what they see and what goes into the recording. Then you'll guide Ada's arm from a controller yourself, with and without latency, and see how the controller changes the data. At the end, you'll work out what one usable demonstration costs with a robot and without one.

1 Cost

A demonstration costs a person-hour

In lesson 4.1, language models learned from texts that people had written long before them, but robots have no such reserve: someone recorded every trajectory on purpose. π0 from lesson 3.6 was trained on more than 10,000 hours of recordings, and each such hour costs at least an hour of human work, usually more. In ALOHA, one demonstration took 8–14 seconds, 50 demonstrations gave 10–20 minutes of data, and recording them took 30–60 minutes: the scene had to be reset, and failed attempts had to be redone.

A lot depends on the device used for recording. The most direct way is teleoperation: a person controls the robot in real time, and the robot records what its cameras saw and what commands it received. The recording is made right away on the robot you need, but the robot is busy for the whole collection, and the operator has to understand what is happening to the robot. You can also take a different path and record the person, not the robot. The person does the task with their own hands, a device records their movements, and these are converted into robot commands later.

To compare the devices, we'll ask each one three questions: what the person holds or wears, what they see during recording and what goes into the recording. The recording contains the observation and the action. The observation is the camera frames and the robot's joint angles, and the action is the command that the policy will later learn to output from the observation.

On the diagrams below, orange marks the person and what they set, blue marks the robot and what is measured, and purple marks the mapping of human motion to robot commands. At the bottom of each diagram is a recording strip: ticks move into the demonstration file, and the denser they are, the more often the signal is recorded. The diagrams are simplified: they show the principle, not real dimensions. Click a diagram to pause it.

Let's start with the device from lesson 1.7. Why was ALOHA guided with a small copy of the arm and not with a joystick?

2 Leader arm

The leader arm: a small copy of the robot

Commanding an arm with six or seven joints is awkward. A joystick or a 3D mouse sets where and how fast to move the gripper, and the joint angles that give such a pose are computed by inverse kinematics from lesson 0.3. Near joint limits and in awkward poses, its solutions jump. The ALOHA authors put it plainly: “Off-the-shelf inverse kinematics (IK) fails frequently in this setting.”

That's why in ALOHA the operator moves two small WidowX arms, and the large ViperX arms repeat their angles joint for joint 50 times a second, so no inverse kinematics is needed. Such an arm is called the leader arm, and the robot's arm is called the follower. The leader arm has the same design as the follower, only smaller. Its weight keeps the operator from moving too fast, and the rubber bands that offload it make sessions longer than half an hour possible. The whole kit, robots included, costs less than $20,000. The ALOHA diagram with two pairs of arms is in lesson 1.7.

GELLO (Wu et al., UC Berkeley, 2023) carries the same idea over to any industrial manipulator. The copy of the arm is built to match the robot's kinematics at 1 : 2 scale from 3D-printed parts. DYNAMIXEL servos sit in the joints, and here they serve as angle sensors with an accuracy of 0.088°. The authors write that a motor isn't really needed: simple angle sensors would do too, and the servo's gearbox gives the handle some resistance when it is moved. Leader arms were made for the UR5, xArm7 and Franka Panda, and the parts for one cost less than $300. Near joint limits and in awkward poses, the operator feels the resistance of the handle, but not the force with which the robot presses on an object: GELLO has no force feedback.

The GELLO authors checked how much the control method affects the result. Twelve participants without teleoperation training solved five tasks on two UR5 arms in three ways: with the GELLO leader arm, a Meta Quest 2 VR headset and a 3D mouse. They had to hang a hat, take a mask out of a case, hand over a banana, fold a towel and plug in a USB connector. The average success was 0.92, 0.72 and 0.63. Over all the tasks there were 1, 5 and 9 timeouts and 1, 5 and 5 self-collisions of the arms. With GELLO, the tasks were also completed faster.

But the copy repeats only the arm. A humanoid also has a head with cameras, hands with fingers and legs, and the operator should ideally see the scene through its eyes: then they see exactly what goes into the recording. How do you control all of this at once?

3 VR headset

VR headset: the robot looks where the operator looks

A leader arm won't do for a humanoid: it has two arms with hands and a head with cameras, and all of it has to be guided at the same time. A VR headset covers both needs. It tracks the person's head and hands and shows them the picture from the robot's cameras, so the operator sees the scene the way the robot will record it.

Open-TeleVision (Cheng, Li et al., UC San Diego and MIT, 2024) runs on the Apple Vision Pro headset but isn't tied to the headset model: a Meta Quest works too. The headset streams the poses of the head, wrists and fingers. The wrist poses are smoothed with a filter, and inverse kinematics converts them into the angles of the robot's arms. The fingers are transferred by retargeting: an optimization chooses the robot's finger angles so that seven vectors between keypoints of its hand match the same vectors of the human hand. The two-finger gripper of the second robot, Fourier GR-1, is closed by pinching the thumb and index finger together. The whole loop runs 60 times a second.

The key idea of the work is the active neck. On the head of the Unitree H1 humanoid, a ZED Mini stereo camera sits on a gimbal with two degrees of freedom, yaw and pitch; the gimbal is assembled from printed parts and XL330 motors. The robot's head turns after the operator's head, and stereo video at 480 × 640 per eye goes to the headset. The operator chooses where to look and sees depth. This works over the internet too: an operator in Boston controlled robots in San Diego, about 3,000 miles away.

Stereo helps noticeably. Four participants solved four tasks while seeing either stereo video or the picture from a single camera in the headset. With stereo, every task succeeded in every trial; with one camera, unloading succeeded in 50% of trials and can insertion in 71%. With stereo, insertion took 58 s versus 85 s, and unloading 67 s versus 97 s. A policy trained without stereo picked up the can in 46% of cases, and one trained with stereo in 92%. Each task needed only 10–20 demonstrations.

But now a network, video and pose mapping stand between the person's hand and the robot's arm, and the operator's arm has neither the weight of a leader arm nor any support: it hangs in the air. The authors also list a limitation: the operator feels nothing with their hands. How do latency and such a controller change the demonstration itself? Let's check on Ada's arm.

🔬 Lab

The operator, latency and the controller

Ada is a home humanoid on a wheeled base; the course labs are built around her. More about her in lesson 0.1.

You are the operator. Ada has to move a cup from the table to a shelf. We see the scene from the side, and the controller sits under the scene, as with a real operator: you should watch Ada's arm, not your own hand. The gripper closes by itself if you hold it at the cup for 0.3 s, and it opens when the cup is on the shelf. Recording starts with the first movement: 50 times a second, as in ALOHA, it stores the angles of Ada's arm and the controller's command.

The controller comes in three kinds. The leader arm repeats Ada's arm at 1 : 2 scale, and its angles go to Ada one-to-one. The hand in the air works like a VR headset: the headset tracks the hand, the tracking jitters, and inverse kinematics computes the arm's angles. The keys work like a 3D mouse: the command sets the gripper's velocity. You can add latency between the controller and the arm, as happens when commands and video travel over a network.

Under the scene you see two recordings: the reference, that is, the first successful recording, and the latest one. For each of them, you see the gripper's path, the gripper's speed over time and the metrics: duration, extra path, pauses and action jitter. Extra path says how much longer the path is than the shortest one. Pauses are the time the gripper stood still, not counting the stops at the cup and on the shelf. Action jitter measures how far the recorded command deviates from its smoothed version.

Under the hood: how the controllers, latency and recording metrics work

Ada's arm is built like the two-link arm from lesson 0.3, with links of 0.5 and 0.4 m. Every 20 ms it receives a command, that is, joint angles, and moves toward it: the joint velocity is proportional to the error with a gain of 14 rad/s per radian, but it doesn't exceed 3 rad/s. Latency works as a command queue: the arm executes the command that the controller issued one latency period earlier. The arm doesn't pass through the table, the shelf or the wall: it gets pushed out, and a contact is counted.

Leader arm. The mouse sets the point of the handle, and the copy's angles are computed from it. Of the inverse kinematics solutions, we take the one closest to the previous angles so that the elbow doesn't flip, and Ada receives the same angles. On a real leader arm, sensors in the joints read the angles and nothing needs to be computed; we have to compute them because the mouse sets a point, not angles. Hand in the air. Tracking jitter is added to the point of the hand: it is a random process with a correlation time of 60 ms and a given spread. Ada's angles are computed by inverse kinematics. We chose the size of the jitter for illustration: the papers didn't measure it. Keys. While an arrow key is held, the gripper's target moves at 15 cm/s, and inverse kinematics again computes the angles.

Metrics. Let \(L\) be the length of the gripper's path after smoothing, and \(L_{\min}\) the length of the polyline from the start to the cup, to the corner of the shelf and to the spot on the shelf. Let \(a_t\) be the recorded command, the gripper's point at tick \(t\), and \(\tilde a_t\) the same command after quadratic Savitzky–Golay smoothing over 9 ticks. Then

\[\begin{gathered} \text{extra path} = \frac{L}{L_{\min}} - 1, \\ \text{jitter} = \sqrt{\frac{1}{N}\sum_{t} \lVert a_t - \tilde a_t \rVert^2}. \end{gathered}\]

Pauses are the sum of the stretches longer than 0.2 s during which the gripper moves slower than 2 cm/s. The half second at the cup and on the shelf that the gripper needs to close or open doesn't count as a pause.

Operator model. The lesson's checks run with an operator model: it guides the arm toward the target by what it sees and waits if Ada's arm lags behind the controller by more than 3.5 cm. With this model, a recording with the leader arm and no latency takes 5.9 s, with 300 ms of latency 14.6 s, and with the keys about 12 s. With the hand in the air and 5 mm of tracking jitter, the action jitter comes out at about 3 mm versus 0.3 mm for the leader arm. This is an illustration, not a measurement of people: your recordings will be different.

The controller and latency change the data itself, not just the operator's comfort. Pauses, corrections and jitter are recorded as part of the “correct” behavior, and the policy learns to repeat them along with the task. That's why ALOHA keeps teleoperation at 50 Hz, Open-TeleVision smooths the hand poses, and HOMIE, which comes up below, reads the operator's hand poses more than 200 times a second. What such demonstrations do to training and how they are filtered is covered in lesson 4.3.

But neither the leader arm nor a two-finger gripper conveys what a person's fingers are doing. How do you record fingers?

4 Fingers

Fingers: sensor gloves and hand video

A multi-fingered robot hand has 12–16 joints instead of a single gripper, and you can't make a copy for it: human and robot fingers differ in length, and sometimes even in number. So you need to record the movements of a person's fingers and transfer them to the robot hand, with the same retargeting as in Open-TeleVision. That leaves the question of whether a robot is needed for this at all.

DexCap (Wang et al., Stanford, 2024) does without a robot. The person puts on Rokoko gloves. They are a motion capture system for the fingers: a tiny magnetic sensor sits in the tip of each finger, and the glove measures where it is relative to the hand. An Intel RealSense T265 camera is mounted on the back of each glove, and SLAM from lesson 0.4 tracks the wrist using its images and inertial sensor. A camera with an L515 lidar on the chest captures the scene in color and depth. A mini-computer in a backpack records everything 60 times a second, the battery lasts about 40 minutes, and the whole system fits within $4,000.

Then the recordings are converted for the robot, which has two Franka arms with four-fingered LEAP hands. Inverse kinematics chooses the hand's angles so that its fingertips end up where the person's fingertips were; the pinky is dropped. The LEAP hand is about one and a half times the size of a human hand, and the transfer takes this into account too. The point clouds from the chest camera are brought into one coordinate frame, and the movements of the person's body are removed from them.

Collecting with DexCap is three times faster than ordinary teleoperation and close to natural movement. Policies trained only on such data, without a single demonstration on the robot, reached an average success rate of up to 72% across three tasks. Where that wasn't enough, a person in the gloves corrected the running policy: they added a small correction to its actions or took over control. With 30 such corrections, full success rose from 0 to 25% on the tea task and from 0 to 20% on the scissors task. DexCap doesn't record force, and the authors name this as a limitation.

You can do without gloves too. AnyTeleop (Qin et al., UC San Diego and NVIDIA, 2023) watches the operator's hand with an ordinary camera: a model finds 21 keypoints of the hand's joints in the frame, and an optimization transfers them to the robot hand. A new robot needs only a file that describes its kinematics. With one RealSense camera, an xArm6 arm and an Allegro hand, AnyTeleop did better than the earlier Telekinesis system on 8 tasks out of 10 and just as well on the other two. The weak spot is easy to see: if the fingers occlude each other or the hand moves fast, tracking is lost.

Gloves record the hands, but not the body. A humanoid also has legs, and the operator's hands are already busy. How do you control the whole robot at once?

5 Exoskeleton

Exoskeleton: a leader arm worn by a person

If you put a leader arm on a person, you get an exoskeleton: its links run along the person's arms, angle sensors sit next to their joints, and the angles are sent to the robot one-to-one, without inverse kinematics. The operator's hands are free of a handle, and they guide both robot arms in full, from the shoulder to the gripper.

AirExo (Fang et al., Shanghai Jiao Tong University, 2023) is a two-arm exoskeleton on a vest: it has 7 degrees of freedom per arm, like the robot, and a gripper with a scissor handle. It is 20% smaller than the robot and printed from plastic, its joints have encoders with a resolution of 0.08°, and the whole exoskeleton costs about $600. It can also be worn without a robot: the camera goes on the exoskeleton's mount in the same spot where it sits on the robot, and the table stands in front of the person the same way as in front of the robot. Take a task where the robot has to push a curtain aside, take an object from a shelf and throw it. The ACT policy from lesson 1.7, trained on 10 teleoperated demonstrations, that is, on about 3 minutes of recording, made it to the end of the task, the throw, in 44% of trials. With pre-training on 100 demonstrations recorded without a robot, it reached the throw in 88% of trials, while 50 teleoperated demonstrations, more than 20 minutes of recording, gave 84%.

HOMIE (Ben, Jia et al., Shanghai AI Laboratory and The Chinese University of Hong Kong, 2025) is a cockpit for the Unitree G1 humanoid. The operator sits and wears an arm exoskeleton with the same kinematics as the robot's arms (3 degrees of freedom in the shoulder, 1 in the elbow and 3 in the wrist), but sized to a human arm. On their hands are gloves with Hall sensors, each with 15 degrees of freedom. With their feet, the operator presses three pedals that set the forward and backward speed, the turning and the body height. Walking and squatting on these commands are controlled not by the person but by a policy trained with reinforcement learning in simulation, so the robot's legs keep their balance on their own. Video from the robot's camera goes over Wi-Fi to a screen in the cockpit, and the link latency is 16 ms. The whole cockpit costs about $500.

HOMIE reads the poses of the operator's arms and hands 263 and 293 times a second without a GPU, while Open-TeleVision does it 60 times. Tasks with HOMIE were completed almost twice as fast as through VR, and over five attempts, five novices cut the average time for handing over an object from 24.8 to 8.0 s. With 50 demonstrations, an autonomous policy squatted and picked up an object in 73.3% of trials and picked and placed it in 80%. Neither HOMIE nor AirExo has force feedback, just like GELLO.

AirExo showed that recordings without a robot can replace most of the teleoperation. But the camera and the table there are still set up as on a particular robot. Can data be recorded anywhere, and in a way that suits different robots without rework?

6 UMI

Collecting without a robot: the UMI hand-held gripper

Teleoperation needs a robot for the whole duration of collection. A robot is expensive and heavy, and nobody carries it around other people's apartments, so data is recorded in a few labs. Yet in lesson 4.1, the diversity of environments turned out to matter more than the number of demonstrations in any one of them.

UMI (Chi, Xu et al., Stanford, Columbia University and Toyota Research Institute, 2024) is a hand-held gripper: a printed two-finger gripper with soft fingers and a trigger that a person holds in their hand. Its only sensor is a GoPro camera on the gripper. The fisheye lens sees 155°, and two mirrors at the edges of the frame give two more views, which makes stereo possible without a second camera. SLAM recovers the gripper's pose in space from the video and the GoPro's inertial sensor, and the gripper width is read from markers on the fingers. The gripper parts cost $73, the GoPro with accessories $298, and the gripper weighs 780 g.

For the recordings to be usable by a robot, the same gripper with the same camera is mounted on the robot's arm. Then the policy sees almost the same frame as during recording, and there's no need to calibrate the camera relative to the robot. The actions are recorded relative to the gripper's current pose: with absolute poses, the policy arranged cups in 25% of trials, with deltas in 80%, and with relative poses in 20 out of 20. The same policy checkpoint on a different arm, a Franka instead of a UR5, passed 18 trials out of 20; both failures happened because joint limits were exceeded. Nobody knows the robot's reach during recording, so the recordings are later filtered by its kinematics. One more correction concerns latency: the camera, the arm and the gripper respond with different latencies, from a few to hundreds of milliseconds, and these are aligned. Without this, the success rate in tossing objects dropped from 87.5 to 57.5%.

In 15 minutes, the hand-held gripper recorded more than three times as many cup arrangement demonstrations as teleoperation with a 3D mouse, even though that's only 48% of the pace of a bare hand: the gripper is heavy and bulky. Three people recorded 1,400 demonstrations in 30 locations in 12 person-hours: in homes, offices and restaurants. A policy trained on this data succeeded in 71.7% of trials in two new locations, while a policy trained on data from a single lab didn't succeed once. Lin et al. from lesson 4.1 also collected their more than 40,000 demonstrations with UMI; about 90% of them turned out to be usable, and SLAM spoiled the rest: dark areas and blank walls get in its way.

The authors state the limitations directly: the scene needs texture, the gripper has two fingers instead of five, and it doesn't record force. There turned out to be many devices, and each has its own cost per hour. What ends up costing more in a demonstration: the device, the robot or the person?

What a usable demonstration costs

Let's compare three methods: ALOHA teleoperation, the GELLO leader arm with a UR5 arm, and the UMI hand-held gripper. The kit prices are taken from the papers. Under the hood: how the collection time and the cost per demonstration are computed

Let \(P\) be the price of a kit, \(B\) the equipment budget, \(N\) the number of people, \(r\) how many demonstrations a person records in an hour, \(g\) the share of usable ones, and \(w\) the operator's hourly wage. We buy as many kits as the money allows, but no more than there are people; each kit lasts \(T = 2000\) hours. Then

\[\begin{gathered} n = \min\!\Bigl(N,\ \Bigl\lfloor \frac{B}{P} \Bigr\rfloor\Bigr), \qquad \text{per day} = 8\, n\, r\, g, \\ \text{cost per demonstration} = \frac{P / T + w}{r\, g}. \end{gathered}\]

For ALOHA, we take \(P = 20\,000\), \(r \approx 67\) and \(g = 1\); for GELLO with a UR5, \(P = 30\,300\), \(r \approx 67\) and \(g = 1\); for UMI, \(P = 371\), \(r \approx 117\) and \(g = 0.9\). The calculation leaves out the cost of resetting the scene beyond what is already counted in the recording rate, as well as repairs, operator training and fine-tuning the policy on robot recordings. The UMI paper didn't need such fine-tuning: the policy was trained only on recordings made with the hand-held gripper.

Money for equipment buys robots, but it doesn't speed up the operator. As long as a robot is needed for recording, the number of people who can work at the same time is limited by the number of robots, and the cost per demonstration is still set by the person's time. Collecting without a robot removes the first limit but pays in other ways: some of the recordings have to be thrown away, and the robot has to be able to repeat what the person did by hand. 7 Today

Robot-free collection has grown to hundreds of thousands of hours

Teleoperation remains the way to get data from exactly the robot that will do the work. π0 from lesson 3.6 was trained on robot recordings, and the data of π*0.6 also includes interventions by an operator who corrects the robot while it works on its own.

But the largest datasets of 2025–2026 were collected without a robot. In November 2025, Generalist AI reported that more than 270,000 hours of real-world manipulation data had been collected for its GEN-0 model in thousands of homes, warehouses and workplaces, with 10,000 hours added every week. In April 2026, in its post about GEN-1, the company specified that the data is recorded by low-cost wearable devices on people and that the base model is trained without any robot data at all; by then, more than half a million hours had accumulated. In August 2026, in its post about GEN-1.5, the company added that people record this data with a pair of hand-held grippers. In November 2025, Sunday Robotics showed a glove that has the same geometry and the same sensors as the hand of its robot Memo; according to the company's CEO, Tony Zhao, the glove offers “higher capital efficiency compared to teleoperation ($200 vs $20,000).”

The next step does without a device altogether: robots are taught from videos of people. In 2025, Figure taught a humanoid to navigate rooms using only first-person videos of people, without a single robot demonstration, and in December 2025, Physical Intelligence showed skill transfer from a few dozen hours of video recorded by people with cameras on their heads and wrists. Lesson 4.4 is devoted to this.

But no device answers the main question: what exactly to record. A thousand demonstrations in one kitchen or ten each in a hundred kitchens? Which demonstrations are better thrown away than kept? We'll take up these questions in lesson 4.3.

9 Takeaways

Lesson takeaways

  1. Every demonstration costs a person's time, and usually more than the recording itself lasts: in ALOHA, 10–20 minutes of data took 30–60 minutes of work. The cost and quality of data depend on the device used to record it.
  2. A leader arm is a copy of the robot arm: its angles go to the robot one-to-one, without inverse kinematics. GELLO costs less than $300 per arm, and 12 novices reached a success of 0.92 with it versus 0.72 with a VR headset and 0.63 with a 3D mouse.
  3. The Open-TeleVision VR headset guides a humanoid's head, arms and fingers through pose mapping and retargeting, and a stereo camera on a turning neck shows the operator what the robot will see.
  4. The controller and latency change the data itself: with latency, the operator moves in steps and waits, tracking jitter gets written into the actions, and the policy learns all of this along with the task.
  5. DexCap gloves and the AirExo and HOMIE exoskeletons record the fingers and the whole body. DexCap and AirExo also work without a robot, and HOMIE hands the legs over to a policy trained in simulation.
  6. The UMI hand-held gripper takes the robot out of collection: the same gripper and camera are later mounted on the robot, the actions are recorded relative to the gripper, and the recordings are filtered by kinematics. With it, 1,400 demonstrations were recorded in 30 locations in 12 person-hours.

Open questions

The operator doesn't feel how hard the robot presses on an object. How do you record tasks where force matters?

In ALOHA, the action is the poses of the leader arm, and the force is set implicitly by the difference between the leader and the follower, which the joint controllers work to close. GELLO, HOMIE and Open-TeleVision give no force feedback, and DexCap and UMI don't record force. A device with force feedback, such as the Omega7 in the GELLO table, costs $40,000. There are other ways too: tactile sensors in the fingers of the robot and the gripper, like GelSight from lesson 0.2, and reinforcement learning on the robot itself, where force becomes part of the reward.

If recording without a robot is cheaper and faster, why teleoperate at all?

Humans and robots differ. A robot may not reach the place where the gripper was in a person's hand, which is why UMI filters out such recordings. A robot has a different speed and different latencies: the UMI policy was run at half the speed at which the person moved, and the latencies were aligned. Frames recorded with a person and with a robot differ, which is why AirExo was still fine-tuned on 10 teleoperated demonstrations, and DexCap needed human corrections while the policy was running. Teleoperation gives data from exactly the robot and the latencies it will work with, and it lets you correct the robot on the fly.
