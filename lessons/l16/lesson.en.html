The Transformer

A policy has to read several cameras, a command and the joint state at the same time, and output a sequence of actions. Almost all modern robot policies do this with a transformer. Our running example is π0, a 2024 model from Physical Intelligence; we'll cover it fully in lesson 3.6, and here we take its inputs and inference time as a reference. In this lesson we'll work through attention on six tokens you can move by hand, then count what tokens cost in real policies and see why the control rate is not the same as the inference rate.

1 Policy inputs

How many inputs a policy has

Take π0, the large 2024 policy from Physical Intelligence, which we'll cover fully in lesson 3.6. On every call the model receives frames from three cameras, a text command like “put the cup in the sink” and the robot's state, and it outputs a chunk of 50 actions. A frame is thousands of pixels, a command is a few words, the state is a short vector of numbers. The inputs differ in nature and size, and a single network has to read them all.

The transformer handles this with one trick: everything is turned into tokens, vectors of the same length. A frame is cut into square patches, a command into words and word pieces, and the state goes through a linear layer and becomes a single token. From then on the network works with a sequence of tokens. The links between them are set up by attention: each token decides which other tokens to look at and what to take from them.

The action chunk becomes tokens too, even though it is the model's output. π0 builds it the same way Diffusion Policy does in lesson 1.5: it starts from noise and refines it over several steps. Only the method is different, flow matching from lesson 1.8, so the steps are called flow steps rather than denoising steps. At every flow step the draft chunk, 50 tokens, one per action, goes into the input together with the observation, and at the output the model gives a correction for these tokens. That's why the sequence below also contains 50 action tokens.

The diagram shows what the transformer does with these tokens. Each token gets its position number added. Then the tokens pass 18 times through a layer of the same design: in attention each token takes information from the others, and the fully connected layer processes each token separately. At the output, the action tokens are taken: they are the correction to the draft. The draft is updated and fed back into the input, 10 flow steps in total. On these steps the observation isn't computed again: it passes through the layers once, and what the action tokens take from it is stored. That's the KV cache, covered in section 5. The tags on the diagram point to the sections of the lesson where each part is covered.

2 Tokens

How an image becomes tokens

Vision Transformer (ViT, 2021) cuts an image into square patches, flattens each one into a vector and projects it with a linear layer. A patch becomes a token, like a word in a sentence. The smaller the patch, the more tokens there are and the better the network sees details. But attention compares every token with every other, so the number of pairs grows as the square of the number of tokens.

Another way: compress the tokens

You can reduce the number of tokens without making the patches bigger. That's what RT-1, Google's 2022 policy (lesson 3.1), does. A frame first goes through a convolutional network and becomes a 9 × 9 grid of features, that is, 81 tokens. Then a small network, TokenLearner, builds 8 tokens out of them: for each of the eight it computes weights for all 81 cells and adds the cells up with these weights. The network learns these weights itself, so they gather where there is something important for the task. Only 8 tokens per frame reach the transformer, and there are about 100 times fewer pairs for attention than with 81 tokens.

Under the hood: ViT and where 196 tokens come from
\[\begin{gathered} N = \frac{H W}{P^2},\\ z_0 = \bigl[x_{\text{class}};\ x^1 E;\ x^2 E;\ \ldots;\ x^N E\bigr] + E_{\text{pos}},\\ E \in \mathbb{R}^{(P^2 C) \times D}. \end{gathered}\]

An \(H \times W\) image with \(C\) channels is cut into \(N\) patches of \(P \times P\). Each patch is flattened into a vector of \(P^2 C\) numbers and multiplied by the matrix \(E\); a [class] token is prepended, and a learned position is added to every token. For 224 × 224 and \(P = 16\) this gives 14 × 14 = 196 patches plus [class] (our calculation). ViT-B has 12 layers, \(D = 768\), 12 heads and 86M parameters.

A transformer isn't always better than a convolutional network. On ImageNet without strong regularization, ViT is a few points behind ResNet and catches up with the best models only with 14–300 million training images. That's why in RT-1 and ACT (a policy with action chunks, 2023; lesson 1.7) the images are encoded by a convolutional network: RT-1 gets 81 tokens from a 300 × 300 frame, and TokenLearner compresses them to 8 tokens per frame. This makes inference 2.4 times faster.

🔬 Lab

Find the cup: attention step by step

Ada is looking for the cup on the table. Each of the six objects is a token. A token has a key: the vector by which it is found. And it has a value: what the token gives back if it is chosen. Here an object's value is its location on the table. The query “where is the cup” is compared with every key by a dot product, softmax turns the scores into weights, and the attention output is the sum of the values with these weights. The diagram below shows these three steps one by one. In the lab everything is computed on two-dimensional vectors that you move yourself, and sliders and switches appear from mission to mission.

So far the query has been compared with every key, but that isn't always what we want. Who looks at whom is decided by the attention mask: before softmax, the score of a forbidden pair is replaced with \(-\infty\), and its weight becomes zero. A language model learns to predict the next word, and in training the whole phrase is already in the input. So a word must not see the words after it, or the model would take the answer from there. Such a mask is called causal: each token sees itself and everything to its left.

π0 has its own mask, a block mask: the tokens are split into three blocks — frames and command, state, actions — and attention is opened or closed for whole blocks. The key point is that the observation doesn't look at the actions. Then its keys and values in every layer don't depend on the draft chunk: they are computed once per chunk and stored while the model refines the actions over 10 flow steps. That's the KV cache, which we'll come back to in section 5. In mission 4 the six tokens of the lab become π0 tokens, and you'll build the mask for them yourself.

The diagram above shows a single attention head: one set of linear layers turns tokens into queries, keys and values, and each token gets one mix of values. If a token needs information about two different things, say which object the command names and where it is in the frame, one head blends them into a single average. That's why a layer has several heads; the base 2017 transformer has eight. Each has its own linear layers, compares tokens by its own features and produces its own mix, and the mixes of all heads are concatenated. Each head's vectors are as many times shorter than the token vector as there are heads, so eight heads cost about as much as one full-width head.

Under the hood: the attention formulas, heads and masks
\[\begin{gathered} \operatorname{Attention}(Q, K, V) = \operatorname{softmax}\Bigl(\frac{Q K^{\mathsf T}}{\sqrt{d_k}} + M\Bigr) V,\\ Q \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times d_k}, \quad K \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times d_k}, \quad V \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times d_v}. \end{gathered}\]

Softmax is taken over rows: for each query the weights sum to 1. Why divide by \(\sqrt{d_k}\)? If the components of \(q\) and \(k\) are independent, with mean 0 and variance 1, then the variance of \(q \cdot k\) equals \(d_k\). At large \(d_k\) the scores spread out, softmax moves into a region where almost all the weight goes to one token, and the gradients are tiny.

\[\begin{gathered} \operatorname{MultiHead}(Q, K, V) =\\ \operatorname{Concat}(\text{head}_1, \ldots, \text{head}_h)\, W^O,\\ \text{head}_i = \operatorname{Attention}(Q W_i^Q,\ K W_i^K,\ V W_i^V),\\ W_i^Q, W_i^K \in \mathbb{R}^{d_{\text{model}} \times d_k}, \quad W_i^V \in \mathbb{R}^{d_{\text{model}} \times d_v},\\ W^O \in \mathbb{R}^{h d_v \times d_{\text{model}}}, \quad d_k = d_v = d_{\text{model}} / h. \end{gathered}\]

Each attention head has its own matrices, so the heads look at different features. A head's width is \(d_{\text{model}}/h\), so all the heads together cost almost as much as a single full-width head. Self-attention: \(Q\), \(K\) and \(V\) come from the same sequence. Cross-attention: \(Q\) comes from one sequence, \(K\) and \(V\) from another. That's how the action queries in ACT read image features, and how the DiT in GR00T N1 (NVIDIA's model for humanoids, 2025; lesson 3.7) reads VLM features.

The mask \(M\) is 0 where token \(i\) may look at token \(j\), and \(-\infty\) where it may not. A causal mask closes everything to the right of the diagonal: \(M_{ij} = -\infty\) for \(j > i\). The block mask of π0 and the prefix-LM in PaliGemma (Google's vision-language model, 2024; lesson 3.2) open a cell if the block number of \(j\) is no greater than that of \(i\): within a block attention goes both ways, between blocks only backward. A padding mask closes the columns of empty tokens.

The 2017 block and its feed-forward sublayer:

\[\begin{gathered} x \leftarrow \operatorname{LayerNorm}\bigl(x + \operatorname{Sublayer}(x)\bigr),\\ \operatorname{FFN}(x) = \max(0,\ x W_1 + b_1)\, W_2 + b_2. \end{gathered}\]

In ViT and Gemma (Google's language model inside π0; lesson 3.2) the norm comes before the sublayer:

\[x \leftarrow x + \operatorname{Sublayer}\bigl(\operatorname{Norm}(x)\bigr).\]

How the lab works. An object has two features: color (red, blue, white) and location (at the front edge, in the middle, at the back of the table). The key of the general head is the color vector plus 0.7 times the location vector, so all six keys are different. The “color” head sees only color, the “location” head only location; their keys have length 1.4. An object's value is its coordinates on the 1 × 1 m table. The query length is at most 3. For \(d_k > 2\), \(d_k - 2\) hidden components are added to the two visible ones: random numbers with variance 1, the same on every run (seed 169). Mission thresholds: cup weight above 0.5; the output more than 15 cm from the object centers with no weight above 0.6; the largest weight below 0.9; pairs above 0.8 and the cup above 0.3 in both heads.

3 Positions

Attention doesn't know order

A weighted sum doesn't depend on the order of its terms. If you shuffle the tokens, each one carries its weight along, and the output doesn't change. For a robot this is a problem: “take the cup, then the bowl” and “take the bowl, then the cup” are different commands. So positional encodings are added to the tokens: vectors that depend only on the position number. In the 2017 paper these are sinusoids of different frequencies.

The diagram shows how such an encoding works. The position number becomes 16 numbers, eight pairs. Each pair is a point on its own circle: with every next position it turns by its own angle. The first pair turns fast and tells neighboring positions apart, the last ones turn slowly and tell distant positions apart. The similarity of two positions is the dot product of their encodings, as with the query and key in the lab: it's high for neighboring positions and lower for distant ones.

Below is a command of four word tokens. The action token asks them: which object to take first? Rearrange the words and look for commands on which Ada gets it wrong.

    Under the hood: sinusoids, RoPE and our toy command
    \[\begin{aligned} \mathrm{PE}(\mathit{pos}, 2i) &= \sin\bigl(\mathit{pos} / 10000^{2i/d_{\text{model}}}\bigr),\\ \mathrm{PE}(\mathit{pos}, 2i + 1) &= \cos\bigl(\mathit{pos} / 10000^{2i/d_{\text{model}}}\bigr). \end{aligned}\]

    Each pair of numbers is a sinusoid of its own frequency: from one turn per 6.3 positions down to very slow ones. Neighboring positions get similar vectors, distant ones less similar vectors. The 2017 paper chose sinusoids in the hope that they would extend to sequences longer than those seen in training. Learned positional embeddings gave almost the same result. In ACT the image positions are 2D sinusoids, in Decision Transformer (a transformer for reinforcement learning, 2021) an embedding of the time step, and in Gemma RoPE: it rotates the query and key vectors by an angle that depends on the position.

    Our command. A word token is 4 content numbers (one per word) and 16 position numbers; without positions the latter are zero. The action token's query likes objects, the cup slightly more than the bowl: that's how “training” turned out. It also likes the start of the command: its positional part matches the encoding of position 0. The answer is the object with the larger weight. Without positions the cup always wins, and Ada is wrong in all 12 of the 24 commands where the bowl comes before the cup. With positions the earlier object gets the larger weight, and there are no errors in any of the 24 permutations.

    4 Cost

    What tokens cost

    Attention compares every token with every other: double the number of tokens and you get four times as many pairs. But only this term grows quadratically. The Q, K, V projections and the feed-forward block process tokens one at a time, and their cost grows linearly. π0 has about 870 tokens at a model width of 2048, and for now it's these linear parts that take most of a layer's compute. Cameras and resolution are a budget: each new camera adds hundreds of tokens.

    “Patch N” cuts the frame into N × N pixel squares; “stride 32” is a feature map of a convolutional network in which one cell covers 32 pixels, as with ResNet18 in ACT. “64 per frame” and “8 per frame” compress the frame's features to that many tokens, as GR00T N1 and TokenLearner in RT-1 do. d_model is the length of one token's vector, FFN is the layer's feed-forward block, FLOPs is the number of multiplications and additions.     Under the hood: what the calculator computes
    • attention: \(4 n^2 d L\) FLOPs — the product \(Q K^{\mathsf T}\) and the mix of the values \(V\);
    • projections and FFN: \(24 n d^2 L\) FLOPs — the matrices \(Q\), \(K\), \(V\), \(O\) and an FFN of width \(4d\);
    • KV cache: \(2 L H_{kv} d_{\text{head}} T\) numbers, 2 bytes each in bf16.

    This is a rough estimate for a standard transformer block: a multiplication and an addition count as two operations, the FFN has width \(4 d_{\text{model}}\), and norms and softmax are not counted. In real models the FFN is sometimes wider, and then the attention share is even smaller. Vaswani et al. compared layers the same way: a self-attention layer costs \(O(n^2 d)\), a recurrent one \(O(n d^2)\), and attention is cheaper as long as \(n < d\). For π0, \(n \approx 870\) with \(d_{\text{model}} = 2048\) (our calculation).

    FlashAttention doesn't remove the quadratic cost: the FLOPs stay \(O(n^2 d)\), but the extra memory is \(O(n)\), and the speedup comes from fewer accesses to GPU memory. We count the KV cache for the prefix, that is, the observation tokens. The Gemma 2B language model inside π0 has 18 layers, one KV head and \(d_{\text{head}} = 256\): about 18 KB per token in bf16, and a prefix of 816 tokens takes about 15 MB (our calculation). The calculator keeps one KV head with \(d_{\text{head}} = 256\) for any model width. For π0 the cache takes little memory; what's expensive is time: the encoders and the pass over the observation take 46 ms of the 73.

    5 KV cache and chunks

    Why 73 ms gives 50 Hz

    When a model builds its output over several steps, the keys and values of the tokens it has already processed don't change. They are computed once and stored. This is the KV cache. π0 caches the observation this way, the images and the command: the block mask from the lab (it forbids observation tokens from looking at action tokens) guarantees that the observation doesn't depend on the actions. The model refines the action chunk over 10 flow matching steps (lesson 1.8), and each step reads the ready K and V instead of running the 816 observation tokens through again.

    The second idea is chunks. π0 outputs 50 actions at once, and the model is rerun once every 0.5 s. The robot gets a new action every 20 ms, even though the model is called twice a second. Below is π0 inference on an RTX 4090 with three cameras. Click the blocks and the tick tape.

    Under the hood: the inference numbers and how other systems work

    π0 has 3.3B parameters: Gemma 2B with the SigLIP image encoder and a 300M action expert. Inference on an RTX 4090 with three cameras: image encoders 14 ms, the pass over the observation 32 ms, 10 flow matching steps 27 ms, 73 ms in total. If the model runs off the robot, the network adds its share: 86 ms. The “no cache” time is our estimate using the same scheme: 14 + 10 × (32 + 2.7) ≈ 361 ms. The KV cache only works at inference: in training and in the first pass everything still has to be computed.

    In the real-time chunking paper (Black, Galliker, Levine, 2025), the KV cache prefill of π0 takes 46 ms with a 20 ms control step: more than two ticks pass during one inference. We'll cover how to stitch chunks without pauses or jerks in lesson 1.7.

    Other systems are built differently; we'll cover them in Part 3. π0-FAST (a variant of π0 with actions as tokens; lesson 3.6) spends about 750 ms per chunk versus ~100 ms for π0: the 2B model decodes 30–60 action tokens one at a time. GR00T N1 (2.2B parameters, 1.34B of them in the VLM) outputs a chunk of 16 actions in 63.9 ms on an L40; the VLM runs at 10 Hz, the actions at 120 Hz. In Helix (Figure AI, 2025; lesson 3.7) the slow 7B VLM S2 runs at 7–9 Hz, while the fast 80M policy S1 runs at 200 Hz and controls 35 degrees of freedom.

    6 Models

    How models output actions

    They all have a transformer inside, but they output actions in different ways. You can write an action as a string of tokens and generate them one by one, like text. You can output the whole chunk in one decoder pass. Or you can get the chunk from noise in a few steps of diffusion or flow matching. The method determines how many model passes one chunk takes.

    Eight common misconceptions

    8 Takeaways

    Lesson takeaways

    1. A policy turns everything into tokens: image patches, command words, the state, actions. The number of tokens is the main lever on cost.
    2. Attention: the query is compared with the keys, softmax gives the weights, and the output is a mix of values. The query length sets the sharpness, and when the weights are spread out the output lands between the options.
    3. Dividing by \(\sqrt{d_k}\) keeps the scores in a working range: without it softmax collapses into a single peak, the gradient is near zero, and the network can't learn to look at other tokens.
    4. The mask decides who sees whom. π0's block mask separates the observation from the actions, so the observation can be cached. Heads look at different features, and together they tell apart what each one confuses.
    5. Attention by itself doesn't know order: positional encodings supply it.
    6. Only the attention term is quadratic. The KV cache speeds up inference, and chunks decouple the control rate from the inference rate: 73 ms per inference and 50 Hz on the robot.

    Open questions

    You want to give a policy a fourth high-resolution camera. What do you calculate before deciding?

    How many tokens the camera adds: in PaliGemma a 224 / 448 / 896 px image is 256 / 1024 / 4096 tokens. How much the attention term grows and what its share of the layer cost becomes. How much KV cache it adds and, most importantly, how much time before the first flow step: for π0 that's already 46 ms of 73. Whether inference fits into the interval between chunk launches. And what training will cost: OpenVLA at 384 px instead of 224 trained 3 times longer with no gain in quality. It's often cheaper to compress the tokens: TokenLearner in RT-1 keeps 8 per frame, GR00T N1 keeps 64.

    Why can π0 control at 50 Hz if one inference takes 73 ms?

    Because the model outputs a chunk of 50 actions, and the robot executes 25 of them at 20 ms each: the model is needed once every 0.5 s, and 73 ms fits into that interval. Inference itself is fast thanks to the KV cache: the observation is processed once, and the 10 flow steps cost 27 ms. Helix and GR00T N1 go further and split a slow VLM (7–10 Hz) from a fast policy (120–200 Hz).
