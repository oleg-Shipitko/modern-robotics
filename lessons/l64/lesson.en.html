6.4 Part 6 · Humanoid robots

Loco-manipulation: the upper-level model sets the goals, the whole-body controller keeps balance

In lesson 6.3, Ada learned to reproduce any feasible whole-body motion, but her commands came from a recording or from an operator in a VR headset. For her to carry a cup to a shelf on her own, the commands have to come from a model that sees the scene and understands the task, and such models respond a few times a second (lesson 3.7). The legs can't wait that long, so the works of 2024–2026 split control into levels. HumanPlus (2024) trains a whole-body tracker in a simulator and, from 40 teleoperation demonstrations, trains a model that outputs target poses for this tracker from camera images 25 times a second. TWIST2, SONIC and Helix 02 put a diffusion policy, a VLA model or a slow system on top of the tracker and collect its data by whole-body teleoperation. In this lesson, you'll compare an Ada controlled directly by a slow model with an Ada that has a whole-body controller beneath the model, train two policies on the same recordings and work out what the upper-level model specifies for the legs in real systems.

1 Task

Carrying, pushing, squatting: the arms and legs work together

Carrying a cup, pushing a door open, squatting for a dropped toy, closing a drawer with a hip when the hands are full. Work with the hands during which the body also walks, squats or bends is called loco-manipulation. Figure explains why it's hard: neither walking nor working with the hands is hard on its own, but together they don't decompose into parts. Lift something and your balance changes; step forward and your reach changes. Classical robotics, the same post explains, works around this by handing locomotion and manipulation to separate controllers and stitching them together with a state machine. The press release from Boston Dynamics and TRI says the same: the humanoids that could already do this kept their balance with one controller and controlled their arms with another.

Ada is the course's home humanoid; since lesson 6.1, she has legs instead of a wheeled base. More about her and her home in lesson 0.1.

Why not train one policy for the whole body and the whole task? Two things get in the way. The first concerns data. Reinforcement learning in a simulator is good at teaching walking (lesson 6.1), but for a task with a cup and a shelf, the simulator would have to render camera frames realistically, model cloth and soft objects and be able to pose all sorts of tasks. The HumanPlus authors name this as the reason why data for tasks is easier to collect on the real robot. That takes teleoperation of the whole body, and recordings are expensive (lesson 4.2). The second difficulty has to do with frequency. The scene and the request are understood by a vision-language model with billions of parameters. In Helix it runs 7–9 times a second, and in GR00T N1 10 times (lesson 3.7). Meanwhile, Digit's walking policy from lesson 6.1 updates the target angles 50 times a second, and the joint controllers recompute the torques 1,000 times.

A wheeled robot has no trouble with frequency. Mobile ALOHA (Fu, Zhao and Finn, Stanford, 2024) consists of two ALOHA arms on a wheeled base and costs $32,000. The operator is strapped to the base with a belt, guides the robot's arms with the leader arms and pushes the base along: with the motors switched off, the wheels turn easily. The recording takes in 14 target arm angles and two base velocities, linear and angular, and the ACT policy from lesson 1.7 outputs all 16 numbers at once. A 14 kg battery sits at the bottom of the base and serves as a counterweight, so the robot doesn't tip over. With 50 demonstrations per task and co-training on static ALOHA recordings, it called an elevator and entered it in 95% of attempts, and without co-training it never succeeded. If a velocity command is late, the wheeled base will go the wrong way, but it won't fall.

For Ada with legs, a late command means a step in the wrong place. Hence the solution the works in this lesson arrived at: split control into two levels with different frequencies and roles. The upper level is taken by a model that sees the scene and understands the task: in Helix it's the vision-language-action model, in HumanPlus a policy that works from camera images. It runs rarely, a few times a second, and sets goals: where to go and what to do with the hands. The lower level is taken by a whole-body controller: a policy like the trackers of lesson 6.3, trained with reinforcement learning in a simulator, which 50 times a second receives a command and the readings of the body's sensors and outputs target joint angles so that the robot keeps its balance, places its feet and guides its hands to the goal. These are the same slow and fast systems from lesson 3.7 that different teams converged on, only now the fast system is given the legs as well. The command is what the upper level passes to the lower one: the torso velocity, the hand positions or the joint angles. What happens if there's no lower level and a model that responds 5 times a second drives the legs itself?

🔬 Lab 1

Ada carries a cup: the model drives the body itself or sets goals for the controller

Ada carries a cup of tea to a shelf two meters from the start. The upper-level model is the same for both Adas. At each of its frames it decides how fast to walk so as to stop at the mark on the floor, and its answer arrives 1/f seconds later, like the slow model of lesson 3.7, which spends its whole period computing. In its head it has an exact model of Ada's body and the same whole-body controller that runs at the lower level of the second Ada, so both make the same decisions about speed and steps. This is the best case for a slow model: a real VLA model has no exact body model in its head.

The Ada with the purple outline gets from the model a ready-made whole-body plan for the time until the next answer: where to press on the floor, where and when to place the feet and how to hold the arm with the cup. This plan is executed by soft PD controllers at the joints. The Ada with the orange outline gets only the velocity from the model, and her legs and arm are controlled 50 times a second by the whole-body controller from fresh sensor readings. The cup is filled to 1.5 cm below the rim, and the tea spills when its surface tilts by more than 20.6°. Along the way, Ada is pushed in the torso several times.

Under the hood: the body, the steps, the upper-level model, the cup and the pushes

Body. The dimensions are the same as in lesson 6.1: a 0.46 m torso, a thigh and a shin of 0.30 m each, the ankle 5 cm above the sole, the heel 6 cm behind the ankle, the toe 12 cm in front of it, and a mass of 28 kg. We treat the torso with the legs as a linear inverted pendulum. The center of mass stays at a height of 0.66 m above the floor and accelerates faster the farther it is from the support point \(p\) under the foot, and a push adds a force \(F\):

\[ \ddot x = \omega^2 (x - p) + \frac{F}{m}, \qquad \omega = \sqrt{g / z_c} \approx 3.86\ \text{s}^{-1}. \]

The support point doesn't go beyond the heel and the toe of the support foot, or beyond the edges of both feet when both are on the floor. Without moving the support, the deviation grows as \(e^{\omega t}\), that is, by a factor of e every 0.26 s. Ada falls when the center of mass gets farther than 0.26 m from the support ankle: the leg can no longer reach. The physics step is 2 ms.

Steps and the whole-body controller. It's convenient to look at the divergent component of motion \(\xi = x + \dot x / \omega\): this is the point where the support has to be placed to stop. With the support at \(p\), it moves away from it exponentially, \(\xi(t) = p + (\xi_0 - p)\, e^{\omega t}\). The whole-body controller, 50 times a second and at each touchdown, chooses the support point within the foot and the step target so that by touchdown \(\xi\) ends up in front of the new foot at a distance of

\[ b = \frac{L}{e^{\omega T} - 1}, \qquad L = v\,T,\quad T = 0.40\ \text{s}, \]

where \(v\) is the commanded velocity. If such a step target is farther than the leg can reach (0.22 m from the center of mass), the step is taken earlier, but no sooner than 0.22 s. This is how classical walking controllers work. On real robots, this is where a trained tracker from lesson 6.3 stands, but for the lab what matters isn't how the controller is built but how often it sees the body.

PD controllers. They pull the torso toward the plan's support trajectory and shift the support by 0.5 cm for every centimeter of deviation, plus another 0.12 cm for every centimeter per second of velocity. To hold a torso that has deviated without a new plan, the support would have to be shifted farther than the torso deviated, so PD controllers alone won't hold Ada: they're soft, like those of the policies in lesson 6.1.

Upper-level model. At each frame it chooses the velocity \(v = \min(1.4\,d,\ 0.45\ \text{m/s})\) from the distance \(d\) to the mark and runs an exact model of Ada without pushes in its head until the next answer arrives, with a margin of 0.12 s. To the Ada with the whole-body controller it hands the velocity for every 20 ms, and to the Ada with the plan the whole plan: the support, the step targets, the touchdown times and the position of the hand relative to the torso.

Cup. The hand is guided by the arm, which tracks its target with a natural frequency of 30 rad/s. The whole-body controller keeps the hand 24 cm in front of a smoothed track of the pelvis and recomputes the shoulder and elbow angles from the fresh position of the torso. For the Ada with the plan, the arm angles are taken from the plan, and the hand moves together with the torso. The surface of the tea swings like a pendulum driven by the acceleration of the hand. Its frequency is the lowest natural frequency of liquid sloshing in a cup of 4 cm radius at a depth of 6 cm, 3.37 Hz, and the damping is 0.05. The tea spills at a tilt greater than \(\arctan(1.5/4) = 20.6^\circ\).

Pushes. The first comes 0.6–1.4 s after the start, and after that on average once every 2.2 s. Each one changes the torso's velocity by 0.1–0.3 m/s forward or backward over 0.1 s, that is, it pushes with a force of 28 to 84 N. Run number k uses seed k, so both Adas get the same pushes. The strong push in mission 4 comes at 2.1 s and changes the velocity by 0.6 m/s, which is 168 N.

Other push series. Over ten series of 20 runs with push seeds from 1 to 200, the Ada with the whole-body plan places the cup at 2 Hz in 0 runs out of 20, at 5 Hz in 0–1, at 10 Hz in 0–1, at 25 Hz in 1–6 and at 50 Hz in 17–20. The Ada with the whole-body controller places the cup at 2 Hz in 16–20 runs out of 20, at 5 Hz in 18–20, at 10 Hz in 18–20, at 25 Hz in 19–20 and at 50 Hz in 19–20, and she fell once in all 1,000 runs. She stops on average 1.4–2.6 cm from the mark.

The same in any browser. The engine's exponential, logarithm, sine and arctangent are built from additions and multiplications, as in lessons 5.2–6.3, so Node, Chrome and Safari compute exactly the same thing.

At 5 Hz, the Ada with the whole-body plan spilled the tea at 1.2 s, right after the first push, and fell at the shelf at 8.4 s. The Ada with the whole-body controller placed the cup in 7.0 s. In a series of 20 runs at 5 Hz, the Ada with the plan never got the cup there: in 15 runs she spilled the tea, in 3 she ran into the shelf and in 2 she fell. At 10 and 25 Hz she no longer falls, but the tea spills in almost every run, and only at 50 Hz, when the model runs as often as the whole-body controller, does the cup end up on the shelf in 19 runs out of 20. The Ada with the whole-body controller places the cup in 19–20 runs out of 20 at any model frequency. At 2 Hz, where the Ada with the plan falls in 19 runs out of 20, the Ada with the controller stops on average 1.9 cm from the mark, and at 50 Hz 1.7 cm. After the strong push at 2.1 s, the whole-body controller placed the next foot 27 cm forward instead of 18, and the Ada with the plan fell at 2.5 s. Without pushes, both Adas place the cup in the same time, 6.6 s: their decisions really are the same.

The upper-level model has enough to do deciding where to go and how fast: those decisions change slowly. Balance and the cup have to be kept by a level that sees the body often. That's how the systems in this lesson are built, starting with the first one that went all the way from data collection to autonomous tasks.

2 HumanPlus

HumanPlus: a whole-body tracker on the lower level, a model working from cameras on the upper level

HumanPlus (Fu, Zhao, Wu, Wetzstein and Finn, Stanford, 2024) was the first to go all the way, from data collection to autonomous tasks. The robot is built on the Unitree H1: 180 cm tall, 33 degrees of freedom, including 6 in each hand, 5 in each leg and one in the waist, with two RGB cameras on the head. The lower level is called the Humanoid Shadowing Transformer (HST). It's a transformer trained with reinforcement learning in a simulator to reproduce human poses from AMASS: 40 hours of motion, more than 11,000 recordings, transferred to the robot's body. As input it gets the proprioception of the last 8 steps and a target pose, that is, the torso's forward, lateral and turning velocities, its tilts and the target angles of all the joints. 50 times a second it outputs target angles for the 19 joints of the body, and PD controllers turn them into torques 1,000 times a second. During training, the load on the torso (±3 kg) and on the hand (up to 0.5 kg), the position of the center of mass, the strength of the motors, the friction and the control latency from 20 to 40 ms were randomized, as in lesson 5.4, and HST transferred to the robot without fine-tuning. It withstands a push to the pelvis of up to 32–100 N depending on the direction, versus 24–40 N for the H1's stock controller, and usually recovers its balance within one or two steps.

HumanPlus collects its data by shadowing, with the robot following the operator's movements. The operator stands next to the robot in front of an ordinary webcam. The WHAM pose estimator recovers the pose of the operator's body 25 times a second, and HaMeR recovers the poses of the hands 10 times a second. Retargeting from lesson 6.2 turns the human pose into a target pose for the robot, and HST executes it. This way one person drives the robot's whole body, including squats and walking, and the equipment costs $50 versus $7,050 for a pair of ALOHA leader arms. In an experiment with six participants, moving objects took 5.20 s on average versus 7.15 s with leader arms and 8.87 s with Meta Quest controllers, and a task where the object lies on a low table and requires squatting could be done only with HumanPlus.

The upper level is called the Humanoid Imitation Transformer (HIT) and is built like ACT from lesson 1.7 with only the decoder kept. It gets features of the images from the two head cameras and the proprioception, and outputs a chunk of 50 target poses, that is, the same command that the retargeting gave during teleoperation. It also predicts the features of future images: according to the authors, this keeps the policy from overfitting to proprioception and no longer looking at the images. HIT runs on board 25 times a second and passes the poses to HST asynchronously. From 40 demonstrations, the robot put on a shoe, stood up and walked in 6 attempts out of 10, whereas ACT, HIT with one camera and replaying a recording never succeeded. From 25 demonstrations, it took a spray-paint can from a warehouse shelf, squatted and put it into a cart on the back of a quadruped robot in 9 attempts out of 10. The authors name the limitations themselves: an ankle with one degree of freedom, an arm with five, fixed cameras. And long routes, they write, will require far more demonstrations and accurate velocity tracking. In HumanPlus, the operator sees the robot from the side. How do other systems collect data?

3 Data

Whole-body teleoperation records the lower level's commands

TWIST2 (Ze, Zhao, Wang et al., Amazon FAR, Stanford, Berkeley and CMU, 2025) divides data collection systems for humanoids into three classes. In the first, the upper and lower body are controlled separately: in HOMIE from lesson 4.2, the operator sets the velocity, turning and torso height with pedals, and a policy trained with reinforcement learning walks on these commands. In the second, the arms and torso are coordinated, while the legs walk on a velocity command, as in AMO. In the third, a tracker reproduces the human's pose in all the joints, including the legs: this is how HumanPlus, TWIST and TWIST2 itself work. Teleoperation in which the operator drives the robot as a whole, from the feet to the hands, is called whole-body teleoperation.

TWIST (Ze, Chen, Araújo et al., Stanford, 2025) captured the operator with an OptiTrack motion capture system 120 times a second, and the latency from the human's movement to the robot's was about 0.9 s, of which 0.7 s went into building the targets. TWIST2 makes do with a PICO 4U headset and two trackers on the shins for $1,000 and is set up in a minute. All its modules run faster than 50 times a second, and its latency is under 0.1 s. The Unitree G1 robot was given a neck with two degrees of freedom for $250 so that the operator sees the scene through the robot's stereo camera. In 18.5 minutes, an experienced operator recorded 98 episodes of moving objects with both hands, and in 19.5 minutes 46 episodes with walking.

The main point of TWIST2 is that the same scheme is used for data collection and for operation. The lower level is one and the same: a tracker that receives a command 50 times a second and outputs target joint angles. The command includes the velocity, height and tilts of the torso, the turning rate and the angles of all the joints. The upper level comes in two kinds. During data collection it's the operator with retargeting, and during operation it's Diffusion Policy from lesson 1.5, trained on the recorded pairs of an image and a command. So the teleoperation recordings already contain ready-made actions for the future policy. HOMIE is built the same way, where the trained policy, as the authors put it, takes over from the operator and outputs the commands for the legs and the arm angles itself, and so is Atlas at Boston Dynamics and TRI: there, the policy uses the same control interface to the robot as teleoperation. The diagram shows how this works.

Diffusion Policy in TWIST2 gets a 224 × 224 image and, instead of raw sensor readings, the history of the commands it has issued itself. This way, the authors write, the upper level doesn't depend on the lower one and accumulates less error. It builds a chunk of 64 commands, about 2 s ahead. Inference runs 20 times a second on an RTX 4090, and 48 commands from the chunk are executed 30 times a second. From 170 demonstrations, the robot bent down, took a cup from a shelf and put it in a box on the floor, and from 50 demonstrations it pushed a T-shaped box to a mark on the floor with its foot and got it there in 6 attempts out of 7. The recordings contain both the operator's commands and the target angles that the whole-body controller output. Which of these should be learned?

🔬 Lab 2

What to learn from recordings: targets for the legs or commands for the controller

The operator walks Ada with the cup to the shelf several times through the same hierarchy as in Lab 1. Once every 0.1 s, the operator decides how fast to walk from what was visible 0.15–0.35 s ago, and the legs are controlled by the whole-body controller. The shelf stands in a different place each time, 1.4–2.4 m from the start. Each recording yields pairs of two kinds. The step policy learns to reproduce what the whole-body controller output 50 times a second: where to press on the floor and where to place the foot. As input it gets the distance to the mark and the state of the body, and it runs without the controller, also 50 times a second. The command policy learns to reproduce the velocity that the operator set 10 times a second, and runs on top of the same whole-body controller.

Both policies are small networks that train right in the browser, in about five seconds. For the first second of each run, Ada is driven by the operator, after that by the policy. The test consists of 20 runs in which the shelf stands in new places. The spread of results over training seeds 1–10 was computed in advance in Node with the same engine and is shown in the summary.

Under the hood: the recordings, the two networks and the test

Recordings. Once every 0.1 s, the operator chooses the velocity \(v = \min(k\,d,\ v_{\max})\) from Ada's position as seen \(\tau\) earlier. Each recording has its own \(k\) from 1.0 to 1.8, \(v_{\max}\) from 0.35 to 0.5 m/s and \(\tau\) from 0.15 to 0.35 s. A recording lasts until Ada places the cup, but no longer than 9 s. The body, the controller and the cup are the same as in Lab 1. When recording with pushes, Ada is pushed the same way as there.

Step policy. As input it gets five numbers: the distance to the mark, the position of the center of mass relative to the support foot, its velocity, the time since the start of the step and the position of the foot in the air. It outputs two numbers: the support point under the foot and the step target, while the step rhythm, 0.4 s, is given to it. A 5 → 48 → 48 → 2 network with leaky ReLU trains for 3,000 Adam steps on batches of 64 pairs with a learning rate of 0.003, which drops tenfold by the end, and minimizes the mean squared error:

\[ \mathcal L = \big\lVert a^{\text{controller}}_t - \pi(o_t) \big\rVert^2 . \]

The arm with the cup is guided for it by the same hand controller as for the whole-body controller, so the lab compares only the legs.

Command policy. As input it gets the distance to the mark and Ada's velocity, and it outputs the velocity for the whole-body controller. A 2 → 16 → 1 network trains for 1,500 Adam steps on batches of 32 pairs.

Test. In 20 runs, the shelf stands at distances from 1.5 to 2.4 m, in mixed order. The cup counts as placed if Ada stopped no farther than 8 cm from the mark. Ada runs into the shelf if the toe of her foot or her torso crosses its front edge.

Seeds 1–10. On training seeds 1–10 without pushes during recording, the step policy places the cup in calm tests in 0–18, 0–14, 0–12 and 0–8 cases out of 20 with 5, 10, 20 and 40 recordings, and in tests with pushes in 0–13, 0–9, 0–6 and 0–4. The command policy places the cup in calm tests in 10–20, 20, 20 and 20 cases, and with pushes in 10–20, 17–20, 15–20 and 13–20. After recordings with pushes, the step policy places the cup in tests with pushes in 6–19, 5–20, 11–20 and 12–20 cases out of 20, and the command policy in 15–20, 17–20, 17–20 and 17–20.

From 20 recordings, the step policy placed the cup in 6 tests out of 20: in 11 it didn't slow down in time and ran into the shelf, in 2 it stopped away from the mark, and in one it fell. The command policy placed the cup in all 20 tests. With 40 recordings, the step policy succeeded in 5 tests out of 20, and on training seeds 1–10 it places the cup in 0–18 tests with 5 recordings, in 0–14 with 10, in 0–12 with 20 and in 0–8 with 40. The command policy needs just 10 recordings to place the cup in 20 tests out of 20 on all ten seeds. With pushes during testing, the step policy succeeded in one test out of 20, and the command policy in all 20 and never once dropped Ada. When Ada was pushed during recording too, the step policy coped with pushes in 20 tests out of 20, and on seeds 1–10 in 11–20.

The step policy learns what the whole-body controller already knows, and learns it badly. Calm recordings contain no deviations and no corrections of them, so its small errors accumulate, as in lesson 1.1, and a torso on legs amplifies those errors on top of that. Recordings with pushes help in the same way as DAgger in lesson 1.2, but in them too Ada was caught by the whole-body controller, so the policy learned a copy of it. That's why the works in this lesson leave the controller at the bottom and learn from recordings only what it can't know: where to go and what to do with the hands. Exactly what the controller receives from the upper-level model, the works decide differently.

🧩 Interactive

What the upper-level model specifies for the legs: velocity, joints or a learned vector

The command that the upper-level model passes to the controller is the main choice in such a system. Which numbers the model outputs and what they mean for the lower level we'll call the interface between the levels. A simple interface is easy to learn, and the model's errors don't bring the robot down, but the model can't control what's hidden below through it. A detailed interface gives it more possibilities, but demands precision from it. Sort the systems by what the upper-level model specifies for the legs. Drag a card or click it and then a bin.

The numbers from SONIC (Luo, Yuan, Wang et al., NVIDIA, 2025) show the price of this choice. The authors connected the SONIC tracker from lesson 6.3 to the GR00T N1.5 VLA model, fine-tuned on teleoperation recordings, in two ways. Through three points, the model outputs the poses of the head and wrists, the waist height and a navigation command, and the leg motion is built by a kinematic planner. This way the robot walked up to a table, took an apple and put it on a plate in 90% of 20 attempts. Through the whole body, the model outputs the 64 numbers of a motion token, that is, a learned code that the tracker can execute, plus 14 hand joint angles. This way the robot opened a trash can by stepping on its pedal in 70% of attempts, and in the longest task it took a can from a table, walked to the trash can, opened it with one foot while standing on the other and dropped the can inside in 60%. When the model output explicit poses of all the joints instead of tokens, success on three tasks fell from 68 to 27% on average, and on the can task from 60% to zero. The authors explain it this way: in the space of whole-body poses, a small error of the model turns into a large tracking error, while compact tokens are easier to learn.

LeVERB (Xue, Huang, Niu, Liao et al., Berkeley and others, 2025) also replaces the explicit command with a latent vector from lesson 3.7. The vision-language model outputs it 10 times a second, and the whole-body controller turns it into target angles 50 times a second. Explicit commands like the torso velocity or the hand pose, the authors write, often produce jittery or unnatural motions, because the model predicts the infeasible. On their benchmark of more than 150 tasks, entirely synthetic, LeVERB succeeded in 58.5% of cases, and a naive hierarchy in 7.5%. On the real robot, the authors have so far replayed vectors recorded in the simulator, without visual feedback. On the other hand, TWIST2 kept the explicit angles of all the joints in the command and thanks to that taught the robot to push a box with its foot. There's no single answer yet: the more of the body the model specifies itself, the more it can do and the more precise it has to be.

4 Today

Loco-manipulation in 2026: levels and a shared interface with teleoperation

In 2026, such a hierarchy has become usual at companies too. Helix 02 (Figure, January 2026) has three levels, and their frequencies were discussed in lesson 3.7. For this lesson, what matters is what's passed between them. In the first Helix, the fast system S1 controlled only the upper body, while in Helix 02 it outputs joint targets for the whole body 200 times a second, and S0 executes them 1,000 times a second. This is how the robot closes a drawer with its hip and lifts the dishwasher door with its foot when its hands are full.

For Atlas (Boston Dynamics and TRI, August 2025), a 450-million-parameter model, a diffusion transformer trained with flow matching, outputs the poses of the hands, the torso and both feet 30 times a second from the images of a stereo pair of cameras on the head, proprioception and a request in words. It builds a chunk of 48 actions for 1.6 s and usually executes 24 of them before the next run. The press release says that a single model directly controls the entire robot and treats the hands and the feet almost identically. But it acts through the same interface as teleoperation, and teleoperation is built on Boston Dynamics' MPC (lesson 0.3), so a lower level that keeps balance exists here too. Policy execution was sped up 1.5–2 times without noticeably affecting the policy's performance.

NVIDIA's repository says that the GR00T N1.5 and N1.6 models worked on top of a decoupled controller: the lower body was driven by a policy trained with reinforcement learning, and the upper body by inverse kinematics. The same repository now holds SONIC and a workflow for the Unitree G1 in which GR00T N1.7 is fine-tuned on teleoperation recordings and controls the robot through SONIC. Gemini Robotics 2 (Google DeepMind, July 2026), according to the company, controls the Apollo 2 humanoid “from feet to fingertips” (lesson 3.7), but how the lower level is built and how often it runs, the announcement doesn't say.

Forces and the body itself remain open. FALCON (Zhang, Yuan et al., CMU, Field AI and Nissan, 2025) trains two agents with reinforcement learning, one for the legs and one for the arms, with a force curriculum, and on the robot carries a load with a force of up to 20 N, pulls a cart with up to 100 N and opens a door with up to 40 N. And in TWIST, the G1's motors overheated after 5–10 minutes of continuous operation, especially during squats.

Eight common misconceptions

6 Takeaways

Lesson takeaways

  1. Loco-manipulation doesn't decompose into walking and work with the hands: a load changes the balance, and a step changes where the hand reaches. Nobody trains one policy for the whole body and the whole task: the task needs data from the real robot, and the model that understands the scene runs a few times a second.
  2. Frequencies and roles are split. The upper-level model decides where to go and what to do with the hands, and the whole-body controller keeps balance 50 times a second. In Lab 1, the Ada whose model handed her a whole-body plan at 5 Hz didn't get the cup there in a single one of 20 runs and coped only at 50 Hz, while the Ada with the whole-body controller placed the cup in 19–20 runs out of 20 even at 2 Hz.
  3. HumanPlus trains the HST tracker with reinforcement learning in a simulator on 40 hours of human motion, collects data by shadowing in front of a single camera and trains HIT, which outputs target poses for the tracker 25 times a second. From 40 demonstrations, the robot put on a shoe, stood up and walked in 6 attempts out of 10.
  4. Whole-body teleoperation records the same commands that the policy will later output: in TWIST2, during data collection they come from the operator, during operation from Diffusion Policy, and the tracker beneath them is one and the same. TWIST2 records about 100 demonstrations in 15–20 minutes with a latency under 0.1 s.
  5. Learning targets for the legs from recordings instead of commands for the controller is a bad idea. In Lab 2, such a policy trained on 20 calm recordings placed the cup in 6 tests out of 20 and got no better as the number of recordings grew, because the recordings contain no deviations. Recordings with pushes rescue it, but then it copies the whole-body controller, which is there anyway.
  6. The interface between the levels is a choice between simplicity and possibilities. The torso velocity and the hand positions are easy to learn, but then the feet are chosen by the lower level. Joint angles allow working with the legs, as in TWIST2. SONIC's learned tokens gave 68% success versus 27% for explicit whole-body poses.

Open questions

Who handles force?

The controllers in this lesson keep balance under pushes, but they don't know that Ada is pushing a door or pulling a cart. FALCON trains a separate agent for the arms that implicitly compensates for the force at the hand and gets up to 100 N on a cart. What force to expect is known to the upper-level model, which sees the door and the cart. How do you pass that to the lower level through the interface between the levels?

Which interface is shared?

Simple commands are easy to learn, joint angles allow working with the legs, SONIC's tokens turned out better than explicit poses, but their meaning is understood only by the tracker they were learned on. Can there be an interface shared by different robots and different upper-level models, like the steps in words of Gemini Robotics ER in lesson 3.7, without losing the legs?
