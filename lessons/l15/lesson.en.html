Diffusion Policy

In lesson 1.3 we found that a policy needs a model of the action distribution; otherwise it averages the detour on the left and the detour on the right and drives straight into the kettle. In lesson 1.4 diffusion learned to draw samples from a distribution of any shape. Diffusion Policy combines the two ideas: diffusion builds not a single point but a chunk of future actions, and the latest observations serve as its condition. The initial noise picks a strategy, and the chunk holds it. In this lesson we'll train such a policy for Ada, find out how many actions of a chunk to execute, how many denoising steps are really needed and why the chunk consists of positions rather than velocities, and look at why the method works.

Ada is a home humanoid on a wheeled base; the course labs are built around her. More about her in lesson 0.1.

1 Task

Push-T: the task from the paper

Picking one strategy isn't enough for a policy: it has to execute it smoothly, on a real robot and in real time. In 2023 Chi et al. proposed Diffusion Policy for this and tested it on 12 tasks from four benchmarks: Robomimic, Push-T, Block Pushing and Franka Kitchen. The paper's main example is Push-T.

In Push-T a round agent, a pusher controlled by the policy or by a person with a mouse, pushes a T-shaped block into a green outline on the table. The task is multimodal: you can approach the block from the left or from the right and push it by the crossbar or by the stem, and operators do it differently. On a UR5 industrial arm Diffusion Policy solved the task in 95% of trials. Earlier methods did worse: IBC (an implicit policy based on an energy model) never succeeded, and LSTM-GMM (a recurrent network with a mixture of Gaussians at the output) succeeded in 20%.

Before comparing policies, let's sort out the metric: what exactly counts as a solution. The reward is the fraction of the goal covered, with a margin: once the block covers 95% of the goal, the reward is already 1. There's no agent in the interactive; you move the block yourself.

The metric says where to end up, but not how to get there. How does a policy pick one way out of several and not abandon it halfway?

Under the hood: the Push-T reward

The reward is the fraction of the goal covered, with a 5% margin:

\[r = \min\Bigl(1,\ \frac{S_{\text{covered}}}{0.95\, S_{\text{goal}}}\Bigr).\]

The benchmark metric is the best reward per episode, averaged over 50 starts. On the real robot the authors measured the overlap of the block with the goal at the last step (IoU): 0.80 for Diffusion Policy, 0.84 for a human operator.

In the interactive the block and the goal consist of a 140 × 35 crossbar and a 35 × 100 stem, and the goal is rotated by 45°. The intersection area is computed exactly: the block and the goal are two rectangles each, and the intersection of convex polygons is found by Sutherland–Hodgman clipping.

2 Chunk

Diffusion builds an action chunk

In lesson 1.4 diffusion generated a point on a plane. A policy needs not a point but a motion. So Diffusion Policy generates an action chunk: the robot's 16 future positions. The robot executes them in turn, one action per control step. Chunks already appeared in lesson 1.3: there a mixture of Gaussians produced them, it chose the detour side once for the whole chunk, and within the chunk Ada kept to that side. What's new here is how the chunk is built: the whole chunk starts as Gaussian noise and turns into a coherent motion in a few denoising steps.

The observations are not noised. Camera frames and the robot's position are only the condition: the network receives them at every denoising step and uses them to decide where to move the chunk. The generation procedure is the same in any situation; only the condition changes.

Why does a chunk hold a strategy? The detour side is chosen once for the whole chunk, and all 16 actions follow that choice. Scrub through the denoising of a chunk next to the kettle and find the denoising step at which the side is chosen. We count denoising steps from one: at step 1 the network makes the first estimate of the clean chunk from pure noise, and step 16 gives the finished chunk. There are 16 denoising steps here too, but that's a coincidence: the chunk has 16 actions, and 16 denoising steps mean that the network refines the chunk 16 times on the way from noise to finished actions. The side counts as chosen from the denoising step at which the clean-chunk estimate at the kettle's level has left the corridor extending 6 cm on either side of the route and stays on one side until the end of denoising: a smaller deviation is still noise, not a choice.

The dashed line is the estimate of the clean chunk at this denoising step, and the ring on it is the estimate's point at the kettle's level: the side is judged by that point. So the strategy is picked by the initial noise, and denoising carries the chunk through to one of the strategies present in the data. How do you turn this into a policy that works on a robot, from the camera to the motor?

Under the hood: denoising a chunk with a condition

Let's denote the chunk at denoising step \(k\) by \(a^k\) and the observations by \(o\). The network \(\varepsilon_\theta\) is trained to guess the added noise, as in lesson 1.4, only with the observations as an input:

\[\begin{gathered} L = \E\,\bigl\lVert \varepsilon - \varepsilon_\theta(a^k, k, o)\bigr\rVert^2,\\ a^k = \sqrt{\bar\alpha_k}\,a^0 + \sqrt{1 - \bar\alpha_k}\,\varepsilon. \end{gathered}\]

A DDIM step from \(k\) to an earlier \(k'\) first estimates the clean chunk, then adds the predicted noise back to it at a lower level:

\[\begin{aligned} \hat a^0 &= \frac{a^k - \sqrt{1 - \bar\alpha_k}\,\varepsilon_\theta(a^k, k, o)}{\sqrt{\bar\alpha_k}},\\ a^{k'} &= \sqrt{\bar\alpha_{k'}}\,\hat a^0 + \sqrt{1 - \bar\alpha_{k'}}\,\varepsilon_\theta(a^k, k, o). \end{aligned}\]

In the interactive the denoiser is exact and has no condition, as in lesson 1.3, and it is built from 40 demonstrations. The chunk consists of 16 sideways offsets of Ada around the kettle: 9 before it, one at its level and 6 after. We judge the side by the clean-chunk estimate \(\hat a^0\) at the kettle's level: more than 6 cm to the right of the route means right, more than 6 cm to the left means left, otherwise no side is chosen. The choice step is the first denoising step from which this side stays the same until the end. The lab below uses the same 6 cm dead zone to count plan switches. In the lab the denoiser is a trained network with a condition.

3 Loop

How the policy is built

One chunk for the whole task isn't enough: the scene changes, and the robot has to react. So the policy runs in a loop. It looks at the two latest frames, the encoder computes their features, denoising builds a chunk of 16 actions, the robot executes the first 8, and the loop repeats with new frames. This is the receding horizon you already met in lesson 0.3: plan far ahead, execute the beginning, re-plan.

Policy loop

    Blocks

    The authors chose the numbers 2, 16 and 8 empirically. A short observation history overfits less, a long chunk holds the strategy, and executing half of the chunk leaves time to react. Let's check how this works on Ada.

    Under the hood: the policy from the Diffusion Policy paper (Chi et al., 2023)

    This is how the policy from the Diffusion Policy paper (Chi et al., 2023) is built in its UNet variant, which the authors recommend as the starting point; the numbers are from the paper.

    • Input. The policy receives observations from the two latest control steps: frames from two cameras and the gripper position.
    • Encoder. Each camera is encoded by its own ResNet-18 without pretraining. It uses spatial softmax instead of global average pooling and GroupNorm instead of BatchNorm. The features are computed once per chunk inference.
    • Denoiser. It's a 1D UNet along the time axis of the chunk, and the observations and the denoising step number enter it through FiLM. There is also a transformer variant with cross-attention to the observations, but the authors recommend starting with the UNet.
    • Training. A random denoising step out of 100 is drawn, and the loss is the squared error between the noise and its prediction. Actions are normalized to the interval [−1, 1], and the weights are averaged exponentially (EMA).
    • Control. The chunk holds target positions, not velocities. For the authors position control was consistently better than velocity control; for the baselines it was the other way round.
    • Size and rate. The noise network has 256M parameters in simulation and 67M on the robot, the encoder 22M for two cameras. The chunk's actions run at 10 Hz, 0.1 s per action, so when 8 actions are executed (6 on the real Push-T) a new chunk is needed every 0.6–0.8 s. The robot's controller interpolates the actions to 125 Hz.

    Under the hood: the policies in the lab

    Observation. The policy sees Ada's positions at the previous and the current control step relative to the kettle, four numbers in total. Ada estimates her sideways position with an error of about 8 cm, as if from an imprecise sensor.

    Chunk. The chunk has 16 actions: Ada's positions 16 control steps ahead relative to the current one, in \(x\) and \(y\), 32 numbers in total. We execute \(T_a\) actions out of 16, then build a new chunk.

    Denoiser. It's a fully connected network. It takes the noisy chunk, the observation and the denoising step number (43 numbers), passes them through two layers of 64 neurons with tanh and outputs the estimate of the clean chunk. The noise schedule is cosine, training uses 100 denoising steps, and the training itself takes 3000 Adam steps with batches of 32, about two seconds in the browser. At inference DDIM runs with the chosen number of denoising steps.

    Regression with chunks. It's a 4 → 64 → 64 → 32 network trained on mean squared error for 2500 Adam steps. It outputs the whole chunk in one pass.

    Runs. Each launch consists of 10 runs: in each, the start is slightly shifted, and controller noise of about 0.5 cm is added to the motion. In the mission with the kettle shift, each launch includes the same 20 runs for any execution horizon: the kettle shifts 12 cm toward Ada's detour side at a control step between the 8th and the 19th. That way execution horizons are compared on equal terms.

    Metrics. We count a touch when the centers of Ada and the kettle come closer than 31 cm. A chunk's side is determined by the mean sideways offset of its points from the kettle while the kettle is no farther than 60 cm from them: more than 6 cm to the right means right, more than 6 cm to the left means left, otherwise no side is chosen. We count a plan switch when a new chunk with a side goes around the kettle on a different side than the previous chunk with a side; chunks without a side don't count.

    🧩 Interactive

    Position or velocity: what the policy outputs

    A chunk of 16 positions says where to be at each control step. The same chunk can be written as 16 velocities: how far to move at each step. The trajectory is the same, but the robot's controller executes the two encodings differently, and Chi et al. chose positions for a reason.

    The controller is a program on the robot that turns the policy's command into motor currents and runs much more often than the policy: in the paper the chunk's actions run at 10 Hz, and the controller interpolates them to 125 Hz. A velocity command says “move by this much”, and the controller counts the shift from where the arm actually ended up: if the previous step missed by a centimeter, that centimeter stays, and the misses add up. A position command says “go to this point”: the controller brings the arm there on its own, and the previous miss is erased by the very next step.

    Latency adds a second difference. The chunk is computed from frames taken several control steps ago, and by the time it arrives, the robot is no longer where it was when the chunk was computed. A velocity command is executed from the new place and leads off course, while the correction to the route, which sits in the chunk's first commands, arrives when their time has already passed. A position is an absolute target: a late or skipped command only means that the controller heads for the same point a little later. Hence the link to chunks: a chunk of positions sets a trajectory the controller returns to on its own, while a chunk of velocities only sets the shape of the motion from wherever the robot ends up. In the paper position control is consistently better than velocity control for Diffusion Policy, while for earlier methods it was the other way round. The authors attribute this to the multimodality of the demonstrations being more pronounced in positions: it hampers methods without a model of the distribution, but not diffusion.

    Check this on Ada. The policy isn't trained here: the chunk of 16 positions is built by the teacher that recorded the demonstrations in lesson 1.3, and it steers Ada back onto the detour route. The same chunk is executed by two controllers under latency and slight execution noise. The lab engine doesn't support this mode, so the execution model here is separate and simplified; it's described in “Under the hood” below.

    Under the hood: the execution model in the interactive

    Chunk. From Ada's observed position, the teacher from lesson 1.3 builds 16 positions ahead: at each step the shift is \(0.65\,(x_{\text{route}} - x)\) plus the route's own shift, at most 9 cm per step. The observation is exact. 8 actions out of 16 are executed, and then a new chunk is needed.

    Latency. A new chunk is requested when execution of the current one starts and arrives \(d\) steps later; until it arrives, Ada continues the old chunk by step number. The new chunk's commands for steps that have already passed are skipped; on the scene these are the gray dots.

    Controllers. Position: \(x \leftarrow x + (p_j - x) + \varepsilon\). Velocity: \(x \leftarrow x + (p_j - p_{j-1}) + \varepsilon\), where \(p_j\) are the chunk's points and \(\varepsilon\) is a Gaussian miss with the given spread in centimeters. The sequence of misses is the same for both controllers and for any settings: ten runs with seeds 1–10. Ada moves forward 6 cm per step without noise.

    Deviation. It's the difference between where Ada ended up after a step and the chunk point she was executing at that step. The deviation “at the kettle” is taken at the step at which Ada is closest to the kettle along the way, and “at the end of the path” at the last step; both are averaged over the ten runs. A touch is counted the same way as in the lab: the centers come closer than 31 cm.

    The model is simplified: there's no inertia and no acceleration limit, the observation is noise-free, and the latency is the same for all chunks. The paper compared control of a real UR5 arm, where the actuator dynamics add to these effects.

    4 Speed

    How many steps real robots take

    Each denoising step is a full pass of a large network. DDPM with 100 steps spends about a second on a T4 GPU. Meanwhile the chunk's actions run at 10 Hz, and with 6–8 actions executed a new chunk is needed every 0.6–0.8 seconds.

    In the lab Ada got by with three or four denoising steps, but not with one. A single step from pure noise gives just one estimate of the clean chunk at maximum noise. Such noise says almost nothing about where the chunk came from, so the best estimate is the mean of all demonstrations for this observation, that is, the answer of regression. In the same way, in lesson 1.4 the score arrows pointed to the mean at high noise. With one step, diffusion turns into regression, and Ada clips the kettle. At the second and later steps there is less noise, the estimate is pulled toward the nearest detour, and the side gets chosen.

    Real systems go the same way: they cut the number of denoising steps, change the generator and build the next chunk while the current one is executing.

What happens at the seam between chunks?

    5 Seams

    Chunk seams and latency

    Chunk seams appeared in lesson 1.3: there a new chunk was ready instantly, at a seam the mixture of Gaussians could pick the other side, and it turned out that seams should be kept consistent rather than averaged. What's new here is inference time. Diffusion Policy spends about 0.1 s on a chunk, and while the network computes, the robot either waits or continues the old chunk. Synchronous inference stops the robot, and the motion comes out with pauses. Asynchronous inference continues the old chunk and switches to the new one when it's ready. But the new chunk was built from outdated frames and may pick a different strategy, so a jump appears at the seam.

    You can't smooth the jump by averaging, for the same reason as in lesson 1.3: the average of two strategies can be an invalid action, and the π0 authors rejected averaging. Real-Time Chunking (RTC) from Physical Intelligence, which lesson 1.3 only named, works differently. The next chunk is built while the current one is executing; the actions that are already going to the motors are frozen, and the rest are inpainted so that the new chunk continues the one being executed. The method works with an already trained model, without retraining, and withstands latencies above 300 ms. The diagram below shows both approaches side by side: RTC on the left, naive chunk replacement on the right.

    We'll cover latency and ways of stitching chunks in detail in lesson 1.7, using ACT (a policy for the bimanual ALOHA robot, 2023) as the example. What remains is to understand why a diffusion policy works better than others at all, and whether the authors' explanation holds.

    6 Why it works

    Why the method works

    The authors give three explanations. The first is multimodality: the initial noise picks a strategy, denoising carries it through to the end, and there's no averaging. The second is the long output: diffusion describes a distribution over the whole chunk, so it can predict a consistent sequence of actions. The third is stable training: IBC has to estimate the normalization, a sum over all possible actions, from negative examples, while diffusion learns the score (the direction in which to move a point, from lesson 1.4) and doesn't need the normalization.

    Results, however, have to be kept apart from explanations. The average gain of 46.9% was measured in simulation. On real robots the authors report individual tasks: 95% on Push-T, 90% on the mug task. The paper doesn't prove that the gain comes from multimodality specifically.

    This explanation is disputed. According to Mazza et al., standard benchmarks have almost no conditional modes (lesson 1.3). A 2025 paper (arXiv 2512.01809) on 28 benchmarks attributes the advantage of generative policies to noise during training and iterative generation steps rather than to the model's expressiveness: a two-step regression policy was barely worse than a flow policy. And when demonstrations from different operators really do diverge, a generative head is needed: in ALOHA Unleashed (Google DeepMind bimanual tasks, 2024; lesson 1.7) diffusion reached 70% success versus 25% for regression with L1.

    The practical takeaway is this. If the data has one strategy, regression with chunks is cheaper and no worse. If operators solve the task in different ways or the scene allows several solutions, you need a generative policy.

    Eight common misconceptions

    8 Takeaways

    Lesson takeaways

    1. Diffusion Policy generates from noise an action chunk of 16 future positions, conditioned on the latest observations. The observations are not noised.
    2. The strategy is picked by the initial noise in the first denoising steps, and the chunk holds it, so the robot doesn't weave.
    3. Receding horizon: the policy predicts 16 actions and executes 8. Execute fewer, and the plan switches sides; execute more, and the robot notices scene changes late.
    4. The chunk consists of positions, not velocities: the controller drives toward an absolute target, execution misses don't accumulate, and a late command does less harm. In the interactive, with a latency of 3 control steps, velocities drifted from the chunk several times more than positions.
    5. Few denoising steps are needed: 16 for Diffusion Policy, 1–3 after distillation; flow matching policies take 10 flow steps in π0 and 4 in GR00T N1. One denoising step without distillation gives almost the mean of the demonstrations, like regression.
    6. Chunk seams are not averaged: the next chunk is built onto the one being executed, as in Real-Time Chunking.
    7. Multimodality is the authors' explanation, not a proven cause of the success. If the data has one strategy, regression with chunks is cheaper and no worse.

    Open questions

    Inference takes 100 ms, and the chunk's actions run at 10 Hz. How do you organize execution so that there are neither pauses nor jerks?

    Build the next chunk asynchronously while the current one is executing, and start inference early, as many actions before the end of the chunk as inference takes. Freeze the actions that will be executed during inference and inpaint the rest of the chunk onto them, as in Real-Time Chunking, instead of averaging chunks. The latency can also be simulated during training. In Hugging Face's LeRobot library, asynchronous inference cut the time per task from 13.75 to 9.7 s.

    The policy outputs positions, and the controller drives toward them on its own. What happens at contact, when Ada needs not to reach a point but to press on an object?

    Through a stiff controller, a target inside an object turns into a force the policy never specified: the controller keeps pushing until it reaches the point. In practice the position is executed by a compliant controller with a force limit, and for contact-rich tasks policies sometimes output not only a position but also a stiffness or a force. How such controllers live inside the loop of a trained policy is beyond the scope of this lesson.
