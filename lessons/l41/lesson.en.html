4.1 Part 4 · Data for training robots

The scale of data in robotics

In Part 3, models for robots grew the same way language models did: RT-1 was trained on 130,000 episodes that 13 robots collected over 17 months, π0 on 10,000 hours of recordings, and π0.5 added web data and subtasks in words to the robot recordings. Language models are trained on trillions of tokens from the internet, and people wrote that text long before the models appeared and for entirely different purposes. Robots have no such corpus: someone recorded every trajectory by hand, and an hour of an operator's work gives an hour of data. We'll work out by what factor these scales differ and why any such comparison is rough, where Ken Goldberg's estimate of a “100,000-year data gap” comes from, and what Lin et al. found in an experiment with 40,000 demonstrations: generalization to new settings grows with the number of different settings and objects, while extra demonstrations in the same setting add almost nothing.

1 Corpus

Trillions of tokens and thousands of hours

By robotics standards, the datasets of Part 3 are record-breaking. RT-1 from lesson 3.1 was trained on 130,000 episodes that 13 robots collected over 17 months. Open X-Embodiment from lesson 3.4 pooled 60 datasets from 22 robots, more than a million trajectories. π0 from lesson 3.6 was pre-trained on more than 10,000 hours of recordings. Are these datasets large by the standards of language models?

Llama 3 (Meta, 2024) was trained on more than 15 trillion tokens from publicly available sources, and Qwen3 (Alibaba, 2025) on 36 trillion. Nobody writes such corpora for the models: people have been publishing books, articles and forum posts for decades, and a corpus is assembled from what is already on the internet. Robots are a different story. Someone recorded every trajectory: an operator guided the robot's arm with a controller or held a gripper with a camera in their own hand, and an hour of recording cost an hour of their work. Ken Goldberg (UC Berkeley) put it this way in a 2025 interview with Berkeley News: “every eight hours of work gives you just eight more hours of data.”

These numbers can't be compared directly, because the units differ. Language model data is counted in tokens, pieces of words that are usually shorter than a word. Robot datasets are counted in episodes, trajectories or hours of recording. An episode is one attempt at a task from start to finish, and a trajectory is the recorded sequence of frames, states and actions of one attempt. An episode can last five seconds or several minutes. An hour of recording with control at 50 Hz contains 180,000 actions, plus frames from several cameras over the same time.

That's why the best-known comparison puts everything on a single scale: time. How many years would a person need to read a language model's corpus, and how many years of recording do robot datasets hold? This conversion is where the “100,000-year data gap” comes from.

🔬 Lab 1

The gap calculator: years of reading and years of recording

The estimate didn't start with Goldberg. In November 2024, Kevin Black of Physical Intelligence showed a slide in a talk, and he himself called it “a very silly thought experiment”: the slide compares language datasets with robotics datasets “in the most shallow way possible”, by time. He assumed that a person reads 238 words per minute and that there are 1.33 tokens per word. According to his slide, the Llama 3 corpus would take 790 million hours to read, while π0's data is 10,000 hours of recording.

In August 2025, Goldberg repeated the comparison in a column in Science Robotics. In his words, the data that modern vision-language models are trained on would take a human “on the order of 100,000 years” to read and view, while the largest robot dataset holds on the order of a year of recording. Hence the “100,000-year data gap” in the column's title. In a footnote, Goldberg cites Black's slide along with his words about a silly thought experiment: the estimate shows the order of magnitude, not an exact number.

Do the math yourself. The sizes of the corpora and datasets are taken from papers and official pages. We set the reading speed and the number of tokens per word as Black did, and you can change them.

Let \(T\) be the number of tokens in the corpus, \(\tau\) the number of tokens per word, \(w\) the number of words per minute, and \(d\) the number of hours in a day when converting to years: 24 or 8, the same for all the bars. The hours of reading the corpus, the years of reading and the years of recording for a dataset of \(h\) hours are computed like this:

\[\begin{gathered} H_{\text{text}} = \frac{T}{60\, \tau\, w}, \\ Y_{\text{text}} = \frac{H_{\text{text}}}{365\, d}, \qquad Y_{\text{robot}} = \frac{h}{365\, d}. \end{gathered}\]

With \(T = 15 \cdot 10^{12}\), \(\tau = 1.33\), \(w = 238\) and round-the-clock reading, this comes to about \(7.9 \cdot 10^8\) hours, as on Black's slide, or about 90,000 years. A fleet of \(N\) devices, each recording \(s\) hours a day, catches up with the corpus in \(H_{\text{text}} / (365\, N s)\) calendar years.

The assumptions strongly affect the answer. Replying to Black in December 2024, Goldberg compared π0 with Qwen2.5 and arrived at 1.2 billion hours for Qwen, about 120,000 person-years. That matches 18 trillion tokens if you count each token as a word; with Black's conversion, it comes to about 0.95 billion hours. Vision-language models are also trained on images and video: in his column, Goldberg converts both word tokens and image tokens into time, and he notes that “YouTube includes about 35,000 years of videos.”

A comparison in tokens also depends on what you count. One second of folding a T-shirt with two arms at 50 Hz contains 700 action numbers, and after FAST compression from lesson 3.6 they fit into 53 tokens. Then π0's 10,000 hours give about \(1.9 \cdot 10^9\) FAST tokens, 7,900 times fewer than 15 trillion; without compression, there are 600 times fewer action numbers than tokens in the corpus. If you add the camera images, the robot's token count grows by orders of magnitude, but the images of neighboring frames are almost identical and contain little that is new. So the gap is an order-of-magnitude estimate, not a measure you can shrink by a few percent.

Different assumptions change the answer severalfold, but not by five orders of magnitude. Goldberg draws a cautious conclusion from this: humanoids that can handle housework are “not going to happen in the next two years, or five years or even 10 years.” He proposes combining learned models with “good old-fashioned engineering” (GOFE): Kalman filters, inverse kinematics and motion planning, that is, the methods from lessons 0.3 and 0.4. Such robots already do paid work and collect data along the way: in four years, Ambi Robotics' parcel sorters gathered 22 years of recordings of real work, more than 200,000 operational hours. The data accumulates without an operator: every hour of useful work becomes an hour of recording.

But while data is scarce, every hour of it is expensive, and it matters what you spend it on. Should you record another thousand demonstrations in the same kitchen, or ten each in a hundred different kitchens? Which will teach a robot faster to work in a home it has never seen?

2 Laws

Data scaling laws in imitation learning

Language models are trained on all the text that could be collected, and nobody has to choose what to record. Roboticists do have a choice, and it costs money. To choose wisely, you need to know how a policy's success depends on what the data contains. For text, such dependencies have been known since 2020: a language model's loss falls as a power law as data, model size and compute grow (Kaplan et al., OpenAI). A scaling law is exactly such a formula: it describes how quality changes with scale. Is there a similar law for robots?

Fanqi Lin et al. (Tsinghua University, ICLR 2025) tested this on real robots. They split generalization into two dimensions: to new environments (a different table, lighting, background, distractor objects) and to new objects of the same category (a different bottle or a different mouse: color, size, shape). A dataset is described by three numbers: M environments, N objects and K demonstrations for each object in each environment. The policy is always tested where it didn't train: in new environments or with new objects. The authors didn't study generalization to new tasks: that, they write, would require data from thousands of tasks.

The demonstrations were recorded with UMI, a hand-held gripper: a person holds a gripper with a wrist camera and performs the task themselves, without a robot, so moving from one setting to another takes almost no setup (more on this in lesson 4.2). The authors trained Diffusion Policy from lesson 1.5 with a DINOv2 ViT-L image encoder. There are two tasks: pouring water from a bottle into a mug and putting the bottle on a coaster (Pour Water), and placing a computer mouse on a mouse pad facing forward (Mouse Arrangement). In total, the authors collected more than 40,000 demonstrations and ran more than 15,000 test rollouts on the robot.

The experiment works like this: out of 32 environments, they pick 1, 2, 4, …, 32, take a fraction of the demonstrations in each, from 1/32 to all 120, train a policy and test it in 8 new environments with 5 trials each. A trial is split into 2–3 stages, the tester gives up to 3 points for each, and the total is normalized so that the maximum is 1. The score was used instead of the success rate because success is too coarse a signal, and the action error on validation demonstrations, according to the authors, often doesn't match how the robot behaves.

The authors draw three conclusions. The first concerns the optimality gap: that's what they call the share of the maximum score that wasn't earned, that is, one minus the score. This gap falls roughly as a power law in the number of environments, objects or environment–object pairs: \(1 - S \approx \beta\, X^{-\alpha}\). On logarithmic axes, this is a straight line with slope \(-\alpha\): each doubling of \(X\) multiplies the gap by the same number, \(2^{-\alpha}\). The fits have correlation coefficients from 0.94 to 0.99. For Mouse Arrangement, the exponent \(\alpha\) is 0.47 over environments, 0.70 over objects and 0.68 over pairs. Environments are harder than objects: with just 8 objects, the score on new objects is already above 0.8.

The second: demonstrations in the same environment quickly stop helping. The curves for half and for all of the demonstrations almost coincide, and for environment–object pairs, even the curves for a quarter and for all of them coincide. The more environments or objects there are, the fewer demonstrations each one needs. There is no power law in the total number of demonstrations: for 8, 16 and 32 pairs, the growth stops at 400, 800 and 1,600 demonstrations. That's why the authors recommend 50 demonstrations per pair for tasks of similar difficulty; dexterous tasks may need more.

The third: diversity matters more than quantity. Even with the same total number of demonstrations, more environments and objects give better generalization. Several objects in one environment help while there are few environments, but with 16 environments the difference disappears, so the authors advise collecting data in as many environments as possible, with one object in each. Following this recipe, four people recorded 32 pairs with 50 demonstrations each in one afternoon for two new tasks: folding a towel and unplugging a charger from a power strip. The policies succeeded in 87.5% and 90% of trials in new environments with new objects.

Why do environments matter more than demonstrations, and where do demonstrations stop paying off? You can check this in a world where every number is recomputed in seconds.

🔬 Lab 2

A scaling curve on a table with a cube

Ada is a home humanoid on a wheeled base; the course labs are built around her. More about her in lesson 0.1.

Ada is learning one task: picking up a cube from the table. The table is the same as in lesson 2.1: a top view, a 96 × 96-pixel image, and the gripper comes down into one of the cells of a 24 × 24 grid. A scene here is an environment–object pair, as in Lin et al. Each scene has its own table color, its own lighting, three or four distractor objects (bowls and cans of different colors) and its own cube with its own color and size. The demonstrations of one scene differ only in the arrangement: the operator lays out the same objects differently and shows where the cube is.

The policy is a small network similar to the “where to pick” map from lesson 2.1. It looks at a 13 × 13-pixel window around each cell and gives the cell a score, and the gripper comes down into the cell with the highest score. On each set, the policy is trained from scratch for the same number of steps, and in the browser this takes less than a second. We test the policy on 100 new scenes that aren't in any set: each has a new table, lighting, distractors and cube. If Ada picked up the cube, the trial is a success, and if the gripper came down somewhere else, it's a failure. The sets are nested, as in the paper: the set of 8 scenes contains the set of 4.

The table is shown from above. On the left are the set's scenes, one arrangement each; the number in a tile's corner says how many demonstrations were recorded in the scene. On the right are the first 16 of the 100 test scenes for the most recently trained model. A green ring means Ada picked up the cube, a red cross means the gripper came down somewhere else, and a dashed frame marks where the cube was.

Horizontal: the number of scenes in the set; vertical: the failure rate in 100 new scenes. Both axes are logarithmic: each step to the right doubles the number of scenes, and going from 100% to 10% takes the same distance as going from 10% to 1%. A point's color shows how many demonstrations were recorded per scene, and the line of the same color is the straight line fitted to those points. Scenes
Under the hood: the table world, the policy and how our experiment differs from the paper

Scenes. The table gets a random light shade. The lighting sets a random multiplier for each of the three color channels: it averages about 1, with a spread of 0.18. There are three or four distractor objects: bowls and cans of random colors and sizes. The cube has its own color and size in each scene. In each arrangement, the objects lie in random places without touching each other, and the lighting changes slightly.

The policy. For each of the 576 cells, we take a 13 × 13-pixel window around its center. The network sees not the color itself but the absolute value of its difference from the table color, which it estimates as the median over the image; differences below 0.04 count as noise. The window gives 507 numbers, which pass through 8 neurons with tanh and turn into a single score. The cell probabilities come from a softmax over the scores, as with the “where to pick” map in lesson 2.1:

\[\begin{gathered} p(i) = \frac{e^{f(x_i)}}{\sum_j e^{f(x_j)}}, \qquad \mathcal L = -\log p(i^\ast), \end{gathered}\]

where \(i^\ast\) is the cube's cell in the demonstration. To keep training fast, the sum in the denominator runs over 32 cells: the cube's cell, the cells of the distractor objects and random ones. We train on every set the same way: we take 300 Adam steps on batches of 8 demonstrations with a step size of 0.005. A grasp counts as a hit if the gripper comes down closer than 4.5 pixels to the cube's center.

The line. From the points of one color with a nonzero failure rate, least squares fits \(\log \beta\) and \(\alpha\) in \(\log(1 - S) = \log\beta - \alpha \log M\), where \(S\) is the success rate and \(M\) is the number of scenes. The paper fits the same way, only on the score rather than the success rate.

How it differs from the paper. We have one object category and environment–object pairs, not three experiments. We count the success rate instead of a tester's score. On every set, the policy trains for the same number of steps, while the paper trained larger sets for longer. The numbers depend on the initial weights: we repeated the whole grid with four different initializations, the numbers shifted by a few scenes out of 100, and the conclusions stayed the same.

On the table, we reached the same conclusions as the paper. The failure rate in new scenes falls almost along a straight line on logarithmic axes, until there are so many scenes that only a handful of failures remain. Demonstrations matter while there are few of them: with one demonstration per scene, the result is noticeably worse than with eight, while sixteen are hardly different from eight. The reason lies in the world itself: the demonstrations of one scene differ only in the arrangement, and the policy scores every cell with the same rule, so a new position of the cube adds almost nothing. A new scene brings a new table, lighting, distractors and cube, which is exactly what the test will bring too.

The line describes how success grows as you add scenes. But someone who collects data usually has a different question: there is already a budget for as many recordings again, so where should they go?

Under the hood: what decides when demonstrations stop helping

The policy scores each cell by the window around it with one and the same rule. If the cube lies somewhere else in a demonstration, that adds nothing to the rule: it can already find the cube in any cell. So new arrangements of the same scene are useful only while there are very few of them: they show the cube next to different distractors and under slightly different light. After that, the network already knows everything it can learn from the scene.

For a real robot, the arrangement matters more. The object's position determines how to approach and turn the arm, where the elbow ends up and what blocks the camera's view; the robot has to learn the motion too, not just the grasp location. That's why the threshold in Lin et al. is higher, at 50 demonstrations per environment–object pair, and dexterous tasks, the authors write, may need more. But in their experiments too, growth stops past the threshold.

Doubling the demonstrations in the same scene gave almost nothing, while a second scene with the same four demonstrations raised success more than one and a half times. But the rule “always add scenes” is wrong too: when each scene has one demonstration, a second one helps as much as new scenes do. It pays best to keep the number of demonstrations per scene at the threshold and spend the rest on diversity. What does this mean for those who collect data for large models?

3 Today

Data collection in 2025–2026

By 2025, the conclusion that “diversity matters more than quantity” had become common wisdom, and data collection moved to where diversity is cheaper. DROID (2024) contains 76,000 trajectories, about 350 hours of recording in 564 scenes from 52 buildings; 50 people recorded them over 12 months on identical robots. AgiBot World (2025) has more than a million trajectories and almost 3,000 hours of recording across 217 tasks in 106 scenes; more than a hundred identical robots collected them. How such datasets are built, how their diversity is measured and how bad recordings are filtered out is the subject of lesson 4.3.

In November 2025, Generalist AI reported more than 270,000 hours of real-world manipulation data for its GEN-0 model, growing by 10,000 hours a week and accelerating. The data is collected by thousands of collection devices and robots in homes, warehouses and workplaces. The authors write about a power-law relationship between the amount of pre-training data and performance after post-training, and about a threshold in model size: models smaller than 7B parameters exhibit “ossification”, meaning they stop absorbing new pre-training data, whereas in language models this was observed at sizes around 10M. In April 2026, GEN-1 was trained on half a million hours, and its base model was trained without any robot data at all: the recordings were made with low-cost wearable devices on people.

Other companies mix sources. For π0.5, Physical Intelligence recorded about 400 hours of mobile robots in roughly 100 homes, but in the first training stage, 97.6% of the examples came from other sources: data from other robots, web data and subtasks in words. The same paper repeated the experiment with the number of environments: the model was post-trained on recordings from 3, 12, 22, 53, 82 and 104 locations with the same number of steps. Success on four tasks in unfamiliar homes generally grew with the number of locations, and the 104-location model worked about as well as a model that had seen recordings from the test homes themselves. For the first version of Helix (2025), Figure made do with about 500 hours of teleoperation. For GR00T N1, NVIDIA built a pyramid: human videos and web data lie at the bottom, simulation and generated video in the middle, and a small amount of real robot recordings at the top. We'll take this pyramid apart in lesson 4.6.

Even half a million hours is about 57 years of recording, roughly 1,600 times less than the 90,000 years it would take to read the Llama 3 corpus. The gap is being closed from all sides at once: demonstrations are recorded more cheaply, models learn from human video, data is simulated, and robots that are already at work collect it. Lesson 4.2 starts with the first of these: where demonstrations come from and what an hour of them costs.

5 Takeaways

Lesson takeaways

  1. Language models are trained on trillions of tokens that people wrote long before the models appeared. Robot data is recorded by someone on purpose, and an hour of an operator's work gives an hour of recording.
  2. The “100,000-year data gap” comes from converting the corpus into reading time. It's a thought experiment by Kevin Black that Ken Goldberg repeated. The assumptions change the answer severalfold, but the order of magnitude stays the same.
  3. In the experiments of Lin et al., the optimality gap on new environments and objects falls as a power law in the number of environments, objects and environment–object pairs: each doubling multiplies it by the same number, which gives a straight line on logarithmic axes.
  4. Demonstrations in the same setting help only up to a threshold: beyond it, the curves coincide. The authors recommend 50 demonstrations per environment–object pair and as many different environments as possible, with one object in each.
  5. Below the threshold, demonstrations are needed: with one demonstration per scene, a second one helps as much as new scenes do. It pays to keep the number of demonstrations per scene at the threshold and spend the rest on diversity.
  6. In 2025–2026, the gap is being closed from all sides: GEN-0 and GEN-1 are trained on hundreds of thousands of hours from collection devices, π0.5 on a mixture of robot recordings and web data, GR00T N1 on a data pyramid, and working robots collect data along the way.

Open questions

According to the fit by Lin et al. for Mouse Arrangement, a score of 0.99 would take 1,191 environment–object pairs. Why can't you trust such a forecast without checking it?

The line was drawn through six points from 1 to 32 pairs, and the forecast goes 37 times further. Any task has an irreducible share of failures caused by errors of the gripper, the sensors and the operator, and a straight line on logarithmic axes doesn't see it. The authors point this out in a footnote: a model with a third parameter, the floor, “does not seem statistically justified given that we have only 6 data points.” They left the forecast to be verified in future work, and in our lab, the points at the bottom of the plot are already noisy because there are so few failures.

For Lin et al., diversity means environments and objects. What other axes of diversity does a model that performs many tasks need?

They include the tasks themselves and the wording of commands, robots with different bodies and different actions (lesson 3.4), viewpoints and camera types, and the manner of different operators. The authors deliberately didn't study generalization to new tasks: it needs data from thousands of tasks. How such axes are counted in large datasets is covered in lesson 4.3: DROID, for example, measures scenes, tasks and camera positions separately.
