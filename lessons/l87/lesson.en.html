Market: companies and strategies

One robot may work in a warehouse, but four parties share responsibility for the outcome: the body manufacturer, the model developer, the integrator, and the support team. A list of companies therefore explains little on its own. We will examine four strategies, distinguish a demonstration video from a pilot and commercial operation, and then build the path from one successful site to a repeatable fleet.

1 Unit of analysis

Read the robotics market by layers, not logos

A robot at a customer site is more than a body and a policy. It also needs sensors and actuators, a behavior model, data collection, quality checks, a connection to the warehouse system, a safe work zone, remote support, spare parts, and someone accountable for downtime. Naming only the body manufacturer makes half the system disappear.

We will therefore start with five layers. The body determines payload, reach, and physical limits. Intelligence turns observations and a command into action. Data helps repair rare failures. Integration connects the robot to an order queue, a machine tool, or a home. Operations manages the fleet, updates, spare parts, and human interventions.

A company may control all five layers or focus on one. Neither choice is automatically better. A full stack speeds up repairs to a failure that spans the body and the model, but it requires a factory, an operations team, and substantial capital. A narrow layer transfers more easily across robots, but the boundary of responsibility becomes harder to manage: a model provider cannot guarantee the stopping distance of someone else's body on its own.

In the diagram, the same failure travels in reverse. The robot misses a box; the site log links the event to the model and body versions; the data enters training; the update goes through validation and deployment again. The more company boundaries the loop crosses, the more important a compatible log and an explicit owner of the outcome become.

2 Four strategies

Which loop does the company close itself?

A vertically integrated system brings the body, model, data, and operations inside one company. Its developer can change the hand after a grasping failure and immediately record new data for that exact design. The weakness is cost: the company must build the robot, the factory, the compute infrastructure, and the deployment organization at the same time.

A hardware platform focuses on a reliable body, fleet management, and a repeatable industrial process. Its models may be proprietary or supplied by partners. Value comes not from a video of a new skill, but from a robot performing a bounded task day after day inside an existing line.

A model for multiple robots transfers shared intelligence across manipulators and humanoids. Shared representations and data can reach a scale unavailable to one platform. Yet every new body still needs an action adapter, latency and safety checks, and its own trials. Supporting a robot is therefore only the first step toward operation at a customer site.

An open ecosystem shares models, datasets, simulation, and hardware interfaces across many teams. It lowers the cost of a first experiment and makes results reproducible. An open repository, however, does not arrive on site to replace an actuator or take responsibility for a warehouse shift. A product still needs an integrator.

Real companies combine these strategies. NVIDIA develops compute, simulation, and open models, while robot manufacturers embed them in their own products. Apptronik builds Apollo while also testing Google DeepMind models. We therefore do not assign companies permanently to four boxes. We ask which loop a specific offering closes.

4 State of the market

The same word “humanoid” can describe different products

Figure builds the body, Helix, the factory, and its own data loop. In 2025, Figure 02 completed a ten-month pilot at a BMW plant: more than 1,250 hours and 90,000 parts moved. These figures establish duration and a specific operation, but BMW still calls the program a pilot. In 2026, Figure announced a commercial agreement with Catalyst Brands; the partner did not disclose the number of robots.

Agility Robotics began with a bounded tote-handling flow. According to its joint announcement with GXO, Digit has operated under a multi-year Robots-as-a-Service agreement since 2024 and has moved more than 100,000 totes. The record alone is not the important part. The partners announced expansion to two new sites, which tests the repeatability of the same product and service operation.

1X chose the home and a product with remote assistance. For an unfamiliar task, the owner can request an expert session. This is not a hidden detail: assistance is part of the early product and a data channel at the same time. The strategy avoids requiring the robot to know everything on day one, but adds intervention cost and privacy questions.

Google DeepMind and Physical Intelligence approach the market from general intelligence. Their models are tested on multiple bodies; Google works with hardware partners including Apptronik and Boston Dynamics. NVIDIA and Hugging Face build an even broader ecosystem: data formats, simulation, open models, training tools, and onboard compute. This path accelerates developers, but a specific deployment still needs a distinct owner of the outcome.

Unitree represents another market: the robot can be purchased as a platform. The verified price of a G1 in the official store was $13,500. That is an actual sale price, not a target. Yet body availability does not specify which skill, safety measures, or support the product includes. Tesla's $20,000–$30,000 price for Optimus, by contrast, remains the manufacturer's target at future scale rather than a price observed in an external sale.

Three comparisons that change the decision

Figure ↔ Agility

Stack breadth versus a bounded task

Figure connects more layers inside one company. Agility and GXO begin with a narrower process and separately test whether the RaaS deployment repeats. The useful comparison is not which company is “more advanced,” but who owns the result the customer needs.

1X ↔ Unitree

Early-access service versus a platform

Remote assistance is part of 1X's product structure. Unitree's price confirms that a body can be purchased, but the skill and operations remain a separate project. The two public prices therefore do not form a fair ranking.

Gemini ↔ Isaac / LeRobot

Transferable intelligence versus an engineering toolkit

Gemini Robotics transfers a model across bodies with partners. Isaac and LeRobot standardize more of the engineering process. Neither option alone promises a ready-to-run operation at the site.

What the customer actually receives

The same word “robot” can describe four different transactions. The first is a research platform: the customer receives the body and interfaces, then builds the skill. The second is a ready-made machine for one process. The third is a service in which the provider owns availability and support. The fourth is early access, where the product is still changing and the customer effectively participates in development.

The distinction appears in acceptance criteria. A platform is accepted for completeness, working actuators, and SDK availability. A production-ready process is accepted for task completion rate, cycle time, and safe stopping. A service also has fleet availability, repair time, and escalation rules. Early access requires separate consent to limitations, updates, and possible remote-operator involvement.

Degrees of freedom, walking speed, and model size are therefore not a complete product description. A buyer needs to know who marks the work zone, connects the robot to the task queue, replaces a failed actuator, and answers a call at night. If these responsibilities are unnamed, they do not disappear; they pass to the integrator or the customer.

A home adds another layer: the owner's trust. Remote assistance may complete a task and collect a rare example, but the camera transmits data from a private space. The product must explain when a person connects, what that person can see, how long a recording is retained, and whether the owner can opt out. This is as much a part of strategy as the model and the hand.

An industrial site asks a different question: how the robot enters an already running process. Even an autonomous part transfer depends on fencing, 5G or Wi-Fi, the machine, the order queue, and nearby employees. The more changes the environment requires, the less the solution resembles replacing one worker with one robot, and the more important it becomes to treat deployment as a product of its own.

5 Strength of evidence

From a video to repeatable deployment

A demonstration proves that a team completed a selected scenario. It may be autonomous, teleoperated, or mixed; unless that mode is disclosed, the conclusion remains narrow. A pilot moves the robot to a customer site and adds real infrastructure, but often keeps engineers nearby and limits the process.

A commercial deployment adds a paid agreement and operation in the customer's workflow. It may still involve only a few robots and one skill. A repeatable deployment demonstrates the next step: the solution transfers to new sites or customers, while setup, support, and metrics become part of the product.

Production rate describes the factory. A partnership describes an intention to work together. A preorder describes demand and refund terms. None of these events can automatically be promoted to evidence of operation. A strong claim names the site, task, duration, human involvement, and data source.

This discipline does not devalue a demo. A video reveals a new capability; a pilot uncovers infrastructure failures; a commercial agreement tests value; repetition tests the product. The error begins when evidence from one level is used to support a conclusion at the next.

Five questions for any market claim

Who reported it? A manufacturer knows how the system works but has an incentive to show the best result. A customer can better describe the robot's place in the operation. Independent testing helps with reproducibility. A source is not false because of its role, but the boundary of its evidence must be stated.

What did the robot do? “Worked in a factory” is too broad. Did it move one part between fixed points, select varied objects, or tend an entire machine? The more precise the start, end, and success rule, the easier it is to judge whether the conclusion transfers.

In what mode? Autonomy need not be binary. A person may set the goal, approve a plan, help in rare cases, or drive every movement. The system may still create value, but the frequency and duration of assistance belong in both the technical claim and the economics.

How long did the test run? Ten successful trials demonstrate a capability. A shift adds charging and changing task flow. Months add wear, new versions, repairs, and staff turnover. Duration matters because it exposes new classes of failure, not simply because it is longer.

What was repeated? A second robot on the same line tests manufacturing and fleet management. The same task at another site tests integration. A different customer tests the contract and service operation. The word “scale” must therefore say exactly what became repeatable.

7 After the pilot

Scale begins with repeatable deployment

A development team can launch the first site through concentrated effort. Its engineers mark the zone by hand, rewrite the order-system integration, and stay beside the robot. If a second warehouse needs the same three months and the same people, the company is replicating projects rather than a product.

The first lever is standardized integration. Connectors to a WMS or MES, the zone map, safety rules, and the acceptance procedure become a repeatable package. A new site then differs by configuration, not by a new program. The second lever is operational data. The fleet records not every frame, but the failure, context, intervention, and system version; these records increase autonomy in the next release.

The third lever is service. Spare parts, diagnostics, updates, and a person for difficult cases reduce downtime. This is less visually impressive than a new skill, but downtime determines how many hours the robot produces a useful result. Selling the body without service transfers this work to the customer.

All three levers must improve together. High autonomy is useless if every site takes twelve weeks to connect. Fast integration cannot save a fleet immobilized by an actuator failure. Excellent service cannot compensate for a policy that calls a person for every tenth tote. Product maturity therefore appears in site launch time, the share of autonomous operation, downtime, and repeat expansions with a customer.

A failure must travel all the way back

Consider a simple case: the robot fails to pick up a tote. At the site, this is one event, but its causes live in different layers. The camera may have lost an edge, the policy may have selected a poor pose, the hand may have applied too little force, or the task queue may have sent a different tote type. The log must connect the observation, command, actuator state, model version, and process context.

The investigation then needs an owner. Without one, the body provider blames the model, the model team blames the camera, and the integrator blames the customer's data. In a mature product, everyone knows in advance who assembles the event package, who decides whether a configuration change is sufficient, and who may roll back a version across the fleet.

The update follows the path in reverse. The corrected example enters the dataset; the new version passes the release gate from Lesson 8.4; it then receives a limited rollout and only afterward reaches the remaining sites. If an update is sent to the entire fleet at once, scale increases both the benefit and the blast radius of one defect.

A data loop therefore cannot be judged only by recorded hours. More useful metrics are the time from event to reproducible example, the share of failures with complete context, and the speed of safe fix deployment. They reveal whether the fleet learns from operations or merely accumulates video.

Repeatability ends wherever every exception still requires the system's original author. A real product converts developer knowledge into diagnostic tools, operator instructions, spare parts, automated checks, and bounded rights to change configuration. Only then does a new site stop being a separate research project.

9 Reading the market

A market map should show what remains unknown

For every company, keep two rows. The first records what is confirmed now: product, site, task, autonomy mode, and verification date. The second records what was announced: a future price, production capacity, a conditional order, or an expected delivery. Mixing the rows quietly turns a forecast into a fact.

A robot count without a denominator says little about a product. We need to know how many robots work at customer sites, how many remain in labs, how long they are available, and what share of the task they complete without help. For the same reason, operating hours from different tasks cannot be added into one ranking: an hour of tote handling and an hour of home cleaning have different complexity and failure cost.

Openness must also be decomposed by layer: model weights, data, simulator, actuator interfaces, and safety components may have different licenses and access levels. Every open boundary lowers the cost of verification and lets another team reproduce part of the result.

Finally, strategy changes. A company may begin as a model provider and later build its own body; a body manufacturer may open an interface and form an ecosystem. This lesson therefore does not assign permanent labels. It offers questions that remain useful after the next announcement: which loop is closed, who owns a failure, what evidence is public, and what must be repeated at a new site?

How to compare two strategies for your project

Start with a narrow process, not company names. Record the input observation, required result, allowed time, safe stop, and a way to verify success automatically. This one line prevents a specific task from being replaced by a generic promise of a “general-purpose robot.”

Next, mark which layers the team already has. A factory may have a WMS, automation engineers, and round-the-clock service but no manipulation model. A research lab may know how to train a policy but not how to operate a fleet. The same provider becomes a different choice for those two customers.

The third row is the boundary of responsibility. Who certifies that a new model version is compatible with the body? Who stops the fleet after an unknown failure? Who pays for downtime during a repair? “We will solve it together” may be enough for a pilot, but not for a repeatable product: during a failure, it becomes a dispute.

The fourth row is the data path. Decide in advance which records leave the site, who can access them, and how a fix returns to the product. A home adds privacy; a factory adds trade secrets; an open ecosystem adds licensing and the ability to reproduce training.

The fifth row is evidence for the next step. A demo should lead to a pilot with a measurable process; a pilot to an agreement and operating thresholds; a first customer to repetition at a new site. If a team tries to prove the entire market immediately, a cheap experiment teaches it nothing. If it sets no next threshold, the pilot can continue indefinitely.

The comparison produces not a brand ranking but a map of unowned responsibilities. A vertically integrated system reduces interfaces but increases dependence on one provider. A modular ecosystem offers component choice but demands strong integration. The right decision depends on which risks the team can own itself.

11 Lesson takeaways

A strategy is a closed loop of responsibility

  • The market becomes easier to read when the body, model, data, integration, and operations are shown separately.
  • A vertically integrated system, a hardware platform, a shared model, and an open ecosystem solve different constraints.
  • A demonstration, a pilot, a commercial agreement, and repeatable deployment support different conclusions.
  • A pilot becomes a product when new sites launch faster, autonomy increases, and downtime is handled by a repeatable process.

After repeatable deployment, the economics still need to be tested. An inexpensive body may spend much of its time idle, while a costly service operation may reduce interventions and produce more successful tasks per shift.
