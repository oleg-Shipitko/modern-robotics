0.1 Part 0 · Introduction

Two paradigms

Robots have welded car bodies more precisely than humans for decades, yet they learned to fold a T-shirt only recently. Rules written by an engineer stop working in the open world, while a neural network trained on examples needs lots of data and can fail confidently without giving any signal. In this lesson you will compare both approaches on the same task, find where each one breaks, and see why the rest of the course follows the data path.

≈ 40 minutes2 labs and 5 interactives
Paradigm 1

Write the rules

An engineer splits the task into modules and writes clear code for each. For the cup task it might look like this:

# perception: the cup is red, look for red pixels
# r, g, b — pixel color: red, green, blue
if r > 0.60 and g < 0.35 and b < 0.35:
    cup = centroid(pixels)  # center of the blob
# planning: a path to the cup around obstacles
path = astar(hand, cup, obstacles)
# control: hand velocity toward the next waypoint
v = pid(path.next - hand)

Ada*

Ada is a home humanoid on a wheeled base: a torso, a head and two arms like a human's, and a wheeled platform instead of legs. She is shared by all the labs in the course. Her environment is a simplified apartment: a kitchen, a hallway, two bedrooms. In every lesson Ada solves one task that real home robots face too: bring the hand to a cup, drive down the hallway to the kitchen, figure out which room she is in. On the same task we compare different methods, classical and learned.

The model is simplified: the scenes are two-dimensional, and the physics and sensors are reduced to what matters for the lesson's topic. But the principles are the same as in real robots: sensors are noisy, motors have torque and speed limits, and the neural networks in the labs are real and train right in your browser.

1 Paradox

Welding a car body is easier than folding a T-shirt

In 1961 Unimate, the first industrial robot, started work at a General Motors plant. Today manipulators weld car bodies to a fraction of a millimeter, around the clock and almost without defects. Yet robots learned to fold a T-shirt reliably only recently, although any teenager can do it.

Hans Moravec noticed why back in 1988: what comes to people without effort — seeing, grasping, walking — turns out to be the hardest for a machine. Chess and calculation are easy for a computer, but the perception and motor skills of a one-year-old are not. This is called Moravec's paradox.

A factory robot is programmed: its motions are set in advance, and the parts always lie in the same places. The world is arranged around the robot, so every situation can be described by rules beforehand. At home you cannot arrange the world around the robot: fabric falls differently every time, and you cannot describe every case in advance.

That is why, instead of rules, a robot is increasingly given examples, and a neural network is trained on them. A model that takes a camera image and a command in words and directly outputs robot actions is called a VLA (vision-language-action). The term was introduced by the authors of RT-2 (Google DeepMind, 2023; lesson 3.3). One such model is π0 (Physical Intelligence, 2024; lesson 3.6): it takes laundry out of a dryer and folds it. The manipulators it controls differ little from manipulators of ten years ago; what changed is the control system.

So a robot's behavior can be obtained in two ways: an engineer writes rules, or a neural network learns from examples. How do the control systems built these two ways differ?

2 Architectures

How a robot's control system is built

The difference is easiest to see on one task. Take the one that runs through the whole lesson: Ada stands at the kitchen table, a camera looks at the table from above, and the hand has to reach the cup.

The classical stack splits the task into modules, which is why it is also called modular: perception finds the cup, state estimation refines where it is, the planner builds a path, the controller drives the hand. Every module runs on rules and heuristics written by an engineer. In the diagram and the labs of this lesson this approach is called "Rules". The data approach replaces the whole chain with a single neural network: the operator guides the hand to the cup many times, and the network learns to turn camera pixels directly into motor commands. This is end-to-end. A network that picks the robot's next action from what the robot sees is called a policy. A rules-based program can be a policy too, but the word is more often used for a neural network. Between rules and end-to-end there are hybrids.

The VLAs from the previous section are end-to-end too: an image and a command go in, actions come out. In RT-2 and π0 this is a single model. But a large model is hard to run often, while the arms have to be controlled fast. So Helix from Figure and GR00T N1 from NVIDIA (both from 2025, lesson 3.7) split the VLA into two levels: a slow vision-language model understands the scene and the command, and a fast policy drives the arms. This two-level architecture is one way to build a VLA. Switch architectures and click the blocks.

Rules: strengths and weaknesses

  • Guarantees. The planner avoids a detected obstacle by construction; the controller's stability is proven mathematically.
  • Rate and precision. Control loops run at hundreds and thousands of hertz.
  • Transparency. Each module is tested separately; a failure shows up at a specific interface.
  • Open world. The rule "red means cup" lives as long as cups are red and the light is daylight. Every new situation needs a new rule, and there are infinitely many situations. This is called the long tail.

Learning from data: strengths and weaknesses

  • Generalization. The network finds features on its own that an engineer would never think to program.
  • No hand-made interfaces. The whole system is optimized for the final goal; no information is lost between modules. In the lesson's reference paper (Levine et al., 2016) the robot inserted a block into a shape sorter in 96% of attempts, while a modular version with separate pose estimation never did: a centimeter of error at the interface killed a task with millimeter tolerance.
  • Needs lots of data. Hundreds of examples even for a simple task, thousands of hours for a general-purpose model.
  • No guarantees and silent failures. Outside its data the network behaves unpredictably and does not report an error.

A similar shift has already happened with text: language processing moved from rules to large language models. But language models trained on text from the internet, and there is no such reserve of data for robots. How this limit is worked around is covered in Part 4.

On paper, rules give guarantees and transparency but cannot cope with the open world. Data gives generalization but needs lots of examples and can fail without giving any signal. Where does each approach actually break?

⚖️
Both paths fix only what was anticipated in advance. A rule is cheap but closes one case, and there are infinitely many cases. Worse, rules start interfering with each other: white balance fixes the evening but amplifies noise and breaks the kettle detector. Data is expensive: every demonstration is operator time. But it covers many variations at once that nobody listed — if those variations made it into the demonstrations.
Notice how little data the hybrid needs: its network learns only "where the cup is", not the whole behavior. Intermediate labeling saves data. Large models use the same idea differently: they first learn from huge general data, and then a few examples are enough for a new skill (Part 3).
Each path has its price. So which approach should you choose?

How the work is divided. No approach wins at everything. Neural networks get what rules cannot describe: open-world perception, language, soft objects. Rules stay where guarantees, rate and precision are needed: speed limits, safety zones, emergency stop. How do you apply this to specific tasks?

    

      
⚠️
Misconception: "end-to-end is always better, it's only a matter of data". Guarantees do not come from data. A network that goes around the kettle 99 times out of 100 may drive into it on the hundredth, and nobody will know in advance.

      
⚠️
Misconception: "a VLA controls the motors directly". Vision-language-action models output goals a few dozen times per second, while the motors are driven by controllers at around a kilohertz. The boundary is shifting: in 2026 the bottom layer of some robots became neural too. Details in Lesson 0.3 and in Part 6.

Even in the most end-to-end systems, classical controllers usually run at the bottom level. The network outputs target joint positions dozens of times per second, and the motor control loop tracks them at hundreds and thousands of hertz.

    

      
Real systems combine both approaches, but the boundary is shifting toward data. With each generation the learned part grows: first neural networks took over perception, then planning, now motion control too.

      
Data has plenty of weaknesses: even a simple task needs hundreds of examples, and a network can be confidently wrong without giving any signal. Yet the industry is betting on it.

5 What's next

    
Why the course follows the data path from here

    
The industry bets on data because rules do not scale. You cannot write a rule for every situation: a kitchen has infinitely many cups, lights and kinds of mess. This long tail is exactly what the rules version of Ada ran into: every fix closed one case and dragged in the next.

    
Data scales differently: to teach a network something new, you do not need to know in advance how the world works — you need to show enough examples. That is why the course follows the data path from here, and brings out the classics when there is no way around them.

    
The main limit became the data itself, and the industry is collecting it in volumes unheard of in robotics. π0 in 2024 was trained on 10,000+ hours of robot demonstrations. GEN-0 in 2025 — already on 270,000 hours, but these are recordings of people working with their hands, captured by wearable devices.

    
It is still a long way to the texts that language models train on: Ken Goldberg called this gap "the 100,000-year data gap". The estimate is rough, he himself calls it a thought experiment, but it conveys the order of magnitude correctly. How the gap is being closed — with teleoperation, human video, simulation and data from robots already at work — is covered in Part 4.

7 Takeaways

    
Lesson takeaways

    
    
      
  1. Rules are modules with clear interfaces: guarantees, rate, transparency. The weakness is the open world and the long tail of situations.

  2. Learning from data: the network finds features on its own and generalizes. The weakness is that it needs lots of data, and failures can be silent: the network does not know it is wrong.
