3.4 Part 3 · Foundation models for robots

Open X-Embodiment: one format for 22 robots

RT-1 and RT-2 from lessons 3.1 and 3.3 were trained on one kind of robot and one fleet's data: 130,000 demonstrations collected over 17 months on 13 Google robots. Most labs can't do that: they have their own robot, their own camera and a few hundred or a few thousand recordings. In language and vision, a model trained on diverse data outperforms narrow models, and in 2023, 21 institutions tested whether this works for robots. They brought 60 datasets from 22 robots into one format and trained the same RT-1 and RT-2 architectures on the mixture. Trained on this mixture, RT-1 was on average 50% more successful on robots with small datasets than the models the authors of those datasets had trained on their own data, and the large RT-2, after the same mixture, succeeded three times as often at skills that weren't in its robot's data. This lesson covers what exactly was aligned, what got lost along the way and where transfer didn't work.

1 Transfer

One robot, one dataset, one model

In lesson 3.3, RT-2 generalized to new objects and commands, but all of its motion data came from one kind of robot: the same 130,000 demonstrations as RT-1's from lesson 3.1, recorded over 17 months on 13 robots of the same design in Google's office kitchens. A lab with one manipulator and two grad students will never have a dataset like that: it has a few hundred or a few thousand recordings made with its own camera and its own controller.

The Open X-Embodiment authors describe the state of affairs like this: “conventionally, robotic learning methods train a separate model for every application, every robot, and even every environment.” Each dataset is narrow along at least one axis: a single environment, a single set of objects or a narrow range of tasks. Even the largest robot datasets are a fraction of the size of vision benchmarks with 5–18 million images and language corpora with 1.5–4.5 billion examples.

Vision and language learned their lesson long ago: a model trained on a large, diverse corpus outperforms narrow models even on their own tasks, and a new task is solved by fine-tuning a general model rather than by training from scratch. In Part 1, imitation learning started from scratch every time, on the data of one robot, and in lesson 1.2 DAgger collected corrections anew for every new route. We've already seen transfer between the tasks of one robot: RT-1 from lesson 3.1 learned 700 commands at once and benefited from the shared data. But robots differ: one has seven joints, another six; the camera sits above the table or on the wrist; an action is recorded as a gripper velocity, a pose change or joint angles; one robot's gripper uses “1 = closed”, another's “0 = closed”.

Hence the question the paper asks directly: if you collect data from many robots in many labs and train one model, will each robot get positive transfer, that is, a higher success rate than a model trained only on its own data? Previous work introduced special mechanisms for this: a shared action space, learned representations, modules for a specific robot. Here the authors chose to test the simplest thing: bring the data into a coarsely aligned format and train the ready-made RT-1 and RT-2 architectures on it, without any special mechanisms for the differences between robots. What did they have to collect for this?

2 Dataset

60 datasets, 22 robots, 21 institutions

By definition, one robot's data isn't enough for such a question, and collecting new data from twenty robots would take too long. So Open X-Embodiment (Open X-Embodiment Collaboration, 2023) doesn't record new demonstrations but pools existing ones: 60 datasets from 34 labs at 21 institutions, more than a million trajectories from 22 robots, from single arms to bimanual robots and quadrupeds. Hence the name: an embodiment is a robot's body with its joints, cameras and way of being controlled, and the X stands for “cross”, as in across different bodies. A trajectory here is a recording of one execution of a task from start to finish: the frames, the robot's states and the actions at every step; in lesson 3.1 the same thing was called an episode. Together they cover 527 skills and 160,266 tasks. Franka has the most separate datasets, while xArm and Google Robot contribute the most trajectories thanks to a few very large datasets.

The authors extracted the skills and objects from the text annotations with the PaLM language model. Most skills belong to the pick-place family, but the long tail contains skills like “wiping” or “assembling”, and the objects range from appliances to food items and utensils. All the datasets are stored in the RLDS format: the episodes sit in tfrecord files, and the format itself accommodates different action spaces and different sets of observations, from a single camera to several cameras, depth and point clouds, and loads into all major deep learning frameworks.

A single repository doesn't make the data compatible yet. Every robot has its own observations and its own action units, and for one model to learn from all of them at once, the recordings have to be brought to one form. What exactly counts as a common format, and what does it cost?

🧩 Interactive

A common format: one frame and seven numbers

Robots' observation and action spaces differ a lot, and the authors name this as one of the challenges of building X-embodiment models. Their alignment is coarse. From each dataset they take one canonical camera and resize its frames to a common resolution; the other cameras, depth and point clouds don't reach the model. Any robot's action is rewritten as a 7-dimensional vector: the gripper position x, y, z, three rotation angles and the gripper opening, or the rates of these same quantities. Before discretization, each dataset's actions are normalized by its own statistics, and the model's output is later de-normalized: converted back into the units of a specific robot, differently for each one. This way the same model outputs the same string of tokens, and each robot reads it in its own units.

Robot 1 · 10 Hzvelocity · z-rotation velocity · closed gripper
Robot 2 · 3 Hzposition delta · rotation delta · gripper
Robot 3 · 5 Hzposition delta · no rotation · gripper

The paper also lists what isn't aligned. Cameras sit at different poses relative to the robot and have different properties, and that stays in the frames. The coordinate frames in which the gripper is controlled aren't aligned, and the action values are left as the robot's controller recorded them: absolute poses, increments or velocities. The authors acknowledge the consequence: “the same action vector may induce very different motions for different robots.” The model has to guess from the image which robot it is giving a command to right now.

Sort the records from the datasets by what happens to them during conversion to the common format. Drag a card, or tap it and then a column.

The format is coarse on purpose: the authors didn't want to add special mechanisms for the differences between robots and tested whether ready-made architectures would do with the data as is. Tokenization is the same in both models: each of the eight dimensions is split into 256 bins. Seven of them describe the gripper motion, and the eighth is the episode termination flag. This is the same eight-number action as RT-2's in lesson 3.3. RT-1 in lesson 3.1 had 11 numbers: three more for moving the base, and instead of the termination flag, a mode: arm, base or end of episode; the common format has no base. Which models were trained on such a mixture, and what did they show?

3 RT-X

RT-1-X and RT-2-X: the same architectures on a data mixture

For a heterogeneous mixture to pay off, the model needs capacity, and the authors take two familiar transformers without changing how they are built. RT-1-X is RT-1 from lesson 3.1, a 35M-parameter network. It takes a history of 15 frames and encodes them with an ImageNet-pretrained EfficientNet, and the command with a Universal Sentence Encoder (USE) vector. Then FiLM layers interweave image and text into 81 tokens per frame, and a decoder-only transformer outputs discrete actions. In lesson 3.1 the history was 6 frames, and the transformer read 8 tokens per frame: the 81 tokens are the output of EfficientNet with FiLM before the compression that TokenLearner performed in RT-1, and the short description of RT-1-X in the Open X-Embodiment paper doesn't mention this step. The paper doesn't explain why the history was extended to 15 frames. RT-2-X is RT-2 from lesson 3.3 in its 55B-parameter PaLI-X version. It consists of a ViT image encoder and a UL2 language model, pretrained on the WebLI web data, and writes the action as text, for example “1 128 91 241 5 101 127”. RT-1-X was trained on the robotics mixture only, and RT-2-X with co-fine-tuning, with web data and robot data in roughly equal parts.

The mixture used in all the experiments is smaller than the full dataset. It covers 9 manipulators from 12 datasets: RT-1, QT-Opt, Bridge, Task Agnostic Robot Play, Jaco Play, Cable Routing, RoboTurk, NYU VINN, Austin VIOLA, Berkeley Autolab UR5, TOTO and Language Table. The authors explain the difference simply: the dataset kept growing, and at the time of the experiments this was all the data. On a robot, the models run at the rate it needs, from 3 to 10 Hz; RT-1-X runs locally, while RT-2-X is hosted in the cloud, and the robot queries it over the network.

There's one pipeline but many robots, and each has its own amount of data: from hundreds of episodes to hundreds of thousands. The authors ask three questions. Does every robot get positive transfer, that is, higher success than a model trained only on its own data? Does the mixture generalize better to new tasks? And which design choices (model size, history, web pre-training, data composition) matter for this? They answer in two tables and one figure, based on 3,600 trials on 6 robots.

🧩 Interactive

The paper's tables: where transfer works and where it doesn't

Transfer can't be checked with a single number: a robot with three hundred episodes needs other robots' data more than a robot with hundreds of thousands. So the authors split the evaluation by domains: a domain is a robot together with its dataset and evaluation site. The domains are divided into small and large by dataset size, and everywhere the models are compared against two baselines. The first baseline, Original Method, is the dataset authors' model, trained only on that dataset: it was tuned for this data, so it isn't easy to beat. The second is the same RT-1, but trained only on this dataset: comparing against it shows what the mixture itself contributes, as opposed to the architecture.

There are five small domains: Kitchen Manipulation on Jaco, Cable Routing, NYU Door Opening, AUTOLab UR5 and Robot Play. In four of them RT-1-X beat Original Method, and its mean success rate is 50% higher than that of either Original Method or RT-1 trained on its own data (Fig. 4 of the paper; the numbers for individual domains appear only in the figure, so they aren't given here). Under the hood: how the evaluations work and what the tables don't show

All the policies in this section are trained on the same mixture of 9 manipulators. The policies were evaluated in real trials: 3,600 attempts in total on 6 robots in the dataset authors' labs, and the lab logos in Fig. 4 indicate where the robot was run. For Table I, Bridge was evaluated on WidowX in two labs, IRIS at Stanford and RAIL at Berkeley, and the Original Method there is LCBC from the Bridge paper; the six skills from the RT-1 paper were tested on Google Robot, where the Original Method is RT-1 itself.

Table II looks at two different properties. The “RT-2 generalization” column repeats the protocol from lesson 3.3: new objects, backgrounds and environments. The “Skills from Bridge” column is called “Emergent Skills” in the paper, but it means something different from RT-2's emergent capabilities: these are tasks with objects and skills that aren't in the Google Robot data but are in the Bridge dataset, recorded on WidowX; they are evaluated on Google Robot. This checks whether a skill transferred from one robot to another.

What the tables don't have: the models weren't tested on robots that weren't in the mixture; robots with very different sensing and actuation, such as the quadrupeds from the full dataset, weren't included in the experiments; and the paper gives no criterion for telling in advance whether transfer will be positive. The authors call all of this directions for future work.

The picture is uneven. The mixture helps the small domains noticeably, while in the large ones the small RT-1-X loses to RT-1 on RT-1's own data: 35M parameters can't hold nine robots at once, and the authors call this underfitting. The large RT-2-X keeps up with RT-1 in the large domains, and on the skills from another robot's dataset it's three times better than RT-2; without the Bridge data, its success on these skills drops by almost half. Model size, frame history and web pre-training all matter. But co-fine-tuning with web data versus fine-tuning on robot data alone changes almost nothing here: 52% versus 47% in generalization and 44.4% versus 48.7% on the skills from Bridge. In lesson 3.3, co-fine-tuning was a key detail of the RT-2 recipe, and the authors themselves note that their finding runs contrary to it. They attribute this to the data: the mixture of nine robots is much more diverse than the single robot's data RT-2 learned from. There's a second circumstance too: this ablation was done on a 5B-parameter model, and at that size RT-2's gap was small as well, 44% versus 42%. It was noticeable, 63% versus 52%, only at 55B, and Open X-Embodiment didn't test fine-tuning without web data on a model of that size.

But real robots don't let you look inside: you can't remove one dataset, change the mixture weights and see a minute later what happened to each robot. Can we set up such an experiment on a table where everything is computed before your eyes?

🔬 Lab

Mixing four robots' data on one table

In the paper, transfer is measured on six real robots, and what happens inside the mixture can only be judged from the final percentages. Let's build a small X-embodiment setup where everything is visible. We take the table from lesson 2.1: it holds cubes and a bowl, the command is “pick up the <color> cube”, and this time four Ada robots work at it.

Ada is a home humanoid on a wheeled base; the course labs are built around her. More about her in lesson 0.1.

They all share the same scenes, but their recordings differ. Robot A's camera sees the whole table, the action is recorded as a cell of a 24 × 24 grid, the gripper uses “1 = closed”, and the dataset has 300 demonstrations. Robot B's camera is closer and sees only the middle of the table, the action is a cell of a coarse 12 × 12 grid, the gripper uses “0 = closed”, and there are 100 demonstrations. Robot C's camera is on the other side of the table, so its frame is mirrored, the action is recorded in millimeters in its base frame, and there are 40 demonstrations. Robot D's frame is slightly cropped, the action is an offset from the home point in centimeters, and its x axis points toward the camera, that is, opposite to the image; there are 15 demonstrations.

The common format is like the paper's, only smaller: a frame from any camera is reduced to a 12 × 12 grid, and the action is two normalized coordinates in the frame, discretized into 24 bins per axis, plus the gripper with “1 = close”. To convert the actions, we normalize them by the robot's workspace; as in the paper, we don't align the coordinate frames. The policy is the same small network for every robot, and it trains here, in your browser. We test it on 60 new scenes on each robot, and an attempt succeeds if the gripper came down on the right cube. First, each policy learns only from its own data.

The four frames show the same table from above as the four robots' cameras see it: A sees the whole table, B the middle, larger, C a mirror image, D the table with its edges cropped. The cubes are colored squares, the bowl is a colored ring. Under each frame is written how the robot records an action. The bars show the hit rate on 60 new scenes: blue for the robot's own policy, orange for the mixture policy. Before training, the dashed ring shows where the operator picked the cube in the first demonstration. After training, the frame shows a test scene: the black ring marks the right cube, the blue cross shows where the robot's own policy lowered the gripper, the orange dot shows where the mixture policy lowered it, and green marks a hit.

Under the hood: the format, the policy, training and our simplifications

The observation of robot \(r\) is its camera frame \(I_r\), averaged over cells down to a \(12 \times 12\) grid. The operator's action is a point \((x, y)\) on the table; in the common format, it becomes normalized coordinates in the frame and bins:

\[\begin{gathered} (u, v) = \phi_r(x, y) \in [0, 1]^2, \qquad b_u = \lfloor 24u \rfloor,\ b_v = \lfloor 24v \rfloor, \\ \text{the robot executes } \phi_r^{-1}\!\Bigl(\tfrac{b_u + 0.5}{24},\ \tfrac{b_v + 0.5}{24}\Bigr) \text{ in its own units.} \end{gathered}\]

The function \(\phi_r\) normalizes the action by the workspace: for grid cells it's \((c + 0.5)/n\), for millimeters, division by the width of the workspace, and for an offset from the home point, \(0.5 + \Delta x / L\). By default, robot D's axis sign isn't included in \(\phi_r\), so its labels in the common format mirror those of the other robots for the same image. The “align” switch adds the axis sign to \(\phi_D\) and \(\phi_D^{-1}\): that's the alignment of coordinate frames that the paper doesn't do.

The policy takes a \(12 \times 12\) frame and the command word, passes them through a network with one hidden layer of 32 neurons and outputs two softmax heads over 24 bins. The loss is cross-entropy over both heads, as in RT-1 with its 256 bins. The image encoder isn't trained here: it is frozen and builds a map of how similar the color of each cell is to the color named in the command. Its role is the same as that of the pretrained EfficientNet and USE in RT-1 and mini-CLIP in CLIPort from lesson 2.1. Training lasts 500 Adam steps with batches of 16 examples. To build each example in a batch, a dataset is chosen first, with a probability proportional to its weight in the mixture, and then a random demonstration from it. For the policies trained on their own data, only one dataset has a nonzero weight. All randomness is seeded, so the numbers repeat from run to run.

There's no frame history here, no gripper rotation or height, and no multiple steps per episode. The robots' cameras differ only in cropping, scale and mirroring, and the objects and the table are the same, so transfer comes easier here than between a Franka in one lab and a WidowX in another. The lab's numbers have nothing to do with the paper's numbers.

The policy trained on its own data worked well for the robot with 300 demonstrations and almost never for the robot with 15, and the mixture evened this out: it added tens of percentage points to the small datasets and changed almost nothing for the large one. The paper shows the same pattern on real robots: small domains gain a lot, large ones only with a large enough model. But robot D improved only after we aligned its axis: normalization alone doesn't make “right” the same word for all robots, and the paper leaves this to the model, which has to recognize the robot from the image. Here, where the cameras are almost identical, there's nothing to recognize it by. And the mixture weights turned out not to be cosmetic: remove the largest dataset and the others drop; raise the weight of the smallest one and the largest one drops.

Open X-Embodiment showed that a common format and a mixture work, and it released the dataset and the models. What has grown out of it by 2026?

4 Today

What grew out of the common format

The main result of Open X-Embodiment isn't the models but a habit: robot data is now routinely released in a common format, and models are trained on mixtures. The next generation of open vision-language-action models, Octo and OpenVLA, were trained on mixtures of Open X-Embodiment datasets, and the question of mixture weights, which our sliders settled, became a separate section of their papers; lesson 3.5 covers them. The models of lessons 3.6–3.8, from π0 to Gemini Robotics, also learn from the data of many robots at once, and their authors describe separately how they mix data from different robot configurations.

The way data is collected has changed too. Open X-Embodiment pooled other labs' recordings after the fact and so had to put up with different cameras and coordinate frames. DROID, collected in 2024, was recorded with a single protocol on identical Franka setups with identical cameras in many labs: such data barely needs aligning. Hugging Face's LeRobot library set an open dataset format into which both the Open X-Embodiment datasets and recordings from desktop arms costing a few hundred dollars have been converted, so any lab or hobbyist can add their episodes to the shared ones.

A question the paper listed among its directions for future work is still open: there's still no criterion for when transfer is positive, and mixture weights are tuned by hand. And the larger the models get, the more obvious it is that a million trajectories isn't much: vision and language models learn from billions of examples. Where to get data for robots (teleoperation, simulation, videos of people) is the subject of Part 4.

6 Takeaways

Lesson takeaways

  1. Open X-Embodiment recorded no new demonstrations: 60 existing datasets from 22 robots and 21 institutions were brought into one RLDS format, which holds more than a million trajectories and 527 skills.
  2. The common format is deliberately coarse: one camera is taken from each dataset, its frames are brought to a common resolution, and the action is written as a 7-dimensional gripper action vector normalized per dataset. Coordinate frames, rates and the meaning of the values aren't aligned, and one vector can mean different motions for different robots.
  3. The models are the same as before: RT-1-X with 35M parameters and RT-2-X with 55B, trained on a mixture of 9 robots without any special mechanisms for the differences between them. Positive transfer came from the data alone.
  4. The mixture helps small domains: RT-1-X beat the authors' methods on 4 of 5 datasets, by 50% on average. In large domains, the small model underfits and loses to RT-1 on RT-1's own data, while the large RT-2-X doesn't.
  5. Skills transfer between robots: on tasks from Bridge, recorded on WidowX, RT-2-X on Google Robot gets 75.8% versus 27.3% for RT-2, and 42.8% without Bridge in the mixture. It takes model size, frame history and web pre-training.
  6. In the lab, transfer was reproduced on the table: the small datasets gained tens of percentage points, the large one barely changed, and the robot with the unaligned axis improved only after alignment. The mixture weights and the data composition change every robot's result.

Open questions

How can you tell in advance whether a mixture will help a given robot or hurt it?

The paper gives no criterion and calls it future work. The experiments show three conditions: the less data a domain has of its own, the more it gains; data-rich domains need a model with enough capacity; and an inconsistent format, like robot D's axis in the lab, breaks transfer. For now, the only practical method is ablations: remove a dataset from the mixture and measure what changed, as in row 3 of Table II. It would be cheaper to predict transfer from how similar the observations and actions of two datasets are, but no such measure has been validated on robots yet.

The common vector describes one gripper. What about the bimanual robots and quadrupeds that are in the dataset but not in the experiments?

The authors explicitly left robots with very different sensing and actuation out of the experiments. Several options appeared later: separate action heads for each robot configuration, masks for missing dimensions, longer vectors with zeros in the unused components. In lesson 3.5, Octo attaches new action heads when fine-tuning for a new robot, and in lesson 3.7, models such as GR00T N1 and Helix describe a humanoid's actions with dozens of numbers, and GR00T N1 also learns from videos of people. All these solutions have one thing in common: the frame and the language stay shared, and the action is adapted to the robot.
