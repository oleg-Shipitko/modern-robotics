6.1 Part 6 · Humanoid robots

RL-based locomotion: how robots are taught to walk in a simulator

In lesson 5.7, a person stood next to the robot and corrected it in every attempt. That won't work for walking: you can't undo a fall with a takeover, and training takes millions of steps. So walking is learned in a simulator, and the finished policy is transferred to the robot. This is how Hwangbo et al. (2019) trained the quadruped ANYmal in four hours of computation, and on the real robot, with no further tuning, the policy broke the robot's previous speed record by 25%. Rudin et al. (2021) cut training down to minutes: 4,096 copies of the robot walk on a single GPU, and after 1,500 PPO updates with a reward of nine terms, a policy for steps and slopes is ready in under 20 minutes. Radosavovic et al. (2024) applied the same scheme to the Digit humanoid: a transformer over a history of 16 “observation, action” pairs learned only in simulation and then walked the streets for a week without a single fall. In this lesson, Ada gets legs. You'll build a reward for walking and see what loopholes the policy finds in it, check whether a network trained on a single velocity command obeys other commands, and use live training to see why there can be both too few copies and too many.

1 Legs

Ada gets legs

Until this lesson, Ada drove: her torso, head and two arms sat on a wheeled base, and that was enough for the hallway and the kitchen. In many ways, wheels are simpler than legs. The base stands on its own even with the motors off, and a motor only has to turn a wheel. But a home is built for the human body: a threshold between rooms, a step at the front door, stairs to the second floor, a rug bunched up into a fold. Where a wheel gets stuck, a person steps over. That's why, from this lesson on, Ada has legs.

Ada is the course's home humanoid; from this lesson on, she has legs. More about her and her home in lesson 0.1.

The new Ada. Her torso, head and arms are the same as before, and the wheeled base has been replaced by two legs: a thigh, a knee, a shin and a foot on an ankle. Real humanoids have about six motorized joints per leg: Digit, which we'll meet at the end of the lesson, has eight joints in each leg, and six of them are actuated. In this lesson's labs, Ada is flat, and we look at her from the side. She has three joints in each leg, her hip is 0.65 m above the floor when her legs are straight, she weighs 28 kg, and the physics is computed with a 2.5 ms time step.

The price of legs. A wheeled base doesn't fall over, while a two-legged robot has to keep its balance all the time. The center of mass has to stay above the support, and the support is an 18 cm foot, or two feet during a step. If Ada leans back past her heel, the ankle can no longer hold her, and only a step can save her. That's why legs are controlled fast: on Digit, the joint controllers recompute torques 1,000 times a second, and the policy outputs new target joint angles 50 times a second.

How do you make a body like that walk? You can design the gait by hand: split a step into phases, set the foot trajectories and keep the center of mass above the support. That's how it was done for decades. Such controllers work in the lab but cope poorly with anything that wasn't built into them. What's wrong with them, and what can you use instead?

2 Task

Walking as a reinforcement learning problem

Hwangbo et al. (ETH Zurich and Intel, 2019) describe how legged robots were controlled before learning. From the control perspective, a legged robot is a high-dimensional, non-smooth system with many physical constraints, and analytical models of robots are often inaccurate. The most popular approach splits the problem into modules: the robot is approximated as a point mass with massless legs, and each module is tuned by hand. In the authors' words, highly trained engineers spend months developing such a controller, and the work is repeated for every new robot or even every new maneuver. Trajectory optimization on the robot automates part of this work, but, as the same authors write, it still performs worse than modular controllers: accurately tracking fast motion with many unexpected contacts is nearly impossible. Lee et al. (2020) add that conventional systems rely on state machines with empirically tuned thresholds. They have become extremely laborious to develop and maintain, and they remain vulnerable to corner cases.

Reinforcement learning from Part 5 shifts the engineer's work from designing the motion to describing the task. In lesson 5.2, PPO taught Ada to drive to the kitchen, and the lesson's calculator showed how many steps lie behind the 20 minutes of training ANYmal in Rudin et al. Their setup is as follows. The policy is a neural network that receives observations 50 times a second: the tilt and velocities of the body, the angles and velocities of the joints, its own previous action and the velocity command, that is, how fast and in which direction the robot should walk. Its output isn't motor torques but target joint angles. A PD controller from lesson 0.3 turns them into torques: \(\tau = K_p (q^* - q) - K_d \dot q\), where \(q^*\) is the target angle and \(q\) is the current one. Hwangbo et al. explain why the output is designed this way: a policy that outputs target angles can stand from the very start of training, while a policy that outputs torques can't. The reward for a step is a weighted sum of many terms: some reward what we want, others penalize what we don't. Finally, thousands of copies of the robot walk in the simulator at once, each with its own floor friction, pushes and other random parameters. This setup is called RL-based locomotion: the motion isn't designed, it's learned. The diagram shows how it all works together.

Transfer to the robot in this scheme works as in lessons 5.4 and 5.5: dynamics randomization and pushes, an actuator model learned from data, and in Lee et al. also a teacher with privileged information and a student that relies on the history of proprioception. Hwangbo et al. showed that a learned actuator model significantly reduces the gap between simulation and reality, and in lesson 5.4 we already saw why: with an ideal or analytical actuator model, their policy couldn't take a single step without falling, while the network trained on four minutes of recordings from the real robot is off in torque by 0.74 N·m on average, versus 3.55 N·m for the ideal model.

That leaves the hardest part of this setup: the reward. You'd think walking is simply moving forward: reward the speed, and the policy will figure out the rest. It will indeed figure something out, but not what we expect. Let's check it on Ada.

Under the hood: Ada's model, the terms and the training

Body. The model consists of seven links in a plane. The torso is 0.46 m long and weighs 18 kg; each leg has a thigh and a shin of 0.30 m each (2.6 and 1.8 kg) and a 0.18 m foot (0.6 kg). The ankle is shifted toward the heel, as in humans: the heel is 6 cm behind it and the toe 12 cm in front. The six joints have PD actuators: \(K_p = 200\), \(K_d = 4\) in the hip and knee, \(K_p = 400\), \(K_d = 4\) in the ankle, with a torque limit of 60 N·m. The physics is position-based (XPBD): a 2.5 ms time step, hinge joints and floor contact with friction \(\mu = 0.9\), eight physics steps per policy decision (50 Hz). A fall is when the torso or the head touches the floor; in the sets where a fall ends the episode, the episode also ends when the torso tilts by more than 1.5 rad.

Policy. The network receives 26 numbers as input: the hip height, the tilt and velocities of the torso, the angles and velocities of the six joints, its own previous action, the command and the foot contacts. The 26 → 64 → 64 → 6 network with tanh outputs an action \(a\), and the target joint angles are \(q^* = q_0 + 0.5\,a\), where \(q_0\) is the default pose with slightly bent knees. During training, the spread of the actions is set by a Gaussian with a trainable \(\log\sigma\), initially \(\sigma = 0.37\); on the scene, the policy outputs the mean, without noise.

Reward per step. The reward is \(r = \sum_i w_i\, f_i\). The terms and their weights: forward, \(v_x\) (weight 1); velocity tracking, \(\exp(-(v_x - v^*)^2/0.25)\) (1); fall, 1 when the torso touches the floor (−1); torso height, \((h - 0.6)^2\) (−2); torso pitch, \(\theta^2\) (−1); bouncing, \(v_y^2\) (−1); energy, \(\sum_j \tau_j^2\) (−0.00002); smoothness, \(\lVert a_t - a_{t-1}\rVert^2\) (−0.02); acceleration, \(\sum_j \ddot q_j^2\) (−0.0000001); air time, \(\sum_f (t_{\text{air},f} - 0.5)\) at the moment of touchdown (1 if the command is above 0.1 m/s). The weights are taken from the Rudin et al. table and adapted to Ada's scale. ANYmal's table has no terms for the height and pitch of the body, but the two-legged Ada can't do without them.

Training. This is PPO from lesson 5.2 with a six-number action: \(\gamma = 0.99\), \(\lambda = 0.95\), clipping 0.2, 5 epochs of 4 minibatches. The Adam step size adapts toward a KL of 0.01 by multiplying or dividing by 1.5, as in Algorithm 1 of Rudin et al. An episode lasts up to 20 s, and on a timeout the critic's target value is bootstrapped with its own prediction. Starts come with a random tilt and joint angles, and each copy gets its own command from the range 0.2–1.0 m/s, held for the whole episode. Seed 1. Node, Chrome and Safari compute exactly the same numbers, bit for bit, because the engine has its own exponential, logarithm, sine, cosine and tanh, as in lessons 5.2–5.7.

What the scene computes. A run lasts 6 seconds (300 steps) with start seed 31. The speed is the mean of \(v_x\) over the steps, “torso on floor” is the share of steps with the hip below 40 cm, and Σ τ² is the sum of the squared torques of the six joints, averaged over the steps. On 20 other starts, with seeds 44, 55, …, 253, the Ada with the “forward only” reward lies down and crawls faster than 1 m/s on 17 starts out of 20, the Ada with the “forward and fall” reward speeds up and falls within the first 2 seconds on 20, the Ada without energy and smoothness spends at least 2 times more torque on 19 (counting the starts where neither Ada falls), and the Ada with the full reward doesn't fall on 19 starts out of 20. Free mode has a fifth set, “everything but air time”: with it, at 0.6 m/s, Ada makes 5.4 steps a second, 11 cm each, and keeps a foot in the air for 0.09 s, while with the full reward it's 3.4 steps of 18 cm and 0.2 s. These are the longer steps for which Rudin et al. introduced the air-time term.

With only the speed reward, Ada falls at 0.6 s and crawls on her torso for the rest of the run at 1.37 m/s, more than twice as fast as the full-reward Ada, who walks at 0.60 m/s. The fall penalty changes the loophole: Ada speeds up to 1.86 m/s, crouching and pitching her torso by 0.71 rad, and falls at 1.0 s. Without the energy and smoothness penalties, Ada follows the command at 0.59 m/s, but her sum of squared torques is 5,785 versus 1,993 for the full reward, 2.9 times more. Only the full reward produced walking without loopholes: the hip at 62 cm, a torso pitch of 0.04 rad, not a single fall.

This behavior is called reward hacking: the policy collects reward without solving the task. The reward says what to achieve, not how, and the policy achieves exactly what's written. Which terms are needed for what's written to match walking, and why is the command in the input random?

3 Terms

A reward of many terms, and random commands

In the lab, each new term closed one loophole. Rudin et al. write that their total reward is a weighted sum of nine terms. Two of them reward tracking the linear and angular velocity commands. Six are penalties that make the motion smoother and more natural: for the vertical velocity of the body, for its rocking, for joint motion and torques, for changes of the joint targets and for collisions, that is, contacts of the knees and shins and the feet hitting each other. The ninth term, the feet air time, rewards longer steps. The command terms share one form: \(\varphi(x) = \exp(-\lVert x\rVert^2/0.25)\). Such a reward never exceeds one and falls off smoothly with the error. Hwangbo et al. explain why a penalty that grows with the error without bound won't do: with a large error, it pays more to fall and end the episode than to try to catch up with the command.

The gait isn't mentioned anywhere in this list. The authors stress that neither the reward function nor the action space has any gait-dependent elements, and yet the policy always converges to a trot. If artifacts appear in the behavior, such as a dragging leg or an unreasonably high or low body, they are removed by tuning the weights. The two-legged Cassie needed one more term, a reward for standing on a single foot. Margolis and Agrawal (2022) turned this dependence of behavior on the rewards into a technique. Their reward has 18 terms, and six of them depend on eight behavior parameters in the policy's input. These are the stepping frequency, the body height and pitch, the stance width, the foot swing height and three leg phase offsets that set the type of gait. From these eight numbers, a single policy can trot, pace and pronk, jumping off all four legs at once. The authors note that gaits learned with a velocity reward alone “have not been shown to successfully transfer to the real world,” and they call auxiliary rewards “biases for generalization.”

The command terms only work if the commands are random. In Rudin et al., the linear velocity and heading command stays constant for the whole episode, but each copy gets its own. Hwangbo et al. draw commands at random from intervals: forward from −1.0 to 1.0 m/s, sideways up to ±0.4 m/s, turning up to ±1.2 rad/s. Radosavovic et al. pick a new random command for the humanoid every 10 seconds. If the command is always the same in training, it is constant in the policy's input, and the policy has no way to link it to speed: what it will do with a different number is never checked during training. We covered the other sources of randomness in lesson 5.4: in Rudin et al., the terrain is chosen by a curriculum that works like ADR, and friction, pushes and observation noise are varied by dynamics randomization. In Lee et al., the curriculum is adaptive: a particle filter picks terrains that are neither too easy nor too difficult.

One more detail concerns the end of an episode. In Rudin et al., an episode lasts at most 20 seconds, but time isn't part of the observation, so the policy doesn't know how much is left. That's why, on a timeout, the critic's target value is bootstrapped with its own prediction, as if the episode continued; we did the same in lesson 5.2. The authors estimate that this gives 10–20% more reward. Now let's see what random commands give in practice.

Both of these policies trained for 500 updates on 512 copies, which took about half an hour each in Node. Rudin et al. have 4,096 copies, and Margolis and Agrawal also have 4,096, with 2.58 billion steps. Why thousands of copies, and not a hundred or a million?

4 Copies

Thousands of copies: why thousands

In lesson 5.2, the calculator already broke Rudin et al.'s 20 minutes down into numbers: 4,096 robots making 24 steps each over 1,500 updates give 147 million steps, more than a month of one robot's experience. There we also saw why you can't just keep adding robots. The rollout batch of an update equals the number of robots times the number of steps each of them makes, so with the same batch, more robots means fewer consecutive steps for each, and GAE needs the rewards of several consecutive steps. What's new here is what the authors' experiment showed. They varied the number of robots from 128, the level of CPU implementations, up to 16,384, close to the limit of a single GPU, for three batch sizes. When there are too many robots, performance drops sharply: each robot gets fewer than 25 consecutive steps, that is, less than 0.5 s. With few robots, performance decreases slowly, because neighboring steps of one robot are similar to each other. Meanwhile, training time falls almost linearly up to 4,000 robots, and the authors name 2,048 to 4,096 robots with a batch of about 100,000 steps as the best compromise. A reference setup with 20,000 robots making 50 steps each learns better, but at the cost of a relatively long training time.

Copies are cheap because the simulation and the training never leave GPU memory: by the authors' estimate, a transfer over the PCIe bus can be up to 50 times slower than the computation itself. The other papers in this lesson show the same scale. Hwangbo et al. generated a quarter of a billion transitions on one computer in four hours, Lee et al. trained the teacher for about 12 hours and the student for about 4, and Miki et al. ran 1,000 environments in parallel with 250 steps each. At the other extreme is training on the robot itself. Smith, Kostrikov and Levine (2022) taught the quadruped A1 to walk in fewer than 20,000 steps, about 20 minutes of wall-clock time, right on a lawn and a trail. They trained off-policy, like SAC from lesson 5.3, with 20 critic updates per robot step. But their reward has two terms, an episode ends only if the tilt exceeds 30°, and the authors themselves write that massively parallel data collection is not often feasible in the real world. You can get a feel for when copies are too few and when they're too many on a smaller training problem.

🔬 Lab 3

How many copies: one budget, different numbers of copies

Here, training really runs in your browser, but the policy is simpler than a network. It's a stepping rhythm of ten numbers: the stepping frequency, the hip swing, the knee bend, the hip's response to the torso pitch, foot placement by speed and others. They are tuned by augmented random search, ARS (Mania, Guy, Recht, 2018). At each update, each of the N copies of Ada gets its own random change of the parameters and tries it in both directions, three episodes per direction, and the rhythm shifts toward where the reward is higher. No gradient is computed here, and each copy gives one estimate of the direction: the more copies, the more accurate the step. The reward comes from Lab 1, “forward and fall,” and an episode lasts 4 seconds. The training budget is always the same, 960 episodes, and the number of copies decides how many updates it pays for: 80 with two copies, 20 with eight and 5 with thirty-two.

Under the hood: the stepping rhythm, ARS and the budget

Rhythm. The phase grows as \(\varphi = 2\pi f t\), the legs move in antiphase, and the half of the cycle where \(\sin\varphi > 0\) is the swing. The hip target is set relative to the vertical, as in SIMBICON (Yin et al., 2007): \(h_0 - A_h\cos\varphi\), plus \(-K_\theta\,\theta\) in stance, which levels the torso pitch, and \(K_r v_x + K_v (v_x - v^*)\) in swing, which places the foot according to speed (Raibert's heuristic). The hip joint angle equals this angle minus the torso pitch. The knee bends as \(k_0 + A_k \max(0, \sin(\varphi + \delta))\), and the ankle keeps the foot horizontal, with an offset. Ten parameters in all: \(f, A_h, h_0, A_k, k_0, K_\theta, K_v, \delta, \delta_a, K_r\). The starting values were found in advance by randomly sampling 600 sets: it's a rhythm that barely stays on its feet.

Search. This is ARS (Mania, Guy, Recht, 2018). An update draws \(N\) random directions \(\delta_k\), and for each of them, two versions of the rhythm, \(z \pm 0.4\,\delta_k\), run three episodes each with different starts and commands. Of the \(N\) directions, the best \(N/2\) are kept, and the parameters shift like this: \(z \leftarrow z + \frac{0.25}{b\,\sigma_R}\sum_k (r_k^+ - r_k^-)\,\delta_k\), where \(b\) is the number of directions kept and \(\sigma_R\) is the spread of their rewards. An update costs \(6N\) episodes, so a budget of 960 pays for 80 updates with two copies, 20 with eight and 5 with thirty-two. With the “forward and fall” reward, an episode's reward is almost exactly 50 times the distance covered in meters. After training, the rhythm is checked on 8 episodes of 4 s each with other starts and commands (seed 9001), and the curve on the chart ends with this check.

Other seeds. The lab trains the rhythm on seed 2. On seeds 1–10 with a budget of 960 episodes, 8 copies beat both 2 and 32 copies on 9 seeds out of 10: they beat two copies on 10 and 32 copies on 9. 2 copies with a budget of 3,840 lose to 8 copies with a budget of 960 on 10 seeds out of 10, and with an equal five updates, 32 copies beat 8 copies with a budget of 240 on 9 seeds. The rewards depend strongly on the seed: the medians over the ten seeds are 25, 81 and 47 for 2, 8 and 32 copies, and for 8 copies they range from 45 to 167. The search is noisy, and the reward doesn't grow monotonically with the budget: on seed 2, eight copies get 89 with a budget of 960, 63 with 1,200 and 123 with 1,440.

With the same budget of 960 episodes, 8 copies scored 89 on the check, two copies 15 and thirty-two 37. Two copies didn't catch up with eight even with a four times larger budget: in 320 updates, they scored 6. And with an equal five updates, 32 copies gave 37 versus 15 for eight copies with a budget of 240. So copies make each update more accurate, but with a shared budget, too many copies leave too few updates. In Rudin et al., the rollout batch plays the role of the budget, and the consecutive steps of each robot play the role of the number of updates, and the best choice also turned out to be in the middle.

A ten-number rhythm and 960 episodes look like a toy next to a network and 147 million steps, but the rule is the same. Copies are needed so that the estimate behind each update isn't just noise, and there have to be enough updates to reach a good policy. For quadrupeds, this recipe produced walking in the mountains and on snow. What changes when there are two legs and the robot is as tall as a person?

5 Humanoid

Digit: a transformer over history and transfer without fine-tuning

A quadruped can stand on its own; a humanoid can't. Radosavovic, Xiao, Zhang, Darrell, Malik and Sreenath (2024) carried the same scheme over to Digit, a humanoid 1.6 m tall, weighing 45 kg, with 30 degrees of freedom. Over a week of full-day testing, their policy walked across plazas, walkways and lawns, dry on a sunny afternoon and wet in the early morning, and the authors didn't observe a single fall, even though there was no safety gantry. It was trained with PPO in Isaac Gym on four A100 GPUs and thousands of randomized environments, and it transferred to the robot zero-shot, without fine-tuning. The manufacturer's high-fidelity simulator served only to filter out unsafe policies. The authors based the reward on biomechanics studies of human walking, without a library of reference gaits, and included energy minimization terms. An arm swing in antiphase with the legs emerged on its own, even though the reward says nothing about the arms.

This work differs from Rudin et al. in two ways. The first is the teacher and the student from lesson 5.5. Reinforcement learning first trains a policy on the full state of the simulator. Then a student, which sees only the robot's observations, learns in two ways at once: with reinforcement learning and by imitating the teacher. The loss includes the KL divergence from lesson 5.2 between the student's and the teacher's actions, and the weight of this divergence drops to zero by the middle of training. The authors explain this scheme by the fact that training a policy with reinforcement learning directly on observations is slow. The second difference is memory. The student policy is a causal transformer over history. Its input is the last 16 “observation, action” pairs, one token per pair, with sinusoidal position encoding, and the causal mask from lesson 1.6 lets each token look only at the previous ones. The model has four blocks of width 192 and 1.4 million parameters. The history lets the policy see how the desired and actual states diverge: from how the body responded to previous actions, it can tell what floor is underfoot and what load is in the hands. The authors call this in-context adaptation, by analogy with language models: the network's weights don't change, the history does. In ablations, the transformer beat MLP, TCN and LSTM by a considerable margin, and a long context beat a short one.

In their next work, the same authors removed the reward from policy training. “Humanoid Locomotion as Next Token Prediction” (2024) casts walking as predicting the next token in a sequence of sensorimotor trajectories, which the authors view as the sentences of the physical world. A transformer with the same width and depth as the RL policy (192 and four blocks), but with 2M parameters instead of 1.4M, learns to predict both observations and actions. Trajectories without actions go into training too: recordings of the manufacturer's controller, recordings of human movements from the KIT dataset made with motion capture, and YouTube videos. In place of the actions, they have a learnable mask token, and no loss is computed at those positions. The model, trained on only 27 hours of walking data, transferred to Digit without fine-tuning and walked around San Francisco for a week. It tracked the command more accurately than the very RL policy on whose trajectories it was trained, and it walked backward at up to 0.5 m/s, even though there was no backward walking in the data with actions. Human motion recordings as a source of what you can't write down as reward terms will be the topic of lessons 6.2 and 6.3.

The authors name the limitations themselves: the policy is not perfectly symmetrical and not perfect at tracking the velocity command, and a very strong pull of a cable attached to the robot knocks it over. Isaac Gym can't model the closed kinematic chains in Digit's legs accurately, so they were replaced with a high-stiffness virtual spring. The robot walks blind, like ANYmal in Lee et al. from lesson 5.5: it can bump into a step and get trapped, although it then gets out by lifting its leg higher.

6 Today

What became standard, and where the limits are

The Rudin et al. recipe lives on in their open code, legged_gym, and in its descendants for humanoids: thousands of copies on a GPU, PPO, a reward of many terms, randomization and a terrain curriculum. In lesson 5.2, Figure 02's walking training was described along the same lines. Margolis and Agrawal released their Walk These Ways controller as open source, and their idea of behavior parameters in the policy's input leads to lesson 6.3, where a single policy receives command masks. Miki et al. added perception to this scheme, and in lesson 5.5 we saw ANYmal climb Mount Etzel without a single fall. Training on the robot itself hasn't disappeared either: Smith et al.'s 20 minutes show that a quadruped with a simple reward doesn't strictly need a simulator.

The limits are the same as in lesson 5.4. A simulator reproduces mud, snow and crumbling ground poorly: Lee et al. write plainly that they trained the policy only on rigid terrain, and in the field it worked thanks to the history of proprioception. People still tune the reward: Hwangbo et al. spent about two days on it for walking and about a week for getting up after a fall. And for a humanoid, something else matters most: walking learned from a reward comes out correct but not human-like, and nobody knows how to write down as reward terms what “human-like” means.

Eight common misconceptions

8 Takeaways

Lesson takeaways

  1. Walking means keeping balance all the time and managing contacts with the floor, and hand-designed controllers, according to Hwangbo et al., took months for every new maneuver. Reinforcement learning shifts the engineer's work to describing the task: the policy outputs target joint angles 50 times a second, PD controllers turn them into torques, and thousands of copies in the simulator collect experience.
  2. The reward says what to achieve, not how, and the policy achieves exactly what's written. With only a speed reward, Ada lay down and crawled at 1.37 m/s; with a fall penalty, she sped up to 1.86 m/s and fell; and without the energy and smoothness penalties, she followed the command, but with 2.9 times more torque. Only the full reward of nine terms produced walking without loopholes.
  3. Rudin et al. use nine terms: command tracking with a bounded kernel exp(−error²/0.25), penalties for bouncing, rocking, joint motion and torques, joint target changes and collisions, and a reward for the feet's air time. The gait isn't mentioned in any of them; it emerges on its own.
  4. A policy learns to read the velocity command only if the commands are random in training. In Lab 2, the random-command Ada held both 0.3 and 0.9 m/s, while the single-command Ada walked at 0.71 m/s at a command of 0.9 m/s: for her, the constant number in the input worked as a neuron bias, and she never learned to speed up when it changed.
  5. There can be too few copies and too many. With a rollout batch of 98,304 steps, 2,048–4,096 robots worked best for Rudin et al., and if each robot gets fewer than 25 consecutive steps, GAE stops working. In Lab 3, on the same budget, 8 copies scored 89 on the check, and 2 and 32 copies scored 15 and 37.
  6. The Digit humanoid learns with the same scheme, plus a teacher and a student and a transformer over a history of 16 “observation, action” pairs. Its policy adapts to the floor and to pushes in context, without changing its weights; it walked the streets for a week without falls and transferred to the robot without fine-tuning. The same authors' next work casts walking as next-token prediction on 27 hours of data, including recordings of people.

Open questions

Can you avoid tuning the reward by hand?

Hwangbo et al. spent about two days on the reward for walking and about a week for getting up after a fall: in their words, some safety concerns, such as high impacts and collisions with fragile components, are not very intuitive to embed in a cost function. Rudin et al. remove gait artifacts by tuning the weights, and Miki et al. tuned the weights by looking at the policy's behavior in simulation. Margolis and Agrawal suggest not choosing a single behavior but learning a family of them and picking the right one live, and Radosavovic et al. replace the reward with data, that is, with motion recordings. Which of these scales to dozens of whole-body tasks, lessons 6.2 and 6.3 will show.

What do you do about what the simulator can't do?

Lee et al. write that present-day simulators are severely limited in their ability to simulate compliant contact, slippage and crumbling terrain, and yet a policy trained only on rigid terrain worked on snow and in mud thanks to the history of proprioception. Miki et al. found the opposite case: the policy exploited the non-vertical edges of the steps in the simulator's height map and transferred poorly to the robot until the stairs in the simulator were replaced with boxes. Where the line runs between “the variety of the world isn't needed” and “the simulator fooled us” has to be found anew for every new task.
