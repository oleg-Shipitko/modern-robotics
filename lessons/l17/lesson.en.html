1.7 Part 1 · Imitation learning

ACT: action chunking

Action chunks appeared in lesson 1.3, and in lesson 1.5 Diffusion Policy built them. It captures complex distributions well: if the data contains several ways to do the same thing, it picks one of them. But it pays for this with time. The chunk is refined from noise over 10–16 denoising steps, and each step is a full pass of the network: about 0.1 s per chunk, which is five control steps at 50 Hz. That is why Diffusion Policy executes 8 actions out of 16 without new frames and only then builds the next chunk. ACT came out almost at the same time as Diffusion Policy, in spring 2023, and solves this differently. It was developed together with the low-cost bimanual robot ALOHA, but it is used on other robots too. A transformer outputs all one hundred actions of the chunk in a single pass, in about 0.01 s. To keep the operators' different styles from being averaged, during training the policy gets a hint: a few numbers about which style this demonstration was performed in. That is why ACT manages to recompute the chunk at every 50 Hz control step. The lesson answers three questions: why a chunk helps even when the data contains a single way; how the decoder outputs the whole chunk at once and why it needs a hint about the operator's style; how the seams between chunks are smoothed and what to do if the model thinks longer than a control step. At the end we compare ACT and Diffusion Policy side by side. Lesson 1.8 comes at it from the other side: how to cut the number of steps of a generative policy without giving it up.

1 ALOHA

Cheap arms and 50 demonstrations

To teach a robot from demonstrations you need arms on which a person can comfortably show bimanual work, and enough data to train on. Precise bimanual systems are expensive, and fine work is hard to do on cheap ones.

In 2023 Tony Zhao, Vikash Kumar, Sergey Levine and Chelsea Finn built a teleoperation rig from inexpensive arms. The operator moves two small leader arms, WidowX, and two large follower arms, ViperX, mirror their angles joint for joint, so no inverse kinematics is needed. A non-expert assembles the rig in under 2 hours. The figure in the paper lists the price as “<$20k”: the base kit costs $18k, with options such as cameras $20k; a ViperX follower arm is about $5,600, a WidowX leader about $3,300. Four Logitech C922x cameras look from the top, the front and the two wrists, shooting 480×640 at 30 frames per second, while teleoperation and recording run at 50 Hz. The rate matters: at 5 Hz operators spend 62% more time on a task.

The hardware is imprecise: ViperX is off by 5–8 mm, and its payload is 750 g. If you simply replay the recorded angles, that error is enough to miss a small part.

The authors make up the missing precision with learning: the ACT policy (Action Chunking with Transformers) watches the four cameras and corrects the motion frame by frame. It was trained separately for each task on 50 demonstrations, 10–20 minutes of data, and each task was tested 25 times.

2 Compounding error

Fifty decisions a second

What's wrong with an ordinary policy? It outputs one action for every frame. At 50 Hz that's 50 decisions a second and 500 in ten seconds, and every decision is slightly off.

The error pushes the arm to where there are fewer demonstrations, and there the next decision errs more. Errors add up: in lesson 1.1 their growth was estimated as \(\varepsilon T^2\), that is, with the square of the episode length.

DAgger from lesson 1.2 added examples wherever the policy drifts. That needs an expert who labels every new state, and ALOHA only has 50 recorded demonstrations.

The other way out is to decide less often: from one frame predict a chunk of \(k\) actions, as in lessons 1.3 and 1.5, and execute it whole. There will be \(k\) times fewer decisions, but between decisions the arm drives blind.

The ACT authors took this idea from psychology, where a chunk is a sequence of actions carried out as one unit. In their work a chunk corresponds to a meaningful piece of work: grasping a corner of a candy wrapper or inserting a battery into its slot. This is the maneuver from lesson 1.3.

Let's check on 200 episodes of a simple model when a chunk pays off. Ada's gripper carries a mug across the table, and we watch how far the mug has strayed from the planned path. Every step this deviation is multiplied by \(\lambda\) — the stability of the system without control. At \(\lambda < 1\) the arm returns to the target by itself, like a position controller. At \(\lambda > 1\) the deviation grows on its own, like a robot that is balancing.

📊 200 runs

Horizontal axis: the mug's deviation from the path at the end of the episode, cm. Bar height: how many of the 200 runs ended with that deviation. Arrows at the edges: runs that went beyond the plot.

Under the hood: the model and what theory says
\[\begin{aligned} &\text{within a chunk:} && x \leftarrow \lambda\,x,\\ &\text{at a decision:} && x \leftarrow (\lambda - c + \delta)\,x + \varepsilon\,\xi. \end{aligned}\]

Here \(x\) is the mug's deviation from the path in centimeters and \(\xi\) is standard noise. An episode is 200 steps of 20 ms, that is 4 s; there are 200 runs, each with its own fixed seed, so the picture repeats exactly. \(\varepsilon = 0.3\) cm is the error at each decision. \(\delta = 0.01\) is the error that grows with the deviation: far from the data the policy errs more. \(c = \max(0, \lambda - 1)\) is the correction the policy learned from the expert: on an unstable system the expert keeps damping the growth of the deviation, and that is in the data. The last decision of any policy falls on the last step, so the comparison is fair. The spread \(\sigma\) is the standard deviation of the final \(x\).

Theory. Simchowitz, Pfrommer and Jadbabaie (COLT 2025) showed that a smooth deterministic Markov policy errs exponentially more in the horizon at execution time than on the data, even with stable dynamics. Non-Markov, non-smooth and stochastic policies get around this, and the authors explicitly name action chunks and diffusion. Zhang et al. (ICLR 2026) proved that with a long enough chunk the compounding of error is bounded if the system is stable without feedback. The mechanism is stability in the control-theory sense. For unstable systems you need noise during demonstration collection so that the data contains corrections.

The argument isn't over. In a preprint by Lazzati et al. (August 2026) shortening the horizon doesn't explain the success: what works is non-Markov expressiveness and “implicit ensembling”. Zeng et al. from Tedrake's group (August 2026) consider the non-Markov nature of demonstrations the main factor, and with long context closed-loop policies work best for them.

A chunk narrows the spread if the system damps deviations by itself, and hurts if it amplifies them. But the per-step policy has a second problem that this model lacks: it gets stuck where the operator paused to think. Will a chunk handle that, and how many actions should go into it?

🔬 Lab

Smoothness versus reactivity

Will a chunk handle pauses? The operator moves the arm unevenly: halfway along the path they stop to think, and the per-step policy gets stuck right there.

At the pause point every demonstration contributes many “stay” frames and one “go” frame. The policy sees only the current frame and doesn't know how long it has been standing, so again and again it chooses “stay”.

More demonstrations won't help: the operator pauses in each one, and the number of “stay” frames grows just as much.

A chunk carries the continuation of the motion: it contains both the pause and what the operator did after it. Ada's gripper carries a mug across the table to a shelf, top view. The policy is trained on ten demonstrations and in a similar position takes the median of what the operator did — that's how a network trained with L1 behaves. Let's compare chunks of 1, 10 and 100 actions on one scene, push the mug mid-chunk and pick the length.

Ada is the home humanoid robot the course's labs are built around. More about her in lesson 0.1.

Under the hood: how the lab is built
  • Demonstrations. 10 runs over 0.8 m at about 0.25 m/s, recorded at 50 Hz. The operator stops at the 34 cm mark. Short pauses are 12 to 19 steps, 0.31 s on average; long ones 22 to 35 steps, 0.58 s on average.
  • Policy. Given the mug's position, we take nearby demonstration frames with Gaussian weights (\(\sigma = 8\) mm). For each step \(j\) of the chunk — the weighted median of the positions those frames reached j steps later. These are absolute target positions, as in ACT. A shift of about 3 mm in a random direction is added to the chunk; away from the data it grows: 3 cm away it doubles. The gripper chases the target with a lag, covering 80% of the distance per step.
  • Why \(k = 1\) gets stuck. At the pause point the data has ten runs of 12–19 “stay” frames and one “go” frame each. The median next position is “stay”, and the gripper stays no matter how much time has passed. A chunk gets through the pause if more than half of the frames are already moving by its end: then the median moves too.
  • The push shifts the mug in the gripper 6 cm sideways. The gripper follows the old plan, and the mug travels with the offset until a new chunk accounts for it. In missions 3 and 4 the push comes after the pause, right after a new chunk starts, that is, at the worst moment. Reaction time runs from the push to the first chunk that sees it. With temporal ensembling we count when the mug is at least halfway back on the path.
  • Outcomes. The mug is on the shelf if it was placed no farther than 3 cm from the mark. The trip is allowed 8 s. A jerk is a step where the gripper's acceleration exceeds 4 m/s².

A chunk gets through the pause, but while it's executing the arm doesn't see the push. The working window in the toy is 14 to 25 actions. Large systems also execute only part of the chunk: in lesson 1.5 Diffusion Policy executed 8 actions out of 16 and then built a new chunk. ACT's chunk is 100 actions. How does one network output a hundred actions at once?

3 Architecture

A hundred actions in one pass

How does one network output a hundred actions at once? It has to turn four frames and the joint positions into 100 actions of 14 numbers, and do it fast: ACT is queried at every step, 50 times a second. Why so often, we'll see in section 5.

The network from lesson 1.1 is built for one action: a frame in, an action out.

In lesson 1.6 we saw three ways to output a chunk. You can emit it piece by piece, the way a language model emits words: one token per network pass. If a token is a whole action, a chunk takes 100 passes in a row; if it's each of the 14 numbers, 1400. There's no time for that at every step. Diffusion Policy from lesson 1.5 built the whole chunk at once but refined it from noise in 10–16 denoising steps, and each step is a network pass. ACT takes the third way, like regression with chunks in the lab of lesson 1.5: the whole chunk in one pass.

ACT takes the transformer from lesson 1.6. The frame from each camera goes through a ResNet18 convolutional network and becomes a 15×20×512 feature map. The map is unrolled into 300 tokens, and 2D sinusoidal positions are added so the network knows which part of the frame each token comes from. Four cameras give 1200 tokens, to which a joint token and a “style” token \(z\) are added — 1202 in all. Here \(z\) is 32 numbers about the operator's manner: during training they are computed by the CVAE encoder, and when the robot runs, \(z = 0\) is fed in. Why that's needed is in the next section.

Then the two parts of the transformer do their work. The encoder is four attention layers in which each of the 1202 tokens takes information from all the others. This is not the CVAE encoder: that one is a separate network, covered in the next section. The decoder outputs all \(k\) actions at once. It works like the attention lab in lesson 1.6. The decoder has \(k\) queries, 100 in ACT. They are learned blanks, one for each slot in the chunk, and each asks “what will the \(i\)-th action of the chunk be?”. A query compares itself with the keys of the camera, joint and \(z\) tokens and assembles its action from their values, and all queries are computed simultaneously, in one pass. Attention where queries come from one token sequence and keys and values from another is called cross-attention. Each action is 14 absolute target positions of the leader arms, 6 joints and a gripper for each; with deltas the result was worse.

Under the hood: ACT by the numbers

The encoder has 4 layers, d = 512, 8 heads. The paper says 7 decoder layers, but in the original code only the first one feeds the output (issue #25 in the ACT repository; LeRobot uses 1). ResNet18 starts from ImageNet weights. About 80M parameters in total, training takes about 5 hours on an RTX 2080 Ti, AdamW, learning rate 1e-5, batch 8, a separate model per task. Inference takes about 10 ms.

The target positions are tracked by the PID controller in the Dynamixel motors. The leader arms' positions are predicted, not the followers': the gap between them, through the PID, implicitly sets the force. Why deltas are worse the paper doesn't explain. A possible explanation comes from lesson 0.3: position targets together with a controller give a system that is stable without feedback, and that is the condition under which chunks help.

1202 tokens in, 100×14 out, about 80M parameters and about 10 ms of inference. The assembled architecture has a block that does nothing at inference — the CVAE encoder. What is it for during training?

4 CVAE

The latent “style” variable

People show the same task differently. One operator goes around the vase on the left, another on the right, and where precision matters less, the motions are even more scattered. The ACT authors say it plainly: for the same observation, a person may move the arm along different trajectories.

For the policy this is a conflict: for the same frame, the demonstrations demand different chunks. Regression with mean squared error averages them, and the mean of two detours leads straight into the vase; that was the conclusion of lesson 1.3. ACT is trained with L1 instead of MSE. L1 doesn't average, it takes the median, the side with more demonstrations. The policy doesn't drive into the vase, but the conflict in the data remains: the network fits one answer to different styles.

The ACT authors propose giving the policy a hint during training about which style this demonstration was performed in. The hint is prepared by a second network, the encoder, a separate transformer, not the one inside the policy. It sees the operator's trajectory, that is, the \(k\) actions the operator recorded, and the joint positions; it gets no images, to make training faster. The encoder compresses all this into \(z\), 32 numbers about how the operator performed this particular stretch. The policy gets the images from the four cameras, the joints and \(z\) and outputs a chunk, which is compared with the operator's trajectory. Since the style is already recorded in \(z\), the policy doesn't have to fit one chunk to different styles and can focus on the stretches where precision matters.

Such a pair of networks is called a conditional variational autoencoder, a CVAE. The encoder compresses the trajectory into \(z\), and the decoder, the policy itself from the previous section, reconstructs the chunk from \(z\). “Conditional” means that the decoder sees not only \(z\) but also the observation: the images and the joints.

But on the robot there is no operator's trajectory: producing it is the policy's job. So \(z\) is trained into a simple form already during training. The encoder outputs not the 32 numbers themselves but the mean and spread of a normal distribution, and \(z\) is drawn from it at random. The loss is \(L_1 + \beta\,\mathrm{KL}\) with \(\beta = 10\). The first term demands that the chunk match the operator's trajectory. The second is a penalty for how far the distribution of \(z\) is from the standard normal: the more information about the style \(z\) carries, the higher the penalty. The larger \(\beta\), the less information gets through \(z\).

On the robot the encoder is discarded, and the policy gets \(z = 0\), the center of the normal distribution that \(z\) was pulled toward during training. It is the same “average” hint in every run.

In the authors' experiments the CVAE turned out to matter on human data. In simulation, on two tasks with demonstrations from a script that always acts the same way in the same situation, success barely changed without the CVAE. On human demonstrations it dropped from 35.3% to 2%. What \(z = 0\) gives at inference is explained in two ways. Pick one explanation and test it by experiment: the operator went around the vase 26 times on the left and 24 on the right; in the diagrams the robot drives bottom to top.

Under the hood: the toy CVAE

The detour side is the only thing that can be encoded. The encoder maps “left” to \(z \sim \mathcal N(+\mu, \sigma^2)\) and “right” to \(z \sim \mathcal N(-\mu, \sigma^2)\). The decoder with \(L_1\) outputs the median, that is, the side that is more likely given \(z\). \(\mu\) and \(\sigma\) are found by brute force to minimize the sum of two terms: the share of wrong sides (an error costs 1, in the same units as \(L_1\)) and \(\beta\,\mathrm{KL}\bigl(\mathcal N(\pm\mu, \sigma^2)\,\|\,\mathcal N(0, 1)\bigr)\).

\[\begin{aligned} \beta = 10:&\quad \mu = 0,\ \text{side error } 48\,\%,\\ \beta = 1:&\quad \mu = 0.39,\ \sigma = 0.92,\ \text{error } 34\,\%. \end{aligned}\]

At \(\beta = 10\) the encoder encodes nothing: \(z\) knows nothing about the side. At \(\beta = 1\) it tells the sides apart. At \(\beta = 10\) the encoder is better off encoding nothing: every bit about the side costs more than it saves in L1. \(L_1\) regression without \(z\) outputs the median of all demonstrations — the side with more of them. If there are equally many demonstrations on the left and on the right, the median is undefined: L1 is equally happy with any trajectory between the sides, including one straight into the vase. Random z are ten numbers from a generator with seed 21.

So what does the CVAE give in the end? On the robot \(z = 0\), and ACT outputs the same chunk every time: of two styles it takes the one with more demonstrations, like L1 regression. It can't choose between styles the way diffusion does. The hint \(z\) only works during training: according to the authors, without it success on human demonstrations drops from 35.3% to 2%. An independent rerun, the 2026 preprint “The Latent That Never Was”, didn't get such a drop, so even this benefit is in question. ACT's strength isn't \(z\) but the chunk of one hundred actions in a single pass: fast and smooth. So ACT suits data with one style or one clearly dominant style. If there are many different styles and they need to be kept, you need a generative policy: after collecting more than 26,000 demonstrations, the ALOHA authors themselves switched to diffusion, and in ALOHA Unleashed, on the ShirtMessy task, it gave 70% against 25% for an ACT variant with L1.

The problem from the lab remains: a chunk of 100 actions sees the world once every 2 seconds. How does ACT react faster without shortening the chunk?

5 Temporal ensembling

Blending overlapping chunks

How to react faster without shortening the chunk? A chunk of 100 actions executes for 2 seconds, and all that time the arm sees no new frames.

A new frame is taken into account only in the next chunk.

In the lab we executed only part of the chunk and composed a new one more often. The reaction got faster, but each new chunk starts slightly differently from how the old one was going, and jerks appear at the seams.

ACT queries the model at every step, and for the current step it averages all the predictions that have accumulated for it — at k = 100, up to a hundred of them. This is temporal ensembling (TE), averaging over time. You already met this trick in lesson 1.3 under the name “smoothing”: there the mean of two chunks with different detour sides led Ada into the kettle. Here we'll look at how it works in ACT and which predictions it trusts more. The weights are \(w_i = e^{-m i}\), where \(i = 0\) is the oldest prediction: at \(m > 0\) each next, fresher prediction weighs slightly less. In the ACT code \(m = 0.01\). Let's look inside and scrub through the process step by step. At the top is a tape: steps run horizontally, the gripper's lateral offset vertically; thin lines are the chunks that predicted the selected step, the thick one is what the gripper executed. Below is the weight of each prediction, from the oldest on the left to the freshest on the right.

🧠 The model's thinking, step by step

Time runs left to right: 20 ms steps horizontally, the gripper's lateral offset in centimeters vertically. The hatched rectangle is the obstacle; the gripper goes around it. The bars below are the weight of each prediction in the action at the selected step.

Under the hood: the formula and the tape
\[ a(t) = \frac{\sum_i w_i\,\hat a_i(t)}{\sum_i w_i}, \qquad w_i = e^{-m i}, \]

where \(i = 0\) is the oldest chunk. In the ACT code the predictions for the current step are stored in the order the chunks appeared, and the weights \(e^{-0.01\,i}\) run from the oldest. At \(k = 100\) the weights sum to ≈ 63.5, so the oldest prediction contributes ≈ 1.6% of the action and the freshest ≈ 0.6%. The smaller \(m\), the faster new observations are taken into account.

In the tape a new chunk starts at every step from where the gripper is now and converges to the chosen detour in 12 steps. In two-strategy mode, chunks before step 100 choose the detour above, and from step 100 on, below. The obstacle occupies steps 132–168 and ±4 cm in height.

Blending smooths the motion but trusts old predictions more, and the average of two different strategies can lead into the obstacle. In practice, temporal ensembling added 3.3 percentage points to ACT's success, in π0 (the large policy from lesson 1.6) it made things worse, and in ALOHA Unleashed, the next policy for the same platform, it wasn't needed: a chunk of 50 actions is executed whole there, and the authors call replanning once a second a limitation. Querying the model at every step is possible because ACT is fast: inference takes about 10 ms. What if the model thinks tens of times longer?

6 Latency and seams

While the model thinks, the robot moves

What if the model thinks for a long time? π0 from lesson 1.6 produces a chunk in 73 ms on an RTX 4090; for Gemini Robotics (Google DeepMind, 2025; lesson 3.7) the path from observation to chunk is about 250 ms. All that time the robot has to do something.

The new chunk arrives late and was computed from a frame that is already stale: the robot has moved on in the meantime.

Wait for the new chunk standing still — the robot freezes. Switch to it as soon as it arrives — a jump at the seam. Blend chunks, as temporal ensembling does — different strategies get averaged.

These approaches have come up before: synchronous and asynchronous inference and Real-Time Chunking in lesson 1.5, temporal ensembling in the previous section. A quick reminder of how each works:

  • Synchronous inference. The robot finishes the chunk, stops and waits for the model to compute the next one.
  • Asynchronous inference. The next chunk is computed while the robot executes the current one, and the robot switches to it as soon as it's ready. There are no pauses, but there can be a jump at the seam.
  • Real-Time Chunking (RTC; Black, Galliker, Levine, NeurIPS 2025). The next chunk is also computed in advance, but the actions that will be executed during inference are frozen, and the rest are inpainted so that the new chunk continues the old one. No retraining is needed.
  • Temporal ensembling. A new chunk is computed at every control step, and all the predictions for the current moment are averaged.

Let's compare all four approaches on one scene and see what each does to the robot's velocity. The robot goes around an obstacle above or below. The policy is generative, like π0: a chunk of 50 actions, and it picks the detour side at random until the robot has moved off the axis. Under the hood: the modes and other solutions
  • Sync. Execute 25 actions, stop, wait for inference, start the new chunk from where we stand.
  • Naive async. Inference of the new chunk starts one latency before the end of the 25 actions, from the frame at that moment. When the chunk arrives, the robot switches to it immediately at the right step, without smoothing the seam.
  • TE. A new chunk arrives at every step, computed from the frame \(d\) steps ago. The action is the average of all chunks with weights \(e^{-0.01\,i}\), as in ACT.
  • RTC (simplified). The first \(d\) actions of the new chunk are the same as the old one's. After that the new chunk is blended with the old one with the old one's weight \(e^{-j/6}\); the last 25 actions are new only. The new chunk continues the same detour side: in real RTC that is done by guidance during generation.
  • Criteria. A pause is any step where the robot moves slower than 5 cm/s; the trip must be made with less than 0.1 s of pauses in total. The velocity jump is the largest change in velocity between adjacent 20 ms steps, threshold 0.15 m/s. The obstacle is a circle of radius 7 cm, the robot 2 cm.

There are other solutions. BID (ICLR 2025; in lesson 1.3 this approach was called consistency) samples several chunks at every step and picks one by consistency with the previous decision; the relative gain is 32%, with EMA 46%, at roughly twice the compute. Asynchronous inference in SmolVLA (a small open policy from Hugging Face, 2025; lesson 3.5) cut the time per task from 13.75 to 9.7 s.

In the toy only RTC survives 200 ms of latency. In the RTC paper, on π0.5 (the next version of π0, 2025; lesson 3.8), with artificial latency of +100 and +200 ms RTC doesn't degrade, the synchronous mode degrades linearly, and temporal ensembling rocks the robot until a protective stop. RTC performs the same motion 20% faster than the synchronous mode and lights a match at latency above 300 ms. In the trained version of RTC (December 2025) the latency is simulated right during training, so there's no extra compute at inference; on π0.6 (lesson 3.8) the result is on par with the original RTC. 7 Comparison

ACT and Diffusion Policy side by side

Both policies output action chunks and came out almost at the same time, in spring 2023, but they solve different problems. Diffusion Policy from lesson 1.5 answers how to capture the distribution of actions accurately when the data contains several ways to do the same thing. ACT answers how to output a chunk quickly and cheaply, so that it can be recomputed at every control step.

Diffusion Policy (lesson 1.5)ACT

So the choice depends on the task. If the data contains several equally valid ways and the policy must confidently pick one, diffusion or flow matching from lesson 1.8 fits. If frequent reaction at 50 Hz matters and the operators' styles are similar, ACT fits. The large policies of Part 3 combine both ideas: π0 (lesson 3.6) builds the chunk over 10 flow matching steps, but computes the observation once and keeps it in the KV cache (lesson 1.6), so the steps are cheap.

10 Takeaways

Lesson takeaways

  1. Action chunking: from one frame predict \(k\) actions. There are \(k\) times fewer decisions, and the chunk gets through the pauses where a per-step policy gets stuck.
  2. Chunk length has a window: short chunks get stuck, long ones react late to the unexpected. Large systems execute part of the chunk: π0 — 25 of 50.
  3. ACT: ResNet18 → 1200 tokens, plus joints and z, encoder-decoder, 100 queries → 100×14 absolute positions in one pass.
  4. The CVAE works only in training: as the authors intend it, \(z\) absorbs the spread of operators' manners, so the decoder doesn't have to average them. At inference \(z = 0\), and ACT is deterministic.
  5. Temporal ensembling trusts old predictions more (\(w_0\) is the oldest). It smooths, but averages different strategies and copes poorly with latency.
  6. RTC stitches chunks under inference latency: it freezes what will have been executed and inpaints the rest.
  7. A chunk helps on a system that is stable without feedback, and can hurt on an unstable one.

ACT is trained separately for each task: 50 demonstrations per skill, one policy — one task. The policy repeats the operator's motions, but knows neither the geometry of the scene nor words, so it can't figure out what exactly to do in a new situation. Part 2 starts from that problem.

Open questions

Inference latency is 150 ms, the robot runs at 50 Hz. What chunk length and execution scheme would you choose, and why?

150 ms is 7–8 steps. The chunk should cover the latency with margin, and the executed part should fit within the time in which you need to be able to react. For example, a chunk of 50 actions (1 s), executing about 25 (0.5 s), as in π0. The synchronous mode would give a 150 ms pause for every half-second of motion, naive switching — jumps at the seams. Better asynchronously with RTC: freeze the 8 actions that will execute during inference and inpaint the rest. Temporal ensembling is risky if the policy is generative and neighboring chunks may pick different strategies.

Why does an action chunk help on a stable system but can hurt on an unstable one, for instance when balancing?

A chunk is executed without feedback. If the system is stable on its own — say, a position controller holds the target — deviations between decisions die out, and there are fewer decisions and fewer errors. If the system is unstable, over the chunk the deviation grows by a factor of \(\lambda^k\), and there's no one to correct it until the next decision. According to ICLR 2026, such systems need noise during demonstration collection so the policy learns corrections; the chunks then have to be short.
