Benchmarks: how models are measured and compared

Two papers each report 80% success, but one tests a short grasp in simulation and the other a long task on a real robot. The numbers are not comparable without the experimental conditions. We will unpack a benchmark, map modern Physical AI suites on shared axes, and put one candidate through three different exams. This reveals which capability each result measures—and where that result stops applying.

1 Anatomy of a comparison

A benchmark ties a number to experimental conditions

Two papers each report 80% success. In one, the robot receives one instruction and one minute on a familiar table; in the other, it completes five consecutive instructions in a new kitchen. The numbers look alike but answer different questions. In Lesson 8.4, a test matrix set the rules for one team. A benchmark makes those rules shared so independent teams can reproduce the evaluation.

A minimal description has six parts. Tasks define the required behavior. The environment and robot embodiment define physics, cameras, observations, and actuator commands. Data specify what training is allowed. The split separates training from evaluation. The metric turns trajectories into numbers. The evaluation protocol fixes the horizon, number of runs, scene reset, random seeds, and software-environment version.

Every part bounds the claim. Success on a simulated manipulator applies to that morphology. New episodes in a familiar kitchen test repeatability in the same scene. Average success on individual tasks describes separate skills, not a long chain. A benchmark is useful precisely because it narrows the question to a reproducible experiment.

A strong result is therefore written as a sentence, not a bare number: model, suite version, split, allowed data, metric, and number of runs. If one part is unknown, the comparison remains a hypothesis. How exactly can one missing protocol line change the winner?

What changes?

The task, object, scene, wording, robot embodiment, or chain length.

What stays fixed?

The observation and action interface, success rule, horizon, and environment version.

What can we claim?

Only performance on the stated split under the chosen metric.

2 Landscape map

Benchmarks test different system capabilities

There is no universal exam for a physical agent. Some suites isolate a short skill in controlled simulation. Others require a chain to continue from the state created by the previous action. Still others vary camera, lighting, and wording, or move the comparison onto a real robot. The map below therefore does not rank suites: it shows where and which capability each one makes observable.

The cost of physical evaluation rises from left to right: simulation offers repeatability, a sim↔real bridge tests the rig's fidelity, and a real-world arena shows actual behavior. The question changes from top to bottom: an isolated skill, a long household chain, or a robustness stress test. Select any node to see the robot embodiment, scale, and scope of the conclusion.

New suites increasingly test more than average success. LIBERO-Plus introduces 10,030 test variants across seven axes; RoboTwin 2.0 combines 50 bimanual tasks with five embodiments; VLABench adds implicit intent and world knowledge; RobotArena∞ constructs controlled simulated changes from real videos. These are different responses to one problem: a familiar scene can hide a fragile model.

3 Measurement rule

One model can pass one exam and fail another

The scene ended after 74 seconds: the robot placed the cup in the sink, but the paper's limit was 60 seconds. Under the strict protocol, this is a failure; under a 90-second limit, it is a success. If another report awards three quarters of a point for completed subtasks, the same trajectory yields a third number. No rule is automatically correct; it must follow from the question.

For a short atomic skill, the final binary outcome is usually what matters. A long household task benefits from both full success and stage-level progress; otherwise a policy that opened the cabinet and retrieved the object is indistinguishable from one that never moved. CALVIN measures the length of a sequence of successfully completed language instructions—a metric intentionally stricter than average single-skill success.

The horizon changes both the number and the strategy. A fast policy may drop objects more often, while a slow policy may complete an episode carefully after a short deadline has expired. When RoboCasa365 changed its horizon, models had to be reevaluated: old and new rows cannot simply be merged. The benchmark version is part of the result.

Intermediate progress helps diagnosis, but its weights introduce a new value model. If opening a door is worth half a point, a system may prefer two easy starts over one complete task. Publish the primary metric and diagnostic metrics separately. In the lab below, the candidate stays fixed while the exam requirements change. That makes the capability added by each benchmark visible.

Candidate P reliably sees the object, brings in the gripper, and performs a short familiar action. Run it in three scenes: a short skill, a five-instruction chain, and a stress test with a shifted camera. Do not switch tables; watch the physical stage at which the robot stops.

Under the hood: which model actually runs the experiment

The candidate has six binary capabilities: perception, reaching, grasping, placement, memory of chain state, and robustness to observation shift. Each exam stage requires one capability. After the first failure, subsequent actions receive no credit because the robot cannot continue from a state it never reached.

This model is deliberately simple but causal: a CALVIN-like chain adds a memory requirement, while a LIBERO-Plus-like test adds robustness. The result does not pretend to be the success rate of a real VLA; it shows why “success” is incomplete without the exam type.

A short exam confirms a specific skill, but it does not rule out failure in a long chain or under a camera shift. To state the distinction precisely, name exactly what was familiar and what was new.

4 Generalization question

The split defines what must be new

If training and test sets contain different recordings of the same task, the evaluation measures repeatability over familiar coverage. Transfer needs a different cut: remove every episode with novel objects from training; hold out entire kitchens and camera configurations for novel scenes; and separate combinations of goals and objects for novel tasks.

A random episode split often looks stronger because the same objects, backgrounds, and phrasings appear on both sides. That is not necessarily data leakage—the protocol may intentionally measure variation across repeated runs. The error begins when a result on that split is described as generalization to new objects or homes.

LIBERO separates transfer types into task suites, while CALVIN includes an ABC→D setup: training uses three environments and evaluation an unseen fourth. RoboCasa365 reports familiar and unseen composite tasks separately. “Unseen” is not enough without the axis: a new background, a new object, and a new composition of skills pose different challenges.

The split also needs organizational protection. If a hidden test is repeatedly used for checkpoint selection, its results begin to steer development. In Lesson 8.4, this was the holdout set; public benchmarks use a closed evaluation server, submission limits, and refreshed test scenes. How do we choose a split for a specific claim?

Under the hood: two different behavior models

The first policy outputs a memorized image coordinate: \(x_m=0.5+c\), where \(c\) is the camera shift. The second uses relative geometry and outputs the target's current coordinate: \(x_r=x_{goal}\). A grasp succeeds when the error is below \(0.085\).

In the original scene, both models produce the same answer, so an ordinary test cannot distinguish the causal mechanism. A controlled target shift creates a counterfactual example: if behavior does not follow the target, the high original score was compatible with a shortcut.

A stress test is useful not because it must lower the score, but because it distinguishes two hypotheses about behavior. We can now record the result in a card that another engineer can understand and reproduce.

5 Result card

How to report a reproducible result

A model name and final score are only the first line of a report. To let another engineer repeat the comparison, begin with the testable claim—for example, “the policy transfers familiar skills to unseen objects in LIBERO-Object.” That sentence immediately rules out broader conclusions about new kitchens, long tasks, and real robots.

Next, fix the model identity. One name may refer to base weights, a fine-tuned version, and several sizes. Record the exact checkpoint, hash or download date, observation-normalization method, action frequency, and decoding parameters. For a remote model, record the service version and evaluation time; otherwise a silent update between series mixes different policies.

Allowed data are part of the comparison. A method trained only on suite demonstrations answers a different question from a large pretrained VLA that uses internet images and Open X-Embodiment. Both results can be useful: the first compares learning algorithms under a controlled budget, while the second evaluates a complete system. A leaderboard should either separate these regimes or mark additional pretraining explicitly.

The environment configuration includes simulator, physics, and asset versions; robot embodiment; cameras; resolution; observations; and the action interface. Even switching from absolute poses to deltas, or changing control frequency, changes the problem for the policy. Save seeds or initial states for randomized scenes; for a real rig, save the placement diagram, calibration, and reset procedure.

Then describe the evaluation: split, task list, episodes per task, horizon, success criterion, rule for technical failures, and aggregation method. If the reported result uses the best of several checkpoints, state which set selected it. Otherwise the test silently enters development and ceases to be independent.

Store the aggregate with individual episode outcomes. A minimal log contains the task, initial state, seed, final outcome, completed stages, duration, intervention, and video link. It lets us open a slice, recompute another aggregation, and separate policy errors from rig failures. A mean score alone cannot do this.

Lesson 8.4 added statistical uncertainty to the average. Public results inherit that rule: run counts and variation do not disappear when a score enters a leaderboard. We do not repeat the interval calculation here, but a card is incomplete without the number of runs, multiple training seeds when they affect the conclusion, and an explicit description of repeated evaluation.

Such a card turns “whose model is better?” into a testable set of differences. We can first align the evaluation protocol, then compare architectures or data, and identify the remaining mismatches as limitations. But which suite belongs on the card when different families ask different questions?

6 Benchmark families

Different suites measure different robot capabilities

RLBench defines 100 manually designed simulated manipulation tasks and provides visual observations, proprioception, and planner demonstrations. Its scale is useful for multi-task and few-shot learning, but a published result must name the task subset and demonstration count; many studies do not use all 100.

LIBERO frames its question around knowledge transfer in continual learning. Its suites separate spatial relations, objects, goals, and long-horizon tasks; the paper contains 130 tasks in total. Averaging them without naming the suite hides what kind of knowledge transferred. CALVIN instead centers a long language-conditioned chain: a policy must solve individual skills and continue from the state created by its preceding action.

RoboCasa365 expands kitchen environments to 365 tasks, including 300 composite tasks, across more than 2,500 kitchens. BEHAVIOR-1K begins with a different question: which complete household activities do people actually want robots to perform? It describes 1,000 activities in 50 interactive scenes containing over 10,000 objects. These large numbers describe coverage; they are not policy-quality scores by themselves.

HumanoidBench ties the task to the body: 27 simulated tasks in locomotion, manipulation, and whole-body control. Its published configuration uses a Unitree H1 with two Shadow Hands. The score therefore describes high-dimensional control of a specific morphology, not every robot called a humanoid.

SIMPLER tests a different claim: can simulation reproduce the relative ordering of real policies and their sensitivity to shifts? Lesson 7.5 introduced correlation and MMRV for this purpose. The broader principle is that we must validate both the policy inside a simulation and the evaluation rig itself against a real robot.

A new wave of stress tests makes weak spots an explicit axis. LIBERO-Plus retains LIBERO tasks but tests 10,030 variants across seven dimensions: object pose, camera, robot initial pose, language, lighting, textures, and sensor noise. RoboTwin 2.0 brings the idea to 50 bimanual tasks and five robot embodiments, randomizing clutter, illumination, background, table height, and wording. These suites do not replace a basic skill check; they ask whether the same skill survives a controlled shift.

VLABench evaluates 100 task categories and more than 2,000 objects while adding implicit intent, world knowledge, and long-horizon reasoning. RobotArena∞ begins with real videos, reconstructs simulated counterparts, and supports controlled changes. RoboArena moves further toward distributed real-world A/B trials. Together they form not a “worse-to-better” ladder, but a chain of evidence from controlled experiments to the physical world.

Choose a family by the failure you need to distinguish, not by leaderboard popularity. If models grasp familiar objects equally well but differ in transfer of spatial relations, use the corresponding LIBERO slice. If the difference appears only after several actions, a suite of isolated grasps will miss it; use CALVIN, composite RoboCasa365 tasks, or a full BEHAVIOR-1K household activity.

A broad catalog and a demanding protocol solve different problems. One hundred atomic skills cover many actions, but do not necessarily test recovery from a policy's own mistake. One long chain probes closed-loop continuation more deeply, but covers less diversity. Mature evaluation therefore often uses a portfolio: a broad suite of short tasks, separate long scenarios, and real-world checks of a few critical cases.

Physical realism is not the only scale either. A simplified simulation can be valuable when thousands of commands reproducibly run one protocol and test an algorithmic hypothesis. A detailed kitchen is better for contact and perception but costs more and may add simulator errors. Choose the rig according to the factor that must remain controlled.

A large 2026 audit compared LIBERO, CALVIN, SIMPLER, RoboCasa, and RoboTwin 2.0 and found four recurring risks: a shortcut may solve the task instead of the claimed capability; model differences may be statistically indistinguishable; repeated development against one test turns it into training; and results depend on data provenance. The practical response is not to abandon these suites, but to add diagnostic interventions, uncertainty intervals, hidden tests, and external real-world evaluation.

This is why the lesson does not turn task, scene, and object counts into a bar-chart ranking. The counts use different units and describe suite structure, not the quality of one policy. “1,000 is larger than 365, so the benchmark is better” is as invalid as comparing cameras by their number of recorded videos without considering viewpoint or purpose.

7 Aggregation

The final score depends on how outcomes are counted

After execution, we have a table of outcomes: tasks, scenes, objects, and repeated trials. To produce one row, we must aggregate them. The most direct method sums every success and divides by the number of episodes. A task repeated 100 times then affects the total ten times more than one repeated 10 times. This is reasonable when repeats represent real traffic, but wrong when every task is meant to have equal weight.

Macro-averaging first computes each task's result, then averages across tasks. It prevents a rare task from disappearing inside a large category, but gives ten episodes of that task the same weight as 100 episodes of another. Reports should therefore show run counts and slices beside the macro-average. Choose micro- or macro-averaging from the question, not from whichever number is larger.

For a long chain, binary full-sequence success is very strict, while average step success is too forgiving. A policy may perform every skill well in isolation yet consistently stop on the second step. CALVIN metrics such as average completed-chain length preserve action order and measure continuation from the state the policy created. Partial progress is useful alongside them, but should not be called full success.

With several splits, it is tempting to average everything into one “overall” score. The weights for novel objects, scenes, and tasks then become the table author's decision. A model can offset weakness in novel scenes with a large advantage in familiar ones. If transfer to a new scene is mandatory, keep it as a separate constraint rather than trading it for points in another category.

Pairwise evaluation aggregates differently. Each pair asks which policy is preferred under one condition; a rating model then connects the comparison graph. If two policies never meet directly and share few opponents, their ordering is uncertain. Report graph coverage, pair count, and the rating interval alongside the rank.

Rank is especially sensitive to the participant set. Adding a strong model or a new task family can reorder the middle of the table even though no existing policy changed. Absolute task outcomes reveal why; a rank alone hides the cause. In Lab 3, we deliberately change the participating sites, not the policies.

Aggregation is necessary for the big picture, but it should not erase structure. A practical report shows the primary metric, prespecified slices, and episode outcomes. The leaderboard then becomes a convenient index into comparable result cards, not evidence on its own.

8 Leaderboard

A ranking requires a shared protocol

A leaderboard helps navigation: it shows which methods were evaluated under one protocol, whether weights and code are available, and how many runs were performed. But a table position does not transfer automatically to another suite. A model ranked first on LIBERO may trail on long CALVIN chains or another embodiment. That is not a contradiction; the measurements cover different domains.

Even under one name, versions must be compatible. Changing the horizon, physics, camera, or reset rule changes the task. An honest table either reevaluates old models or separates the new version. “RoboCasa365 v1.0.1, Composite-Unseen, 50 tasks” carries more meaning than “RoboCasa: 43%.”

An aggregate score also encodes weights. Averaging 50 atomic and 5 composite tasks gives each atomic task one row; averaging categories first gives each category equal weight. A good report therefore publishes the aggregate with slices instead of using the average as a substitute for analysis.

Full standardization is difficult in the real world. Robots live in different labs, and household diversity is valuable precisely because it does not reproduce a single scene. RoboArena handles this pairwise: two policies run on the same task in the same environment, the evaluator is blind to their names, and a network of sites adds diverse conditions. 9 Limits

A benchmark measures only its stated properties

High episode success may say nothing about control latency. A policy that spends two seconds on every frame can perform well in a simulation with paused time yet fail to catch a falling object. Latency, control frequency, and jitter need separate metrics—the topic of Lesson 8.6.

Short independent episodes hide continuous operation. They do not measure actuator heating, camera drift, accumulated scene-reset errors, time between interventions, or recovery after failure. Lesson 8.3 showed how step reliabilities multiply; these properties require a separate multi-hour run.

Simulation may fail to reproduce contact, transparency, cables, and wear. A real-world arena is limited by its available platform and safe tasks. Human preference can assess smoothness and appropriateness, but needs evaluator instructions and agreement checks. No format covers everything, so mature evaluation combines several levels instead of collapsing them into one universal score.

Safety does not reduce cleanly to average reward. A policy may complete more tasks and collide with a person once; high aggregate success does not make that tradeoff acceptable. Count dangerous events, excess force, and workspace violations separately and treat them as constraints. The internal release gate from Lesson 8.4 remains mandatory even when a public benchmark lacks the relevant scenario.

Morphology also defines a boundary. A wrist camera sees an object differently from a humanoid head camera; a parallel gripper and a five-finger hand have different action spaces; a mobile base changes reachability. Transfer between embodiments is a separate experiment. The word “manipulation” in two names does not make their policy interfaces compatible.

An instruction's semantics may be familiar even when the sentence is new. Simple word reordering tests language robustness, not a novel goal. Conversely, familiar words can specify a new composition of actions. A language split must say what was held out: exact phrasings, goal templates, or the tasks themselves. Without that, “new instruction” is ambiguous.

Human evaluation adds properties that are hard to formalize: the motion looks natural, the object is placed carefully, and the outcome matches intent. But preference depends on the question, video angle, and evaluator culture. If one person chooses “faster” and another “gentler,” the pair has no shared criterion. Instructions and evaluator agreement become part of the protocol, just like the simulator horizon.

Benchmarks also rarely measure the cost of a result. Two models may achieve equal success while one needs ten times more demonstrations, compute, or manual resets. That may be acceptable for a research question but decisive for a product. Report training budget, inference speed, operator labor, and hardware wear as separate axes rather than inferring them from success rate.

Finally, a public test becomes an optimization target. Teams tune architectures, data, and prompts to a popular suite; its score rises faster than capability beyond it. Countermeasures include new hidden tasks, multiple split axes, published slices, and external real-robot evaluation. A leaderboard shows progress on a fixed question, not product readiness.

10 Today

Evaluation is moving from one rig to a network of sites

Modern suites are expanding in four directions. First, task and scene diversity: RoboCasa365 and BEHAVIOR-1K move from atomic skills to composite household tasks. Second, diagnostic shifts: LIBERO-Plus and RoboTwin 2.0 vary observations, language, and the physical scene in controlled ways. Third, simulation-to-reality validation: SIMPLER and RobotArena∞ test the measurement rig itself. Fourth, distributed real-world evaluation: RoboArena collects double-blind pairs on the DROID platform across organizations.

Scale does not remove the need for a shared protocol. RoboArena's official page allows sites to choose tasks but requires the same task and environment within each pair. The paper reports more than 600 paired real-world episodes across seven policies. That supports a relative ranking over the collected network of conditions. Other robots and the product-specific release gates from Lesson 8.4 still require their own tests.

A living benchmark needs version control. Fixing physics or extending the horizon improves the test, but breaks direct comparability with older rows. A mature platform archives the protocol, marks reevaluated models, and does not carry scores between versions with a conversion factor. A rerun is a new experiment.

Tables increasingly ship with launch code, an environment container, task configurations, raw logs, and a video viewer. These artifacts do not guarantee a correct conclusion, but they make mismatches visible: a different camera, truncated instruction, incorrect reset, or technical failure. In robotics, reproducibility begins with an observable trajectory; it does not end with one installation command.

For a product team, a public benchmark is an initial filter. It helps select a model family and identify known strengths. The team then moves the candidate into its own test matrix and checks safety, continuous operation, and production task traffic. A strong public result narrows the search, but release still follows the gates of the specific product.

A practical one-minute reading of a new table works like this. First state the capability implied by its name. Then find the robot embodiment, action interface, split, and version. Check the primary metric, number of runs, and available slices. Finally, list the properties absent from the protocol. If you cannot fill out this card, it is too early to use the number for an engineering decision.

The next gap matters especially for a physical agent: an action must be both correct and timely. How do we measure the path from camera frame to actuator command and schedule loops with different frequencies in real time?

12 Summary

Lesson takeaways

  1. A benchmark defines a reproducible experiment: tasks, environment and embodiment, data, split, metric, protocol, and version.
  2. A short skill, a long chain, a stress test, and a real-world A/B pair impose different requirements; success on one does not transfer automatically to another.
  3. The split states the generalization question: new episodes, objects, scenes, and tasks are different tests.
  4. The modern landscape runs from RLBench and LIBERO to LIBERO-Plus, RoboTwin 2.0, SIMPLER, RobotArena∞, and RoboArena. It is a collection of different measurements, not one scale.
  5. A controlled shift distinguishes the intended capability from a shortcut that happens to work in a familiar scene.
  6. A pairwise arena makes policies comparable within one task, but the final ranking still depends on pair and site coverage.
  7. A leaderboard does not replace tests of safety, reliability, latency, and product readiness.

Open questions

Can every capability be combined into one overall score?

Weights can be assigned, but they become a hidden value model: one number decides how many novel scenes compensate for a poor long-horizon task or high latency. An overall index is more useful for engineering when accompanied by mandatory constraints and separate slices.

How do we compare a closed model when its training cannot be reproduced?

Separate training reproducibility from evaluation reproducibility. A closed model can run through a fixed interface on a hidden server, with versions, outcomes, and videos published. But causal explanations of its result will remain weaker than for an open system.
