Sim-to-real transfer: dynamics randomization

In lessons 5.2 and 5.3, policies learned in a simulator, where a step costs almost nothing: PPO trains walking on thousands of copies of a robot in minutes. But a simulator is a single model of the robot, with one set of masses, one friction value and motors that execute a command instantly. On a real arm, the cup may be heavier, the joints stiffer and the motors weaker, and the command reaches the actuator late, so a policy learned on one exact model can oscillate and miss on the robot. In 2018, Peng et al. trained an arm to push a puck and varied 95 simulator parameters in every episode: on the real robot, such policies brought the puck to the goal in 67–89% of attempts, while the policy trained without these variations succeeded in none of 10. Tan et al. and Hwangbo et al. added an actuator model and latency to the simulator, and in 2019 OpenAI made the variation ranges grow on their own, and a hand trained only in simulation solved a Rubik's cube on the real robot. We'll look at what exactly doesn't match, how to train a policy on many worlds at once, what this costs, and how to choose the width of the ranges without tuning them by hand.

1 The gap

How an arm in the simulator differs from a real one

In lesson 5.2, Rudin et al. trained the ANYmal quadruped to walk on 4,096 copies at once, and 20 minutes on a single GPU were enough. In lesson 5.3, SAC saved steps because on a real robot, every step costs time and wear. We'd like the best of both: train the policy in a simulator, where a step is almost free, and transfer it to the robot without fine-tuning. Lesson 4.5 showed what gets in the way for vision: light, paint and shadows in the renderer differ from what the camera sees, and image randomization helps, with random tablecloths, light and paint in every training scene. For a policy that commands motors, the gap lies elsewhere: in the dynamics, that is, in how the body responds to a command.

The simulator knows the robot's nominal model: the masses and dimensions of the links from the drawings, the friction from the datasheet, and a motor that delivers exactly the torque requested, at the same instant. On a real robot, everything is a little different. The masses are known with an error: Hwangbo et al. estimate that ANYmal's inertia differs from the drawings by up to 20% because of the cables and electronics the drawings leave out. Friction varies from joint to joint and over time. A motor can't deliver more than its limit: Tan et al. write that in a real motor, the torque stops growing along with the current. And the command doesn't arrive instantly. On the Minitaur quadruped, 15–19 ms passed between the policy and the motor, while in the Bullet simulator, where the policy was trained, a command took effect immediately. The time from the policy's decision until the actuator starts executing it is called control latency.

Each mismatch seems small on its own. But Peng et al. sent the same trajectory to the Fetch arm in the simulator and to the real one and got noticeably different joint angles. And a policy trained with reinforcement learning actively seeks out the ways the simulator differs from the world: the authors write plainly that such policies tend to exploit the idiosyncrasies of the simulator. The diagram below shows how latency and a weak motor change the path of the same command.

In the nominal world, the same controller brings the gripper to the target in 0.6 seconds and holds it there, while on the real arm, it moves more slowly and oscillates around the target until the end of the attempt. Which of the mismatches are dangerous for a policy trained with reinforcement learning, and which will it survive? Let's test this on Ada's arm.

Under the hood: the arm dynamics, an actuator with latency and the spread across seeds

Dynamics. Let \(q\) be the shoulder and elbow angles, and \(\dot q\) and \(\ddot q\) their velocities and accelerations. The arm's motion obeys the equation from lesson 0.3:

\[ M(q)\,\ddot q + C(q, \dot q) + G(q) = \tau. \]

Here \(M(q)\) is the inertia matrix, \(C\) holds the centrifugal and Coriolis torques, \(G(q)\) is the gravity torque, and \(\tau\) is the motor torques. The masses are point masses: 1.2 kg at the elbow and 0.9 kg at the gripper, plus the cup \(m\). The integration step is 5 ms.

The actuator. The policy outputs a gripper shift, and the arm controller converts it into joint targets \(q^*\) through the Jacobian. The motor of joint \(i\) delivers

\[ \tau_i = s \cdot \operatorname{clip}\bigl(K_{p,i}\,(q^*_i - q_i) - K_{d,i}\,\dot q_i + \hat G_i(q),\ \pm\tau_{\max,i}\bigr) - \tau_{\text{fr},i}. \]

Here \(K_p\) and \(K_d\) are the PD controller gains (110 and 55 N·m/rad, 7 and 3.5 N·m·s/rad), \(\hat G\) is the gravity compensation computed for the nominal 0.25 kg cup, \(\tau_{\max}\) is the torque limit (30 and 14 N·m), \(s\) is the motor strength as a fraction of nominal, and \(\tau_{\text{fr}}\) is the joint friction: dry friction of magnitude \(f\) plus viscous friction \(0.3 f \dot q\). The latency \(d\) works like this: a new joint target takes effect \(d\) ms after the policy's decision, and until then, the actuator keeps driving toward the previous one.

The policy and the test. The observation contains the joint angles and velocities and the vector from the gripper to the target. The policy has no memory: it sees only the current moment. The 20 test targets are the same in all worlds, and during the test, the policy acts with its mean action, without randomness.

Spread. The numbers in the missions are computed for random seed 3. Across six seeds, with a 1 kg mug and no latency, 90–100% of attempts succeed, and with 40 ms of latency and the nominal cup, 30–55%. The boundary beyond which Ada holds the cup in fewer than half of the attempts lies at 40–50 ms. With motors at 60%, 70–85% of attempts succeed, and at 50%, 45–65%, while joint friction of up to 1.3 N·m has almost no effect.

The policy survives a heavy cup and stiff joints. These are constant disturbances: the arm sags or moves more slowly, the policy sees that the gripper isn't where it should be, and corrects. Latency is different: it breaks the feedback loop itself. The policy corrects an error that no longer exists by the time the command is executed, and the arm oscillates around the target. Weak motors are a third case: when there isn't enough torque to hold the cup, no policy will help. And Ada's real arms have latencies of 25–45 ms. How do you train a policy that survives this too, without knowing in advance which arm it will get?

2 Randomization

Dynamics randomization: learning on many worlds at once

The policy from the exact simulator saw one world and adapted to it, including its errors. You could spend years refining the simulator, but you can't measure everything: Tobin et al. list nonrigidity, gear backlash and wear-and-tear, which the model doesn't have at all, and latency also changes from run to run. Lesson 4.5 offered another way for images: make the simulator not exact but varied, so that the real world becomes one of the variants for the model. Peng et al. (OpenAI and UC Berkeley, 2018) carried this idea over to physics. At the start of every episode, the dynamics parameters are drawn at random from given ranges, and the policy learns on all these worlds at once. This is called dynamics randomization. Training looks for the policy with the highest expected return, averaged not only over trajectories, as in lesson 5.2, but also over worlds:

\[ \max_{\pi}\ \mathbb{E}_{\mu \sim \rho_\mu}\Bigl[\ \mathbb{E}_{\tau \sim p(\tau \mid \pi, \mu)}\Bigl[\ \sum_{t=0}^{T-1} r(s_t, a_t) \Bigr] \Bigr]. \]

Here \(\pi\) is the policy, \(\mu\) is a set of dynamics parameters (masses, friction, latency), \(\rho_\mu\) is the distribution they are drawn from, \(\tau\) is the trajectory of one episode, \(p(\tau \mid \pi, \mu)\) is the probability of getting it when acting with policy \(\pi\) in a world with parameters \(\mu\), \(r(s_t, a_t)\) is the reward at step \(t\), and \(T\) is the episode length. Without randomization, there is no outer average: there is one world, and \(\mu\) is always nominal.

In Peng et al.'s work, a Fetch arm with seven degrees of freedom has to push a puck to a random point on a table. The reward is binary: 0 if the puck is at the goal and −1 otherwise. In total, 95 parameters were varied: the link masses from 0.25 to 4 times nominal, joint damping, the puck's mass from 0.1 to 0.4 kg and its friction from 0.1 to 5, the table height, the controller gains from half to twice nominal, observation noise and the time between actions. With the random time between actions, the authors modeled the controller's latency. Masses and friction stay fixed for the whole episode, while the time between actions and the noise change at every step. Training took about 100 million steps, roughly 8 hours on a 100-core cluster, and the authors did almost no calibration of the simulator to the robot.

On the real robot, the policy trained without randomization failed in all 10 attempts. With randomization, a network without memory brought the puck to the goal in 67% of attempts, and a network with memory, an LSTM that receives the current state and the previous action as input, in 89% of 28 attempts, almost as in simulation (91%). When a packet of chips was attached to the bottom of the puck, changing its friction against the table, success stayed at the same level: 91%. An ablation showed which randomizations matter most: without the random time between actions, success fell to 29%; without observation noise, to 25%; with fixed link masses, to 64%; and with fixed puck friction, to 48%.

Briefly, why the policy needs memory: from how the world responded to its past actions, it can work out which of the worlds it is in. We'll discuss this in detail in the next lesson. Here we're interested in randomization itself, so we'll test it on Ada's arm without memory: the policy sees only the current state.

PPO. Everything is as in lesson 5.2. With the same budget, randomization pulled through all six arms, and the price was speed in the nominal world. But our “real” arms differ from the nominal one in exactly the parameters we varied during training. If the gap lies in something the simulator can't do at all, such as how the actuator works, varying parameters is useless. What then?

3 Actuator

Actuator model and latency: bringing the simulator closer to the robot

Randomization helps when the real world resembles one of the simulator's variants. But if the simulator is built differently from the robot, no matter how you vary the parameters, the real world won't be among the variants. Tan et al. (Google, 2018) ran into this with the Minitaur quadruped. Its eight direct-drive motors are run by a position controller, and in the Bullet simulator, this controller was modeled as a constraint that is satisfied exactly by the end of the step. The authors write that with large gains, such motors “can remain stable in simulation but oscillate in reality.” The policy trained in this simulator learned a slow walk, and the real Minitaur fell to the ground immediately.

So the authors brought the simulator closer to the robot. They took the Minitaur apart and weighed and measured its links. They described the motor as an ideal DC motor, with a correction: in a real one, the torque stops growing along with the current, and they set this dependence with a piecewise linear function fitted to measurements. They measured the latency, 3 ms for the motor controller on the microcontroller and 15–19 ms for the policy on the onboard computer, and added it to the simulator. The authors' conclusion: without the actuator model or without the latency, the learned policies don't work on the robot. With both, the Minitaur learned to gallop and trot: the gallop reached 1.34 m/s in simulation and 1.18 m/s on the robot and used 35% less power than a hand-tuned gallop. They used randomization too, with mass ±20%, latency 0–40 ms, motor strength 80–120% and ground friction 0.5–1.25, but noted that “if the model discrepancy is too big, even a robust controller trained with random perturbations cannot overcome the reality gap.”

The Minitaur's motor can be described by a formula because its drive is direct. With ANYmal's series elastic actuators, this didn't work. In lesson 0.2, we trained a small network to reproduce a real actuator from its recordings: that's an actuator model from data. Hwangbo et al. (ETH Zurich and Intel, 2019) did this for all 12 of ANYmal's actuators: a network with three layers of 32 neurons predicts the torque from the position errors and velocities over the last 0.02 s. The data, more than a million examples, were collected in less than 4 minutes. The network's mean error was 0.74 N·m versus 3.55 N·m for the ideal actuator model. For comparison, the authors trained policies with an ideal actuator and with an analytical model that they had tuned for more than a week. Neither could make a single step without falling: the legs shook violently, and the authors attribute this to delays that these models get wrong.

ANYmal's policy was trained in a simulator with these actuator networks inside: it computed almost 500,000 steps per second on a single computer, and training took no more than 11 hours. The error of the inertia estimated from the drawings, up to 20%, was handled by randomization: 30 variants of the robot with link masses ±15% and centers of mass ±2 cm. ANYmal reached 1.5 m/s, 25% faster than the previous record for this robot. The authors sum up what each technique did: the learned actuator model significantly reduces the reality gap, while the randomness in the model makes the policy sufficiently conservative. And they write that in practice, both approaches, a more accurate model and a more robust policy, “are typically employed in conjunction.” So how much of this caution is needed?

Under the hood: how the width works and the spread across seeds

Width. For each parameter, the shifts of the range boundaries from nominal at width ×1 are fixed: cup mass from −0.15 to +0.35 kg, joint friction from −0.1 to +0.4 N·m, latency from 0 to +50 ms, motor strength from −25 to +10%. At width \(k\), the range equals the nominal value plus these shifts multiplied by \(k\), within physical limits: the mass is at least 0.02 kg, friction is at least zero, latency is at most 150 ms, and motor strength is at least 30%. At ×3, the ranges are: cup up to 1.3 kg, friction up to 1.3 N·m, latency up to 150 ms, motors from 30% strength.

Why slower. A policy without memory doesn't know which world it got, and it picks one behavior for all the training worlds. If those include a world with 150 ms of latency, a fast approach to the target ends in oscillation there, and the reward over all such worlds pulls the policy toward a slow, cautious approach, even in a world with short latency. Tan et al. saw the same on the Minitaur: with randomization, the returns have a lower mean but also a smaller spread across worlds, and in the authors' words, “the controllers learn to behave conservatively.”

Spread. The numbers in the missions are computed for seed 3. Across six seeds, ×½ gives 94–100% successful attempts on the real arms, and ×1 gives 97–100%. Width ×3 gives 40–98% successful attempts versus 97–100% for ×1, and the arm takes 0.84–1.02 s to reach the target versus 0.69–0.78 s, so it is slower on all six seeds. On one of them, the ×3 policy never learned the task in 12,000 steps: even in the nominal world, it held the cup in 3 attempts out of 20.

Even narrow randomization shifts the policy toward behavior that transfers beyond the training range. Too wide a range makes it cautious all the time: there are too many worlds where a fast approach is dangerous. That's why the width is chosen to match the real spread with a small margin, as Tan et al. did, and for that, you need to know the spread. In Dactyl from lesson 4.5, the ranges had to be tuned by hand, and the full randomization cost 100 years of simulated experience versus 3 years without it. Can the ranges be chosen automatically?

4 ADR

Automatic domain randomization: the ranges grow on their own

Tuning the ranges by hand is expensive: in Dactyl, as the authors write, it “required a significant amount of manual tuning and a tight iteration loop between randomization design in simulation and validation on a robot.” By the time of the Rubik's cube work (OpenAI, 2019), the Shadow hand and the simulator had changed, and the old manual randomization no longer transferred: a policy trained with it on the new setup made 42.5 turns of the cube in a row on average in simulation and 2.7 on the real hand. Instead of tuning the ranges again, the authors made them tune themselves. This is automatic domain randomization (ADR).

Training starts in a single world, with the parameters measured during calibration of the real hand. Each parameter has its own interval \([\phi^L_i,\ \phi^H_i]\) from which it is drawn, and at first, both ends coincide with the calibrated value. From time to time, ADR sets one random parameter to one of the boundaries of its interval, draws the rest from their current intervals, and checks whether the policy copes. The results are accumulated separately for each boundary. When \(m\) of them have accumulated, ADR compares their mean \(\bar p\) with two thresholds:

\[ \bar p \ge t_H\ \Rightarrow\ \text{the boundary moves outward by } \Delta, \qquad \bar p \le t_L\ \Rightarrow\ \text{inward by } \Delta. \]

Here \(t_H\) and \(t_L\) are the upper and lower performance thresholds, and \(\Delta\) is the step size. OpenAI measures performance by the number of successful turns in a row in an episode, with these settings: \(t_H = 20\), \(t_L = 10\), \(m = 240\), \(\Delta = 0.02\); boundary checks are done in half of the episodes. This creates a curriculum: first, the policy solves the task in a single world, and then worlds are added exactly as fast as it masters them. The authors measure how wide the distribution is with the ADR entropy: the mean over the parameters of the logarithm of the interval widths.

On the same new setup, ADR was trained for as long as the manual randomization, about 14 days. A snapshot of the policy taken when the ADR entropy reached 0.126 nats per parameter made 13.3 turns in a row on average on the real hand, versus 2.7 for the manual one; the wider the ranges had managed to become, the better the transfer. A policy trained longer and at a larger scale made 32 turns on average and 42 at the median. The authors also compared ADR with fixed ranges of different widths: “the larger the fixed randomization entropy, the longer it takes to train from scratch,” while ADR “makes progress much faster due to its curriculum property.”

The policy for the Rubik's cube was trained for several months, about 13,000 years of simulated experience. With it, the Shadow hand solved a cube whose solution required 15 face rotations in 60% of attempts, and one requiring 26 rotations in 20%. Without being trained on such disturbances, it kept turning the cube in a rubber glove, with its fingers tied together, and while the cube was poked with a pen and a plush giraffe. The authors explain this by meta-learning: in the first seconds of an attempt, a policy with memory figures out which world it is in; from the LSTM's hidden state, the cube size can be predicted with over 80% accuracy. Nobody programmed this ability; it emerged from the breadth of the worlds. Let's see how ADR widens the ranges of Ada's arm.

Under the hood: ADR in the paper and here, entropy and the spread across seeds

The algorithm. The distribution of worlds is a product of uniform intervals, one per parameter: \(\lambda_i \sim U(\phi^L_i, \phi^H_i)\). OpenAI measures the width of the whole distribution with the ADR entropy:

\[ H(P_\phi) = \frac{1}{d} \sum_{i=1}^{d} \log\bigl(\phi^H_i - \phi^L_i\bigr), \]

where \(d\) is the number of parameters, and \(\phi^L_i\) and \(\phi^H_i\) are the boundaries of the interval of the \(i\)-th parameter; the entropy is measured in nats per parameter. With probability \(p_b\), a worker performs a boundary check, and otherwise it simply collects experience for training; OpenAI uses \(p_b = 0.5\), and the data from boundary checks also go into training the policy.

In our version. We use \(p_b = 0.5\), a boundary buffer of \(m = 5\) attempts and thresholds of \(t_H = 80\%\) and \(t_L = 40\%\) successful attempts. The step is a quarter of the shift at width ×1: 0.0375 and 0.0875 kg for the mass, 0.025 and 0.1 N·m for friction, 12.5 ms for latency, 6.25 and 2.5% for motor strength. The lower boundary of latency is zero and doesn't move. We don't show the entropy: for an interval of zero width at the start, it equals minus infinity.

The comparison. For comparison, we take the fixed ×3 range from lab 2 from the first step, with the same PPO, the same budget and the same seed. “Reached 90%” means the first test (every 1,200 steps) at which the policy holds the cup in at least 108 attempts out of 120.

Spread. The numbers in the missions are computed for seed 3. Across six seeds, ADR reaches 90% on the real arms by step 3,600–4,800, and the fixed ×3 range by step 10,800–14,400. By the end, ADR succeeds in 97–100% of attempts, and ×3 in 93–100%. ADR's time to target is 0.68–0.73 s, and that of ×3 is 0.75–0.93 s, so ADR is faster on all six seeds. By the end of training, ADR's upper boundary of latency reaches 75–150 ms, the lower boundary of motor strength drops to 63–81%, and the upper boundary of cup mass reaches 0.6–0.78 kg. With the threshold \(t_H = 100\%\), the latency range comes out no wider than with 80% on four seeds out of four, and with 60%, no narrower.

ADR doesn't know in advance what matters. It widens each range as long as the policy copes at its edge, and finds on its own where the limit lies: the policy learned to survive latency with a large margin, but not weak motors with a heavy cup, because there isn't enough torque there. The curriculum also saves steps: at the start, the policy learns in a simple world, and the hard worlds are added when it's ready for them. What of this lives on in today's systems?

5 Today

Actuator models, randomization and curricula together

The recipe of this lesson, a more accurate model where it can be measured and randomization where it can't, has become standard for policies trained with reinforcement learning in simulation. Rudin et al., who in lesson 5.2 trained ANYmal to walk in 20 minutes on 4,096 copies, prepared the transfer to the robot like this: they randomized the ground friction from 0.5 to 1.25, pushed the robots every 10 seconds, accelerating the body to up to 1 m/s in a random direction, and added noise to the observations at a level measured on the real robot. The actuator torques were computed by a network trained on data, as in Hwangbo et al.'s work.

Their curriculum works like ADR, only for terrain. Each robot has its own difficulty level: the step height grows from 5 to 20 cm, and the slope up to 25°. A robot that made it to the edge of its terrain gets a higher level in the next episode, and one that didn't cover even half of the required distance gets a lower one. The difficulty adapts to the policy without manual tuning. As lesson 4.5 mentioned, humanoids are taught to walk the same way, such as the legs of HOMIE from lesson 4.2; we'll talk about this in more detail in part 6. For now, Ada has a wheeled base, and her arm in this lesson is the same case, only simpler.

Randomization without memory has a ceiling: the policy doesn't know which world it is in and picks one behavior for all worlds, so it is cautious everywhere. On the real ANYmal, Rudin et al. limited the speed to 0.6 m/s because the robot's height map is imprecise, and they pointed to the teacher-student setup of Miki et al. (lesson 5.5), which, in their words, “provided outstanding robustness even in adverse conditions.” Peng et al. already fed the world parameters to the critic as input, and in OpenAI's work, a policy with memory figured out the cube size on its own. How to make use of the fact that everything is known in the simulator, we'll cover in the next lesson.

Eight common misconceptions

7 Takeaways

Lesson takeaways

  1. The simulator knows the robot's nominal model, while the real robot has different masses, friction and motor strength, plus control latency. Not all mismatches are equally dangerous: on Ada's arm, a 1 kg mug barely bothered the policy from the exact simulator (100% successful attempts), while 40 ms of latency dropped success to 45%.
  2. Dynamics randomization trains the policy on a distribution of worlds. For Peng et al., the policy without it failed in all 10 attempts on the robot, and the policy with it succeeded in 67–89% of attempts. On Ada's six real arms, with the same budget of 12,000 steps, the share of successful attempts was 78% without randomization and 99% with it.
  3. Robustness is paid for with speed: the policy picks a behavior that is safe in all the training worlds. In the nominal world, the randomized arm took 0.55 s to reach the target versus 0.49 s, and at width ×3 on the real arms, 0.88 s versus 0.72 s at ×1, with no more successful attempts.
  4. Randomization can't cover what the simulator can't do at all. Tan et al. added a motor model and latency, and without them, the Minitaur policies didn't work on the robot; in Hwangbo et al.'s work, ANYmal's actuators are computed by a network trained on 4 minutes of data, and with an ideal actuator, the policy couldn't make a single step. In practice, a more accurate model and randomization go together.
  5. ADR starts from the calibrated world and widens each range as long as the policy copes at its boundary. For OpenAI, this raised transfer from 2.7 to 13.3 cube turns in a row and made it possible to solve a Rubik's cube. On Ada's arm, ADR reached 90% on the real arms by step 4,800, versus 10,800 for the wide range from the first step, pushed latency out to 75 ms and stopped motor strength at 63%.

Open questions

A randomized policy works “on average” across all worlds. Why not give it the world parameters as input?

In the simulator, the parameters are known, and a policy that sees them can be fast where the world allows it and cautious where it doesn't. But a real robot has no masses, friction or latency at its input: they have to be estimated from how the arm responds to commands. Peng et al. gave the parameters only to the critic, which is needed only during training, and gave the policy memory. How to turn the simulator's knowledge into a policy that doesn't need it on the robot, we'll cover in lesson 5.5.

How do you tell what exactly to randomize and how widely?

You can measure what can be measured, such as masses, latencies and motor characteristics, and randomize around the measured values with a margin, as Tan et al. did. You can check with an ablation what matters for transfer, as Peng et al. did, for whom random latency turned out to matter more than the masses. Or you can hand the choice over to ADR, but then you need to check that the ranges grew toward where the real robot lies, and not into worlds that don't exist: wide worlds make the policy more cautious.
